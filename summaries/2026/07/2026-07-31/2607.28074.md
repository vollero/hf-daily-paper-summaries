# Echoverse: Deep, Evolving Environments for Training Computer-Use Agents at Scale

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-31.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.28074) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28074)


## One-sentence takeaway

Echoverse couples database-grounded, resettable computer-use worlds with iterative environment repair and model training, raising a 9B agent’s average success from 36.5% to 67.1% across 14 synthetic evaluation splits and further improving held-out performance through reinforcement learning.

## Problem and motivation

Computer-use agents need interactive applications where actions have real, observable consequences, but important workflows are often login-gated, stateful, unsafe to modify, difficult to reset, and inaccessible to backend verification. Synthetic applications address access and reset, yet the authors argue that scaling their count alone overlooks three determinants of training value: workflow depth, targeting of specific agent failures, and continued repair as the agent evolves (Sections 1 and 3.3).

The identified gap is therefore not environment availability but environment quality: generated applications may look plausible while containing broken workflows, incoherent state, infeasible tasks, or incorrect verifiers that turn world defects into misleading supervision (Sections 3.3 and 4.6).

## Approach and contributions

- **Operational workflow depth:** A world is “deep” when it supports every targeted workflow end to end through the interface and leaves database effects precise enough to verify. Workflow-derived, machine-checkable claims test this property during construction (Section 3.3; Table 1).

- **Two-phase environment factory:** Hand-written seeds are expanded into specifications and complete React/FastAPI/SQLite applications; agent-based builders, verifiers, triagers, and fixers then repair them. Tasks are subsequently regrounded on live database state and checked for feasibility and solvability (Sections 4.1–4.3; Figure 2).

- **Database-grounded grading:** Read tasks compare answers with database-derived references, write tasks compare required changes with the database diff, and mixed tasks require both. An LLM performs the final semantic comparison but sees the reference and observed outcome rather than the trajectory (Sections 3.1–3.2; Appendix C).

- **Co-evolution:** Each failed rollout is triaged as either a defect in the environment/task/verifier or a genuine model failure. World repairs precede using remaining failures as training signal, and task goals are not weakened to inflate pass rates (Sections 4.6–4.7; Figure 1).

- **Full-domain and capability worlds:** Ten worlds model multi-step application domains, while two specialized worlds expose date pickers and nested filters across varied layouts, including held-out widget families (Sections 5.1–5.3; Figures 3–4).

- **Supervised and reinforcement learning:** Passing GPT-5.4 trajectories provide verifier-filtered distillation data; a subsequent group-relative policy-gradient stage combines terminal database-grounded reward with judged per-step shaping (Sections 4.8, 7.3).

- **Benchmark release:** Four runnable worlds—EchoStay, EchoForge, and the two capability worlds—are released with seed databases, evaluation tasks, and grounded graders; training tasks are withheld (Section 8).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|

The results support the authors’ claims that workflow-complete worlds can yield stronger transfer than shallow ones, targeted control variation can generalize beyond trained layouts, repairing worlds can improve downstream training data, and the environments can support RL.

They do not isolate every causal component. In particular, the EchoStay comparison deliberately changes the environment, available tasks, corpus, and verifier together. The evidence also does not establish general scaling laws, superiority over all environment-generation systems, or broad open-web performance beyond the reported benchmarks and ablations.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Teacher ceiling:** The supervised corpus contains only successful GPT-5.4 trajectories, so it inherits the demonstrator’s behavioral coverage and cannot directly teach recovery from student-specific mistakes (Sections 4.8 and 7.1).

- **Depth experiment is small:** The shallow-versus-deep comparison covers two live domains with only a few dozen tasks each. The authors explicitly treat it as evidence of possible regression, not a quantitative estimate of depth’s value (Section 6.2).

- **Imperfect control of depth:** Shallow and deep corpora are comparable in size but not trajectory-matched, so depth is not the only conceivable difference between them (Section 6.2).

- **Co-evolution components are confounded:** EchoStay’s environment, task set, verifier, and training corpus evolve jointly; the paper states that an environment-only or verifier-only ablation is not defined under its design (Section 6.7).

- **Judged components remain:** Although ground truth comes from the database, GPT-4.1 performs semantic outcome comparisons, and RL adds a GPT-4.1-vision step reward. Thus the pipeline is not entirely free of model-judge error (Sections 3.2 and 7.3; Appendix C).

- **Permissive verification may hide side effects:** Extra database changes are tolerated when the required change is present. Analyst inference: this could count harmful or unintended side effects as success unless separately constrained (Section 3.2; Appendix C.2).

- **Narrow RL evidence:** RL uses five worlds, one 9B starting policy, 32 GPUs, 125 held-out validation tasks, and a little over two epochs; no ablation separates terminal reward, dense shaping, task filtering, or the modified grouping rule (Sections 7.3–7.5).

- **Synthetic-to-live validity:** Most main evaluations occur inside synthetic worlds. Live-web tests are broader but use model-based published judges and differing hosted/datacentre browser conditions (Sections 6.1 and 6.5).

- **Partial release:** Only four of twelve worlds and evaluation tasks—not training tasks—are released, limiting independent reproduction of the complete supervised and RL results (Section 8).

## Open research questions

1. **Which definition-of-depth components drive transfer?**  
   The paper bundles workflow completeness, coherent cross-actor state, dependencies, and groundability. A next experiment could construct matched worlds that remove one property at a time while holding tasks and trajectory counts fixed.

2. **How much does dense judged reward contribute beyond grounded terminal reward?**  
   The RL run combines both signals without a reported ablation. A controlled study could compare terminal-only, dense-only, and composite rewards using identical task pools and rollout budgets.

3. **Can co-evolution gains be decomposed?**  
   EchoStay changes the application, tasks, references, and corpus together. A factorial experiment could freeze three components while modifying one wherever task feasibility permits, then measure verifier accuracy, corpus quality, and downstream policy success separately.

4. **Do broader world families continue improving live-web transfer?**  
   Environment diversity outperforms repeated trajectories within the tested range, but the number and ordering of environments are limited. A randomized scaling study across multiple world subsets could estimate whether breadth gains persist and which domain properties predict transfer.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text gives detailed methods, settings, and headline outcomes, but several referenced tables are present only as captions rather than numerical contents. Consequently, the per-split scores in Table 4, full live-web results in Table 6, per-world corpus composition in Table 10, exact RL configuration in Table 9, environment database statistics in Table 3, and detailed benchmark/task counts in several tables are unavailable. The text also does not provide repeated-run variance, statistical significance, training hyperparameters for supervised fine-tuning, compute cost for environment construction, human-QA effort, verifier error rates, or reward-component ablations.
