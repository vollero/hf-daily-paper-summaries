# See2Think: Do Multimodal Models Really Use Intermediate Visual States?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-31.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.26769) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26769)


## One-sentence takeaway

See2Think finds that multimodal models often act on intermediate visual states, but their accuracy benefits are model- and environment-dependent, with faithful rendering—not action selection—the clearest bottleneck.

## Problem and motivation

Multimodal models increasingly crop, annotate, sketch, or otherwise modify images while reasoning, yet the existence of these traces does not show that the model uses them or benefits from them. The authors identify two gaps: existing task collections may contain text-solvable shortcuts or limited visual diversity, and evaluations rarely diagnose action relevance, rendering fidelity, and downstream use through matched interventions (Sections 1–2; Figure 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **See2ThinkBench:** 1,200 visually dependent problems, with 100 samples in each of 12 categories spanning 2D structured, 3D scene, and real-world reasoning. Caption-only solvability filtering, answer-format conversion, automatic checks, and manual review reduce textual shortcuts and ambiguity (Sections 2.1–2.2; Figures 2–3).
- **Visual Action-of-Thought (VAoT):** A model-agnostic inference protocol that alternates textual thoughts, structured visual actions, externally rendered states, and subsequent reasoning (Section 3; Figure 4).
- **Controlled diagnosis:** Four matched settings—CoT, VAoT-NoRender, VAoT, and VAoT-WrongRender—separate action planning, correct rendering, and behavioral sensitivity to corrupted feedback (Section 4.1).
- **Process evaluation:** Action Relevance, Render Faithfulness, and Feedback Uptake are scored for a key visual step, allowing visual-state utility to be distinguished from behavioral dependence (Sections 2.3 and 4.4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| No inference strategy is universally best. | Overall accuracy was highest with CoT for GPT-5.5 (50.17%) and Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct (38.67%), VAoT for GPT-o3 (43.75%), and VAoT-NoRender for Gemini 3.5 Flash (58.17%); all settings used all 1,200 samples. | Table 5; Sections 4.2–4.3 |
| Action selection is much stronger than rendering. | Across 4,800 VAoT trajectories, overall Action Relevance ranged from 0.958–0.985, versus 0.594–0.630 for Render Faithfulness on the 0/0.5/1 scale. | Table 3; Section 4.4 |
| High visual-feedback uptake does not ensure high accuracy. | Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct had the highest overall Feedback Uptake, 0.871, but only 35.67% VAoT accuracy. | Tables 3 and 5; Section 4.4 |
| Unfaithful rendering is especially harmful in 2D and 3D tasks. | At Render Faithfulness 0, the VAoT-NoRender→VAoT benefit-minus-harm balance was −6.1 pp for 2D and −9.3 pp for 3D; rates use aligned trajectories within each task-group/score bin. | Figure 7; Table 6 |
| Corruption reveals strong dependence in 3D reasoning. | As VAoT Feedback Uptake rose from 0 to 0.5 to 1, the 3D VAoT-minus-WrongRender accuracy drop increased from 3.5 to 10.3 to 15.5 pp. The analogous overall drops were −2.3, 3.4, and 3.1 pp. | Figure 8; Table 7 |
| Corruption frequently changed answers even when net accuracy effects were modest. | Semantic answers changed for 32.7%–54.2% of the 1,200 samples per model. | Table 8; Section 4.4 |
| Automatic process judgments received qualified human support. | In a stratified audit of 240 trajectories and 480 ratings per target, 92.9%–96.9% of judgments were rated at least partially reasonable; strict “reasonable” rates were 70.2%–88.3%. | Table 4; Section 4.5 |
| WrongRender quality materially affects estimated dependence. | Only 56.7% of 120 audited corruptions passed all five criteria and 78.3% failed at most one; VAoT-minus-WrongRender drops were 8.82 pp on the 68 strict-pass cases and 3.19 pp on the 94 acceptable cases. | Tables 11–12; Appendix F.2 |

The evidence establishes that models can be behaviorally sensitive to returned visual states and that rendering quality mediates their benefit or harm. It does not establish that intermediate images universally improve reasoning, that the process scores are ground truth, or that corruption sensitivity always reflects correct and beneficial visual reasoning.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:**

- Only four multimodal models were evaluated, limiting generalization to other or future systems (Section 6).
- VAoT depends on an external renderer and an automatic judge, both of which can introduce errors despite human validation (Section 6).
- WrongRender effects depend on corruption quality; the measured accuracy drop changes across quality filters (Section 6; Appendix F.2).

**Analyst inferences supported by the text:**

- Caption-only filtering reduces but does not eliminate language-prior shortcuts; the authors explicitly state that retained samples are not guaranteed to be impossible to solve from language priors (Appendix A.2).
- The human process audit is diagnostically stratified rather than representative of all 4,800 trajectories, so its agreement rates may not estimate full-benchmark judge reliability (Appendix F.1).
- Strict human agreement is substantially lower than partial-or-better agreement, particularly for Action Relevance and Render Faithfulness, indicating meaningful scoring-boundary uncertainty (Tables 4 and 10).
- Free-form accuracy and process metrics both rely on GPT-5.4 judgments, creating a shared evaluator dependency; the supplied text reports human validation for process scoring but not an equivalent audit of free-form answer judgments (Appendix B.1; Sections 4.1 and 4.5).
- The main corruption experiment tests only task-relevant `modify_key` interventions, not the supported non-key corruption condition, leaving specificity to task-relevant evidence incompletely controlled (Appendix B.5).

## Open research questions

1. **Do the conclusions generalize to other model architectures and visual-action systems?** Four models cannot represent the broader model landscape. A next experiment could repeat all four conditions across more open and proprietary models with identical sample-level alignments.

2. **Can better rendering convert behavioral dependence into reliable accuracy gains?** Rendering is the dominant observed bottleneck, but faithful renders yield only neutral or mildly positive transition balances in several groups. A controlled experiment could compare the current renderer with learned and deterministic renderers while holding model plans fixed.

3. **Is sensitivity specific to task-relevant corruption?** The paper evaluates `modify_key` but not its supported `modify_non_key` condition. A paired experiment could compare faithful, key-corrupted, and non-key-corrupted feedback on the same trajectories.

4. **How robust are the process conclusions to evaluator choice?** Process scores come from one automatic judge and a limited stratified audit. A plausible next study would compare several independent judges against a larger, randomly sampled expert annotation set.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high.** The supplied text includes the complete paper, detailed tables, prompts, appendices, and audit results.
