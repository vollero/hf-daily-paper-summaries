# AI Tour Meeting: Group Travel Planning by LLM Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-31.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.18806) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18806)


## One-sentence takeaway

AI Tour Meeting provides a domain-specific, persona-based multi-agent framework for negotiating group itineraries, with experiments showing reliable execution for models at least comparable to Qwen3.5-4B and increasingly difficult consensus as simulated preference conflict grows.

## Problem and motivation

Group travel planning must reconcile participants’ competing costs, schedules, and preferences. Existing group-planning systems primarily support human users, while general multi-agent frameworks lack built-in itinerary structures, travel-constraint validation, and proposal-voting workflows. The authors address this gap with a simulation environment in which persona-conditioned LLM agents negotiate itineraries, potentially supporting behavioral studies and automatic evaluation of group recommender systems (§1; Appendix B).

## Approach and contributions

- **Travel-specific multi-agent framework:** Agents discuss structured itineraries through conversation and voting phases until unanimous satisfaction or an execution limit is reached (§2.1–§2.3; Algorithm 1).
- **Configurable deliberation:** Personas, model parameters, turn rules, voting rules, explanation styles, constraints, and context-management strategies can be varied (§2.2–§2.3; Appendix E).
- **Integrated actions and validation:** Agents can search, ask, reflect, propose, vote, and score; invalid outputs or constraint violations trigger up to three retries (§2.2; Appendix E).
- **Monitoring and interfaces:** The framework records system and discussion metrics and provides a GUI, analytics dashboard, Python API, and human-participant mode (§2.4–§2.5; Figures 2–3; Appendix C).
- **Empirical demonstrations:** The paper validates execution across five model configurations and up to ten agents, then studies preference conflict and speaking-order effects (§3–§4; Tables 1–3; Figure 5).

The experiments establish that the implementation can execute the specified synthetic workflows across several models and group sizes, and that its monitored outcomes respond systematically to controlled preference conflict and speaking position. They do not establish correspondence with human group behavior, real-world itinerary quality, persona fidelity, or superiority to another planning framework or recommender system.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated restriction:** The code uses an NTT proprietary license limiting use to research purposes (Appendix A).
- **Analyst inference — synthetic validity:** Meetings and personas were generated by gpt-5.4-mini, and satisfaction was evaluated by an LLM judge; no human validation is reported (§3; §4.1).
- **Analyst inference — narrow behavioral setting:** The main analyses use three Qwen3.5-9B agents, synthetic preferences, fixed constraints, and only 50 meetings per condition (§4.1–§4.2).
- **Analyst inference — incomplete statistical reporting:** Figure 5 is described as showing significant differences and Wilson 95% intervals, but the supplied text gives neither acceptance rates nor test statistics or \(p\)-values (§4.2; Figure 5).
- **Analyst inference — limited causal attribution:** Conflict levels change several outcomes, but no ablation separates persona prompting, turn rules, voting rules, explanation styles, search, or context management.
- **Analyst inference — resource scaling:** Ten-agent meetings averaged 471.5 minutes and more than 3 million input tokens in the reported environment, indicating substantial scaling cost; duration is explicitly hardware-dependent (Table 3; Table 1 note).
- **Analyst inference — no external baseline:** The evaluation compares agent models and configurations, not AI Tour Meeting against existing group-planning systems or general multi-agent frameworks.

## Open research questions

1. **Do simulated negotiation patterns match human groups?** This remains open because the reported behavioral evidence comes exclusively from LLM agents. A next experiment could run matched scenarios with human groups and compare turns, compromise patterns, satisfaction, and victim rates.

2. **Which framework components produce better consensus?** Turn rules, voting rules, explanations, search, and context strategies are configurable but not ablated. A factorial experiment could vary these components while holding personas, models, and scenarios fixed.

3. **How robust are results to model and judge choice?** The behavioral analyses use Qwen3.5-9B participants and an unspecified LLM-as-a-judge configuration. Cross-model replication with blinded human ratings could measure whether the conflict and speaking-order findings persist.

4. **Can larger meetings remain efficient without degrading fairness or validity?** Table 3 shows steep increases in turns, tokens, and duration through ten participants. Experiments with parallel voting, context compaction, and alternative facilitation policies could test cost-quality tradeoffs at larger scales.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the framework description and reported tabular results; **medium** for interpreting behavioral findings because they depend on synthetic agents and LLM-based ratings.

Material missing information includes the exact synthetic-meeting generation prompts and samples, satisfaction-judge model/settings, Figure 5’s numerical acceptance rates and statistical tests, human validation, comparisons with existing systems, component ablations, repeated-seed variance, detailed itinerary-quality assessment, and independent reproducibility evidence.
