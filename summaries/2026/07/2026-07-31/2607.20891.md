# Is Deep Research Reliable? Misleading Knowledge Induces False Conclusions

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-31.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.20891) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20891)


## One-sentence takeaway

Controlled experiments show that deep-research agents frequently adopt apparently credible false evidence—especially when introduced just before synthesis—and that verification prompts and post-processing reduce but do not eliminate this failure.

## Problem and motivation

Deep-research agents perform long-horizon planning, retrieval, evidence accumulation, and report synthesis in open information environments. The authors argue that prior reliability work mainly studies short-horizon retrieval or explicit poisoning attacks, leaving unclear how ambient, credible-looking misinformation propagates through intermediate research states and becomes an endorsed conclusion. This matters because stronger search and report-generation capabilities do not necessarily provide reliable evidence verification (§§1–2.2).

## Approach and contributions

- **MisKnow-Agent:** Constructs a task-specific false conclusion, generates documents across three authority tiers and four source styles, and retains them only after unanimous rejection by multiple search-enabled verifiers plus manual screening (§3.3).
- **Controlled evaluation corpus:** Produces 5,933 misleading documents for 100 DeepResearch Bench tasks (§4.1).
- **Workflow-level reliability evaluation:** Measures false-conclusion adoption—not mere retrieval or mention—across two open-source frameworks with three backbone LLMs and one closed-source system (§§4.1, 5.1).
- **Factor analysis:** Varies retrieval rank, lifecycle entry point, authority, style, document budget, framework, and backbone model (§§4.2–4.5).
- **Defense study:** Tests verification prompting before research, claim-level refinement after research, and their combination (§§3.4, 5.2).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Retrieval-list position had little effect after retrieval. | Mean FCAR was 65.2%, 65.7%, and 64.2% for front, middle, and back placement; 1.5-point total spread across six open-source configurations. | §4.2, Fig. 3a |
| Late exposure was substantially more harmful. | Mean FCAR was 34.5% at cold start and mid-research versus 85.0% immediately before synthesis, a 50.5-point increase; every configuration reached 77–94% late-stage FCAR. | §4.2, Fig. 3b |
| Credibility cues increased adoption. | High-, medium-, and low-authority sources produced 48.0%, 43.3%, and 36.8% mean FCAR. Paper, blog, news, and post styles produced 48.0%, 43.7%, 37.0%, and 29.7%. | §4.3, Fig. 4 |
| One misleading document could suffice. | Raising the budget from one to five documents changed mean FCAR only from 46.2% to 48.7%, without a consistent dose response. | §4.4, Fig. 5 |
| Framework choice outweighed backbone variation under matched conditions. | Mean FCAR was 66.7% for DeerFlow and 29.3% for WebThinker. Same-LLM framework gaps were 53, 34, and 25 points, versus at most 16 points across LLMs within a framework. | §4.5, Fig. 6 |
| The failure generalized to the tested closed-source system. | Gemini Deep Research reached 54% FCAR for high-authority and paper-style sources; with one versus three documents, FCAR rose from 27% to 54%. Native online search remained enabled. | §5.1, Fig. 7a–c |
| Tested defenses were incomplete and model-dependent. | Baselines were 60–76% FCAR; pre-, post-, and combined defenses yielded ranges of 37–57%, 20–58%, and 15–62%. For Intern-S1-Pro, the combination reached 62%, worse than either defense alone (57% and 58%). | §5.2, Fig. 7d–f |
| Automated FCAR judgments closely matched one human annotator. | On 300 randomly sampled reports: 99.0% agreement, κ=0.978, 99.0% accuracy, 98.46% precision, 100.0% recall, and 99.22% F1. | Appendix B |

The evidence establishes susceptibility within controlled, simulated exposure conditions across the evaluated systems and tasks. It supports associations between adoption and timing, presentation, framework design, and defenses, but does not establish prevalence on the uncontrolled public web. Explanations of why WebThinker and DeerFlow differ are trace-based interpretations rather than separately validated causal mechanisms (§4.5).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-defined scope:** This is a content-level reliability test, not prompt injection or an active corpus-poisoning attack; misleading documents are inserted into isolated retrieval pools rather than published online (§3.2).
- **Controlled task sample:** Evaluation covers 100 DeepResearch Bench tasks, so generalization to other task distributions, languages, or professional settings is not established (§4.1).
- **System coverage:** The main comparison contains two open-source frameworks and three backbones; the closed-source result concerns one system with a different local-source exposure mechanism (§§4.1, 5.1).
- **Analyst inference—judge dependence:** Most reports are scored by one LLM judge. Appendix B shows strong agreement with one human annotator on 300 reports, but does not establish agreement across multiple annotators or all experimental conditions.
- **Analyst inference—synthetic evidence realism:** Documents are generated around predefined false conclusions. Authority and style are controlled, but ecological similarity to naturally occurring misinformation is not quantitatively validated in the supplied text.
- **Analyst inference—limited statistical reporting:** The supplied results provide rates and point differences but no uncertainty intervals or significance tests.
- **Reproducibility gap:** Appendix E names the prompt templates but the retrieved text does not include their contents; release and access details for code, corpus, and annotations are also not established.

## Open research questions

1. **Would continuous verification at every state transition outperform endpoint defenses?** Existing pre/post defenses remain incomplete (§5.2). A next experiment could verify each newly retrieved claim before it enters memory and compare FCAR, report quality, and computational cost.

2. **Do the findings transfer to naturally occurring misinformation?** The study uses generated, controlled documents (§3.2). A blinded evaluation on archived real-world corrections, retractions, or disputed claims could compare synthetic and natural exposure effects.

3. **What causes the sharp late-stage vulnerability?** The timing effect is large, while the proposed explanation centers on reduced opportunity to challenge evidence (§4.2). An intervention could vary remaining search steps independently of insertion time and inspect evidence retention and citation trajectories.

4. **Can defenses avoid harmful re-retrieval?** Combined defenses sometimes underperform individual defenses because refinement uses the same mixed environment (§5.2). A plausible test would compare source-separated verification, provenance-aware retrieval, and the paper’s same-pool refinement.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported design and numerical results because the supplied text includes the methodology, settings, figures’ reported values, and judge-validation statistics.

Material missing information includes the actual contents of Tables 1–3, complete Appendix E prompt templates, per-task or domain-level results, uncertainty estimates, compute and cost details, full human-annotation protocol, corpus/code release status, and complete per-configuration defense values.
