# Beyond Borrowed Histories: Person-Aligned User Simulation for Interactive Role-Playing Evaluation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-31.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.27816) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27816)


## One-sentence takeaway

PALATE evaluates role-playing systems on candidate-constructed multi-turn dialogues with person-specific user simulators and satisfaction rubrics, revealing both fixed-history bias and substantial user-dependent differences hidden by aggregate rankings.

## Problem and motivation

Existing role-playing-agent benchmarks commonly score continuations of fixed, externally produced dialogue histories using user-independent rubrics. The authors identify two problems:

- Inherited history affects the candidate’s response, so scores conflate the candidate’s capability with prior character-side quality (Introduction; Figure 1; Appendix A.1).
- Role-playing satisfaction depends on individual preferences, which a fixed generic rubric may not capture (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

Because real role-play is jointly constructed over multiple turns, the authors argue that the appropriate evaluation unit is a particular user–RPA pair rather than an isolated response or universally ranked RPA.

## Approach and contributions

- **Person-aligned interactive benchmark:** PALATE trains a separate user simulator for each of five people using per-user LoRA adaptation on their natural conversations. Frozen simulators then interact freely with every candidate from fixed character openings, allowing each RPA to help construct its own trajectory (Sections 3.1–3.2; Figure 2).

- **Personalized satisfaction measurement:** For each user, a shared meta-procedure induces a frozen rubric from training conversations, turn-level satisfaction annotations, and subsequent reactions. Each resulting rubric contains four user-specific experience drivers and scores new responses together with the reaction they elicit (Section 3.3; Figure 3; Appendix C.3).

- **Three-track evaluation:** Candidate trajectories receive Personalized turn scores, shared Generic turn-quality scores, and whole-session scores for long-horizon behavior. Per-user and per-track profiles are primary; the scalar Overall score is explicitly display-only (Sections 3.3–4.2).

- **Controlled evidence against fixed histories:** Rewritten high- and degraded-quality histories preserve user turns and plot facts while varying character-side realization, demonstrating systematic effects on subsequent candidate scores (Appendix A.1).

- **Evaluation assets:** The planned release includes annotated conversations, five per-user LoRA adapters and frozen rubrics, 300 bilingual character cards, trajectory indices, prompts, configurations, scores, and aggregation code (Appendix E).

On a frozen 1,600-input subset, agreement with GPT-5.5 was weaker for Session: **0.697** for Claude Opus 4.8 and **0.529** for DeepSeek V4 Pro, compared with Personalized correlations of **0.909** and **0.924**. | Tables A.5–A.6 |

These results support the authors’ claims that fixed histories can bias evaluation, personalized rubrics can improve average agreement with the five users’ held-out satisfaction orderings, and turn, session, and person-specific quality are not interchangeable.

They do **not** establish that PALATE rankings match end-to-end human rankings across all 16 candidates, that the five simulators represent a broader user population, or that Personalized scoring is uniformly superior: U2 favored the Generic rubric, and Session rankings were notably judge-sensitive.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations**

- Only five extensively annotated users are covered, limiting population-level conclusions (Section 6).
- There is no end-to-end human-ranking reference spanning every candidate in Table 2; component validation and the main comparative evaluation are separate (Section 6).
- All human source conversations used one DeepSeek V4 Flash RPA, so learned behavior may reflect that collection environment and need not cover reactions to every candidate (Appendix B.1).
- The ten-card panel can overlap with simulator training characters; evaluation tests new trajectories and cross-candidate reuse in a familiar domain, not character-level holdout generalization (Appendix B.3).
- Session and small top-end ranking differences are sensitive to the LLM judge (Section 4.2; Table A.6).
- Full per-user tuning scored higher in the small U3/U4 ablation, but LoRA was chosen for cost, storage, deployment, and overfitting considerations (Appendix A.2).

**Analyst inferences**

- The main evaluation’s personalized results inherit any behavioral mismatch in the simulators; near-chance 2AFC performance does not by itself prove faithful preferences, exit decisions, or long-horizon behavior.
- The same user data support both simulator training and rubric induction, albeit with held-out validation, so both components may share source-environment biases.
- Personalized rubric agreement of 0.613 is meaningfully above chance but leaves substantial unexplained disagreement.
- The MiniMax-aligned comparison is limited: it reconstructs six public dimensions under PALATE’s judge and interface rather than reproducing the original unpublished protocol (Section 4.3; Appendix C.5).
- The supplied text specifies planned release artifacts, so actual availability and exact reproducibility cannot be confirmed from this paper alone.

## Open research questions

1. **Does PALATE preserve real users’ rankings across candidate RPAs?**  
   This remains open because the paper lacks an end-to-end human comparison covering all 16 candidates. A next experiment could have the same users interact with a stratified subset of candidates and compare human candidate rankings with simulator-derived rankings.

2. **How well does person-aligned evaluation scale beyond five users?**  
   The small cohort cannot establish population coverage or the amount of data needed per person. A larger study could vary cohort size and per-user annotation budgets while measuring simulator fidelity and rubric agreement.

3. **Can simulators generalize across source RPAs and unseen characters?**  
   Training histories all came from one RPA, and the evaluation panel is not a character-level holdout. A factorial experiment could hold out both source models and character cards, then test behavior fidelity and preference agreement separately.

4. **How can whole-session evaluation be made less judge-sensitive?**  
   Session rank correlations across judge families were only 0.529–0.697 on the rejudged subset. A plausible experiment would compare multi-judge ensembles, calibrated human session labels, and dimension-specific aggregation against the current single-overall-score procedure.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the paper’s stated methods, reported results, and acknowledged limitations because the supplied text includes the full main paper and appendices.

Material unavailable or unresolved in the supplied text includes:

- End-to-end human rankings over the 16 candidates.
- Statistical uncertainty or significance tests for most leaderboard differences.
- Evidence from more than five users or from character-level holdouts.
- Human validation of complete simulated long-horizon trajectories, beyond turn-level simulator tests.
- Confirmation that all planned assets were actually released and independently reproducible.
- Detailed participant demographics, which prevents assessing cohort representativeness.
