# DataPrep-Bench: Benchmarking LLMs as Training Data Preparators

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.20465) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20465)


## One-sentence takeaway

DataPrep-Bench evaluates training-data construction and pre-training utility prediction through downstream model performance, finding that synthetic domain data often hurts and that the proposed DAS metric predicts utility best across four of six evaluated domains.

## Problem and motivation

LLMs increasingly construct or select their own training data, but existing studies use different source materials, base models, training recipes, and downstream tasks, making comparisons largely anecdotal. The authors target two linked problems: converting long-form raw sources into useful supervised data and predicting a candidate dataset’s downstream training value before incurring fine-tuning costs (Sections 1 and 3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Unified benchmark:** DataPrep-Bench evaluates both data construction and data-quality prediction across six domains using shared sources, models, fine-tuning protocols, and downstream benchmarks (Sections 1 and 3).
- **Downstream-grounded construction track:** Methods convert identical raw corpora into QA data; base models are fine-tuned on those outputs jointly with Dolly-15k and evaluated on domain tasks (Sections 3.1–3.2).
- **Data-Construction-Skill:** A skill-guided agent separates reusable schemas, filtering rules, coverage requirements, and validation utilities from agent planning and execution (Section 4.1).
- **Quality-evaluation track and assets:** Metrics predict the measured downstream utility of public candidate SFT datasets, with Pearson correlation as the principal criterion; candidate pools and performance records are released (Sections 3.3–3.4).
- **Distributional Alignment Score:** DAS uses negative MMD between fixed-encoder representations of a candidate dataset and a disjoint domain proxy (Section 4.2).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Synthetic domain data frequently reduced performance relative to Dolly-15k alone. | Across two base models and several construction families; for example, Llama-3.1-8B Math fell from a Dolly-only average of 11.7 to 5.8–11.0 for all reported synthetic-data generators. | Section 5.2; Table 3 |
| Data-Construction-Skill produced a large Finance gain on Llama-3.1-8B. | Finance average rose from 15.1 with Dolly-15k alone to 34.2 with Skill, a 19.1-point absolute improvement; DataFlow-Skill scored 36.5, so Skill was not the best absolute result in that table. | Table 3 |
| Construction methods were strongly domain- and model-dependent. | On Qwen2.5-7B, DataFlow-Skill led Finance at 64.8, while agent GLM-4.7 led Math at 25.3; Skill scored below the Dolly-only Science baseline on both base models. | Tables 2, 4, and 5; Section 5.2 |
| DAS had the best or tied-best cross-model average in four domains. | DAS averages were General 0.68, Math 0.86, Science 0.72, and Medical 0.77 across Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, and Mistral-7B-v0.3. | Tables 6–7 |
| DAS exceeded \(r=0.70\) across Math, Science, and Medical at the domain-average level. | Cross-model averages were 0.86, 0.72, and 0.77, respectively; Math reached 0.93 and 0.94 on two individual base models. | Tables 6–7; Section 5.3 |
| Finance and Law remained difficult for DAS. | DAS cross-model averages were 0.18 for Finance and 0.36 for Law; each pool contained only one in-domain candidate alongside seven out-of-domain candidates. | Table 7; Section 5.3 |
| DAS incurred substantial but lower compute than two cited high-accuracy baselines. | Average time per candidate was 306.02 seconds, versus 459.37 for BERTVendi and 434.99 for Deita-Quality, excluding model loading and target-set embedding. | Table 6 |

These results establish comparative performance under the paper’s fixed sources, training protocols, candidate pools, and base models. They do not establish that DAS will generalize to other embedding models, pool compositions, scales, or domains, nor that synthetic data is generally harmful outside the tested setup. The construction results also conflate data quality and quantity because output sizes were intentionally uncapped (Section 8.2).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Finance and Law contain only one in-domain candidate each, limiting score variation and statistical power; the authors plan to expand specialized-domain pools (Sections 3.4, 5.3, and 6).
- **Author-stated:** DAS depends on a proxy dataset and fixed text embeddings; generic embeddings may not capture professional conventions in Finance and Law (Sections 4.2.3 and 5.3).
- **Author-stated:** Data-Construction-Skill underperforms in Science and some open-ended reasoning settings, potentially because its backbone produces sparse outputs and its structured extraction style mismatches narrative-answer benchmarks (Section 5.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Construction quality is confounded with yield: outputs range from hundreds to hundreds of thousands of samples, and no size-controlled ablation is reported (Table 8; Section 8.2).
- **Analyst inference:** DAS sensitivity to the proxy choice, encoder, 5,000-sample draw, and RBF bandwidth is not ablated in the supplied text (Sections 5.1.2 and 8.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Pearson estimates rely on only 8–14 candidates per domain; uncertainty intervals and repeated-sampling stability are not reported, although p-values are part of the protocol (Sections 3.3–3.4).
- **Reproducibility risk:** The complete Data-Construction-Skill specification is omitted from the paper text and delegated to an external repository (Section 9.3).

## Open research questions

1. **Does DAS remain predictive under different proxies and encoders?** Proxy and representation choice are central assumptions but lack reported ablations. A next experiment could cross every domain with multiple disjoint proxies, embedding models, sample sizes, and kernel bandwidths.

2. **How much of construction performance comes from data quality versus volume?** The benchmark deliberately permits large yield differences. A controlled experiment could equalize sample count or training tokens across generators and plot utility against data budget.

3. **Can construction skills handle open-ended scientific reasoning?** Skill trails the Dolly-only baseline in Science on both construction base models. A next experiment could compare structured extraction with reasoning-chain and narrative-case skill variants while holding the backbone and output budget fixed.

4. **Can richer signals improve Finance and Law prediction?** All tested metrics struggle in these domains, and the pools are highly imbalanced. A next experiment could expand in-domain candidates and combine DAS with domain-aware embeddings or reference-free quality scores, as proposed in Section 6.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the benchmark design and tabulated comparisons, because the supplied text includes complete methods, protocols, tables, and appendices.

Material missing or unclear:

- No robustness or confidence intervals for construction scores.
- No DAS ablations over proxy, encoder, bandwidth, or sampling seed.
- No size- or token-matched construction comparison.
- No complete in-paper content for the released skill.
- Significance markings from Tables 6–7 are not reliably preserved in the retrieved Markdown, so significance cannot be assigned to every displayed correlation.
- Release licenses, artifact availability details, and full computational cost of downstream fine-tuning are not specified.
