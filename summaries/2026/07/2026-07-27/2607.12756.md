# VisCo: Leveraging Large Language Models as Intrinsic Encoders for Visual Token Compression

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.12756) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12756)


## One-sentence takeaway

VisCo compresses VLM visual inputs into layer-wise memory-token KV caches using the frozen model’s own backbone plus lightweight encoder-side LoRA, retaining 85.3% of LLaVA-1.5-7B’s reported average performance with one token while adding a one-time encoding cost.

## Problem and motivation

VLMs can produce hundreds of visual tokens, increasing self-attention latency and KV-cache memory and hindering real-time or resource-constrained deployment. The authors argue that training-free pruning and merging lose global or fine-grained information under tight token budgets, while training-based alternatives commonly require external modules or extensive backbone adaptation. They identify an underused capability: the pretrained VLM itself already has strong information-encoding priors that could support compression with lighter training (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Intrinsic asymmetric autoencoder:** VisCo uses the pretrained VLM as both encoder and decoder with shared backbone parameters. Only encoder-side LoRA adapters and learnable memory tokens are trained; decoding removes LoRA and freezes the backbone (Sections 3.1–3.3, pp. 3–5).

2. **Native causal aggregation:** Memory tokens are appended after visual tokens, allowing them to attend to all preceding visual tokens through the model’s existing causal attention pattern, without a custom interaction module (Section 3.2.1, pp. 3–4).

3. **Hierarchical KV transfer:** Rather than forwarding final encoder states, VisCo stores each layer’s memory-token keys and values and inserts them directly into the corresponding decoder KV cache as prefixes (Sections 3.2.2–3.3, pp. 4–5; Figure 3).

4. **Lightweight supervised adaptation:** Training uses teacher forcing for one epoch on roughly 10% of LLaVA-665K; trainable components are the memory tokens and rank-64 Q/V LoRA adapters (Sections 3.3 and 4.1, p. 5).

The evidence supports strong benchmark retention at aggressive token budgets across three tested backbones and shows that hierarchical KV propagation is materially better than the paper’s value-passing alternative. It also documents KV-memory reductions and favorable amortized latency in long-response or repeated-query settings.

It does not establish universal superiority across VLM architectures, modalities, or deployment hardware. Comparisons with VoCo-LLaMA and MatryoshkaQuery are explicitly non-equivalent because their training and settings differ. Table 4 also does not support the unqualified textual claim that VisCo+ “consistently” improves the original model, because MMVet declines.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated future scope:** Adaptive token allocation by image and extension to video remain future work (Conclusion, p. 8).

- **One-time encoding overhead:** VisCo adds an LLM-based compression pass. It trails FastV initially in the reported short-answer setting and becomes faster on average only after three turns over the same image (Section 4.6; Figure 7).

- **Limited hardware evidence:** Efficiency measurements use one 48GB NVIDIA RTX 6000 GPU, so hardware-level generality is not demonstrated (Section 4.6).

- **Analyst inference — incomplete component isolation:** The supplied ablation compares hierarchical KV passing with value passing, but does not separately isolate memory-token learning, LoRA rank or placement, each encoder layer’s contribution, or training-set size.

- **Analyst inference — comparison heterogeneity:** Baseline availability differs by backbone; VisionZip‡ and PruMerge+ use different data quantities, while stronger references use heavier and non-comparable training (Section 4.3).

- **Analyst inference — inconsistent complementary-representation claim:** VisCo+ improves five of six reported benchmarks but substantially worsens MMVet, weakening the paper’s broad claim of consistent enhancement (Table 4).

- **Analyst inference — reproducibility gap:** The text gives core hyperparameters and training data but does not establish code/checkpoint availability, random seeds, repeated-run variance, or statistical significance.

## Open research questions

1. **Can token count adapt to image complexity?** The method uses fixed budgets, and adaptive allocation is left as future work. A next experiment could learn a per-image stopping or routing policy and compare accuracy–latency Pareto curves against fixed 1/18/36-token settings.

2. **Which layers carry the information needed at extreme compression?** VisCo transfers memory KV entries from every layer, while the supplied ablation only contrasts hierarchical transfer with final-value passing. Layer-dropping or layer-group ablations could measure each depth range’s contribution.

3. **Why does VisCo+ harm MMVet despite improving five other benchmarks?** Table 4 shows that complementary memory is not uniformly beneficial. Gated fusion between original and memory KV caches, evaluated per task and question type, could test whether selective use avoids this regression.

4. **Does the approach extend efficiently to video?** Only still-image inputs are evaluated, and video is explicitly future work. A next experiment could test temporal memory tokens on video QA while measuring performance, cache growth, and encoding overhead across clip lengths.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the architecture and reported in-paper results because the supplied text includes the complete main paper, equations, tables, and experimental descriptions.
