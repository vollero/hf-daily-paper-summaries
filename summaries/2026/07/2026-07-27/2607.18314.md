# Interactive Training 2: Auditable Control Plane for Live Model Training

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.18314) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18314)


## One-sentence takeaway

Interactive Training 2 provides an open-source, trainer-agnostic control plane that safely applies human or automated requests at explicit training-loop control points while preserving an auditable event history.

## Problem and motivation

Experiment trackers expose live metrics but generally do not provide a common way to alter an active run. Researchers must implement trainer- and experiment-specific callbacks for operations such as changing learning rates, evaluating, checkpointing, or stopping.

The authors identify a gap between observing training and safely controlling it: different controllers lack a shared request protocol, training code needs authority over when changes take effect, and decisions and outcomes need a unified audit trail (Introduction; Sections 2–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Shared control protocol:** Applications register typed, optionally bounded settings and structured actions. Humans, scripts, heuristics, and LLM agents submit the same request format through a common queue (Sections 3 and 4.1).
- **Safe application semantics:** The training loop selects explicit control points, validates requests, applies accepted changes before the next step, and records success or failure (Section 4.2).
- **Auditable interface:** A customized Aim workspace combines metrics and controls with an ordered journal linking controller decisions, requests, results, evaluations, and checkpoints (Sections 1 and 4.5).
- **Multiple integration paths:** The implementation supports a Hugging Face Trainer callback, optimizer-step wrapping, and direct integration into custom PyTorch or RLVR loops (Section 4.3).
- **Optional cross-round LLM controller:** A plan/act/reflect agent can use registered controls and summaries of earlier rounds; each new round nevertheless starts with a fresh model and optimizer (Section 4.4).
- **Released artifacts:** The authors report open-source code, five workflow journals, an Aim capture, a live interface, and smoke tests (Introduction; Appendices A, D, and E).

| The released traces document substantial controller activity. | Across 47 LLM-guided rounds in five sessions: 1,207 summarized successful actions, 3.23M input tokens, 0.68M output tokens, and $36.54 at the configured GPT-5.5 prices. | Appendix D, “Execution volume” |

The evidence supports protocol breadth, traceability, and concrete examples in which prior reflections appeared in subsequent plans. It does **not** establish that LLM steering improves optimization: the authors explicitly describe the scores as contextual rather than controlled comparisons (Section 5). Successful-action counts measure execution volume, not action quality, and the text does not provide aggregate comparisons against human, heuristic, or conventional hyperparameter-optimization controllers.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:**

- Training blocks while the LLM responds, and late responses after training has advanced are not handled.
- Applications remain responsible for validating custom actions.
- Types, bounds, permissions, and human controls cannot guarantee safe or optimal actions.
- High-stakes deployment would require approval gates, budgets, rollback policies, and task-specific safety checks (Limitations).
- Released journals omit hardware, wall-clock time, and per-step telemetry (Appendix C).

**Analyst inferences supported by the text:**

- **No causal optimization claim:** Round trajectories are illustrative, not controlled comparisons, so improvements cannot be attributed confidently to the LLM or control plane (Section 5).
- **Narrow controller evaluation:** All recorded agent runs use GPT-5.5 with high reasoning effort; robustness across models, prompts, humans, heuristics, or search controllers is not established (Section 4.4; Appendix C).
- **Limited safety validation:** The demonstrated audit path includes success and states that failures are recorded, but the supplied text reports no systematic fault-injection, adversarial-request, permission-bypass, or rollback evaluation.
- **Incomplete quantitative detail:** The retrieved text names Tables 1, 4, and 5 but does not include their row contents, preventing verification of per-workflow configurations, scores, action totals, and costs.

## Open research questions

1. **Does interactive control improve training outcomes over matched alternatives?**  
   The paper explicitly avoids controlled optimization comparisons. A next experiment could compare LLM, human, heuristic, and standard search controllers under identical compute, token, and intervention budgets across multiple seeds.

2. **How reliable is the protocol under malformed, conflicting, or delayed requests?**  
   Application-specific validation and late LLM responses remain unresolved. Fault-injection tests could measure rejection correctness, latency, queue ordering, and state consistency under concurrent and delayed requests.

3. **What is the runtime cost of instrumentation and synchronous control?**  
   Hardware, wall-clock time, and per-step telemetry are absent. Benchmarking instrumented versus uninstrumented loops across integration paths could quantify throughput, pause time, and logging overhead.

4. **Which audit and recovery mechanisms are sufficient for high-stakes training?**  
   The authors state that current bounds and permissions do not guarantee safety. A next study could evaluate approval gates, action budgets, automatic rollback, and checkpoint recovery against deliberately harmful controller proposals.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly describes the architecture, intended guarantees, limitations, and several illustrative traces. However, the actual contents of Tables 1, 2, 4, and 5 are missing beyond captions or prose summaries. Material unavailable information includes per-workflow models and datasets, complete baselines and round scores, seeds beyond the separately identified Muon trace, hardware and runtime overhead, variability across repeated trials, detailed failure counts, and systematic safety or concurrency tests.
