# CAST: Game Solvers as Turn-Level Teachers for LLM Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.25308) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25308)


## One-sentence takeaway

CAST improves long-horizon LLM agents by adding solver-derived changes in cost-to-go as stable turn-level credit within RLVR, outperforming same-base trained baselines across three games and transferring better on average to two held-out agent domains.

## Problem and motivation

Long-horizon game agents usually receive only a terminal success signal, assigning identical credit to every turn and obscuring which actions caused success or failure. Existing process supervision can require costly search, additional learned evaluators, or comparable intermediate states, while supervised learning from solver trajectories covers only expert-visited states. The authors seek accurate, inexpensive feedback on states visited by the agent’s own policy (§1; §2.1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Solver-derived credit:** CAST queries a game solver before and after each LLM action and uses the decrease in cost-to-go as a shifted solver advantage: optimal progress scores \(+1\), no progress \(0\), and harmful actions negatively (§2.2, Eqs. 4–5).
- **Stable integration with RLVR:** It applies `asinh` compression and batch RMS normalization, then adds the resulting turn-level signal to GRPO’s trajectory-level outcome advantage (§2.2, Eqs. 6–8).
- **Logit-free distillation interpretation:** Under four explicit assumptions—including a soft-optimal solver and a locally small signal—the authors derive an equivalence to on-policy cross-entropy distillation using one scalar per sampled action rather than teacher logits (Theorem 2.1; Appendix C.5).
- **Practical extension:** Exact solvers are evaluated alongside a DQN-derived value estimator, testing whether approximate state values can provide similar guidance (§3.5; Appendix C.4).

The results establish strong comparative performance for this base model, training budget, and evaluation protocol, including within-game difficulty shifts and zero-shot transfer averages. They do not establish statistical significance, superiority to the strongest closed-source models overall, or broad transfer beyond the two held-out domains. The learned-value and ablation claims are mostly qualitative because their plotted endpoint values are absent from the supplied text.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated theoretical conditions:** The distillation equivalence assumes a soft-optimal solver, small solver advantages for the local approximation, GRPO consistency, and a frozen state-visitation distribution. Departures weaken or alter the interpretation (Appendix C.5.2, C.5.7).
- **Partial observability:** Minesweeper does not have the same exact shortest-path value as the other games; it uses a deterministic, peek-free solver-completion cost on a fixed latent board, and some generated boards may require probabilistic guesses (Appendix B.1; C.3).
- **Analyst inference—narrow training scope:** All primary training experiments use one 4B base model and three small, text-rendered puzzle environments. This limits claims about other model scales, modalities, stochastic environments, or open-ended tasks (§3.1).
- **Analyst inference—limited ablation coverage:** Component ablations are reported only on Sokoban, so their necessity across Minesweeper and Rush Hour is not established (§3.4).
- **Analyst inference—transfer attribution:** ALFWorld uses its seen validation split, and only ALFWorld and WebShop are tested. Their gains are compatible with transferable behavior but do not isolate which learned capability caused the improvement (Appendix B.2).
- **Analyst inference—uncertainty reporting:** Main trained results are means over three runs, but Tables 1–2 provide no standard deviations, confidence intervals, or significance tests (§3.1).
- **Analyst inference—comparison asymmetry:** Open-source and closed-source models use different decoding temperatures, and closed-source references use one run; direct cross-block comparisons therefore have additional uncertainty (Appendix C.6.2).

## Open research questions

1. **How robust is CAST to inaccurate or systematically biased value teachers?** Only one learned DQN teacher is tested qualitatively on Rush Hour. A next experiment could vary teacher error and calibration across all three games and relate value error to policy performance.
2. **Do the shaping components generalize across domains?** Ablations cover only Sokoban. A factorial ablation of `asinh`, RMS normalization, and \(\alpha\) on every game would test whether their effects depend on value scale or partial observability.
3. **Does solver-guided training transfer beyond two text-agent benchmarks?** Current OOD evidence is limited to ALFWorld and WebShop. A next experiment could evaluate unchanged agents on additional long-horizon domains with stochastic transitions and different action interfaces.
4. **How closely does practical CAST match its distillation interpretation?** The theorem relies on several approximations. Comparing CAST gradients with explicit teacher-policy cross-entropy under controlled soft solvers would directly measure the claimed equivalence.
