# CoRT: Counterfactual Replay for Token-Level Rubric-Guided Policy Optimization

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.25659) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25659)


## One-sentence takeaway

CoRT improves rubric-guided language-model reinforcement learning by using criteria-conditioned versus criteria-free token likelihoods to redistribute GRPO’s response-level advantage across tokens, without changing the reward or training an auxiliary relevance model.

## Problem and motivation

Rubric-based reinforcement learning provides criterion-level judgments about properties such as correctness and formatting, but GRPO-style pipelines collapse them into one scalar reward and broadcast the resulting advantage uniformly across every response token. This creates a credit-assignment mismatch: tokens directly responsible for satisfying a criterion receive the same update magnitude as incidental content.

The authors seek token-level credit without the separate supervision, data-generation pipeline, and relevance model required by learned approaches such as RTT (Introduction; Related Work, “Fine grained credit assignment”).

## Approach and contributions

- **Counterfactual token signal:** CoRT scores each sampled response under both the original rubric-conditioned prompt and a matched criteria-free prompt. The per-token log-likelihood difference is treated as a proxy—not a calibrated measure—for dependence on the rubric (CoRT, “Counterfactual Scoring”).

- **Advantage redistribution:** Bounded, response-normalized weights derived from those contrasts redistribute the signed GRPO advantage across tokens while preserving its response-level mean and direction (Rubric Signals in Group-Relative RL; CoRT, “Replay-Margin Token Credit” and “Policy Objective”).

- **Stable integration:** A SmoothStep activation schedule gradually introduces weighting, while response normalization keeps the average token multiplier at one. Gradients through the weights are stopped (CoRT, “Scheduled Response Normalization” and “Policy Objective”).

- **No additional labels or generation:** The method adds one criteria-free scoring pass but requires no token relevance labels, additional generation, verifier calls, or auxiliary scorer (CoRT, “Computation”).

- **Objective compatibility:** The authors also integrate CoRT with DAPO and GSPO, suggesting that its credit-allocation mechanism is not tied exclusively to standard GRPO (Results, Table 3).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope limitation:** CoRT’s signal may weaken when criteria barely affect token likelihoods, are redundant with the original prompt, or when response rewards are noisy (Conclusion).

- **Author-stated locality limitation:** The method estimates reliance only along observed trajectories. Longer-horizon attribution across turns, actions, and tool calls remains untested (Conclusion).

- **Diffuse and absence-style criteria:** Criterion-removal case studies found localized effects for visible formatting cues but weaker concentration for global properties such as language and minimum sentence count (Appendix A, Table 9 discussion).

- **Additional computation:** CoRT requires another forward scoring pass for every sampled response, although it avoids extra generation and verifier calls (CoRT, “Computation”).

- **Analyst inference—selection risk:** Most primary benchmark results select the best validation score within the first 500 steps rather than a common fixed checkpoint. Step-500 CoRT values are reportedly supplied separately, but their numbers are missing from the retrieved text (Experimental Setup, “Evaluation”; Appendix A, Table 4).

- **Analyst inference—baseline comparability:** Table 1 baseline rows were taken from RTT, while CoRT rows were newly run under the stated shared protocol. The text says reward settings and evaluation protocols match, but does not provide enough detail to verify complete run-level equivalence (Table 1 caption).

- **Analyst inference—uncertainty:** No repeated-seed analysis, confidence intervals, or significance tests appear in the supplied text, limiting conclusions about robustness.

- **Analyst inference—attribution validity:** The likelihood contrast measures sensitivity to removing rubric text, not causal token importance or correctness. The authors explicitly describe it only as a proxy (CoRT, “Counterfactual Scoring”).

## Open research questions

1. **How reliable is replay contrast as token-level attribution?**  
   The paper provides qualitative and control examples but no supplied large-scale comparison against human token annotations. A next experiment could measure localization precision and recall on independently annotated criterion-bearing spans.

2. **Does CoRT help with global, semantic, or absence-based criteria?**  
   Criterion-wise controls suggest that global requirements produce diffuse signals. An experiment could stratify results by criterion type and compare full-list removal with criterion-specific interventions.

3. **How sensitive are gains to checkpoint selection and randomness?**  
   Primary results use best-within-500-step validation selection, and no multi-seed uncertainty is reported. Repeating matched GRPO and CoRT runs across seeds at fixed checkpoints would quantify variance and statistical reliability.

4. **Can the mechanism extend beyond single-turn token credit?**  
   The authors identify multi-turn actions and tool calls as future work. A plausible test would apply criterion-specific replay at each turn of a controlled tool-use benchmark and compare token-, turn-, and action-level allocation.
