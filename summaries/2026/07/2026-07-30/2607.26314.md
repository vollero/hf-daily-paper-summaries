# StealthBench: Measuring Operational Stealth in Autonomous Offensive-Security Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.26314) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26314)


## One-sentence takeaway

StealthBench finds that autonomous offensive-security agents frequently solve tasks while leaving avoidable operational traces, with no evaluated model exceeding 53.8% safe success across its dockerized scenarios.

## Problem and motivation

Existing offensive-security benchmarks primarily measure whether agents can discover or exploit vulnerabilities, not whether they do so without leaking credentials, triggering defenses, contaminating artifacts, or altering target-owned resources. The authors argue that this missing operational-stealth axis matters both for safe deployment of offensive agents and for building defensive monitors capable of detecting their OPSEC failures (Section 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Operational-stealth benchmark:** Eleven human-verified incidents from bug-bounty trajectories are converted into 14 dockerized scenarios spanning six OPSEC dimensions: credential handling, destructive operations, detection cascades, telemetry injection, artifact contamination, and noise discipline (Sections 2.3 and 3.1).
- **Trajectory-level evaluation:** Agents receive neutral prompts and interact with vulnerable applications using deliberation and shell tools. Complete trajectories are evaluated by three LLM judges using task-specific rubrics and majority voting (Figure 1; Sections 2.4–2.5).
- **Compound performance metrics:** Safe success jointly measures task completion and a stealth-panel pass; Stealth@Solve measures tradecraft among solved runs; reckless solve rate measures successful but non-stealthy runs (Section 2.6).
- **Evaluation assets:** The authors report releasing the benchmark dataset, dockerized harness, interactive leaderboard, sanitized ATIF trajectories, and OpenTelemetry exports (Abstract; Figure 1; Section 3.1).
- **Empirical characterization:** Eight models are evaluated, with judge consistency measured using pairwise Cohen’s κ, Fleiss’ κ, and split-vote rates (Sections 3.1–3.2 and 4.4).

Although Section 1 says there are four contributions, the supplied text contains substantive content for only three numbered entries; the fourth entry is blank.

The evidence establishes frequent OPSEC failures in this particular containerized benchmark and strong agreement among the selected LLM judges. It does not establish agreement with human experts, real-world detectability, causal effects of training data, or broad generalization beyond the evaluated agents, prompts, scenarios, and harness.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:**

- The source corpus contains only 11 incidents from limited offensive-security contexts, and the six dimensions are not exhaustive (Section 6).
- The benchmark does not cover social engineering, attacks on ML models, physical-security robots, or several proposed OPSEC dimensions such as timing discipline and protocol conformance (Section 6).
- All current judging uses full trajectories; reduced-context judging was not ablated (Sections 2.4 and 6).
- No human-expert labels validate judge accuracy. High inter-judge agreement demonstrates consistency, not correctness (Sections 4.4 and 6).
- Every judge model is also among the evaluated agent models, creating a risk of correlated blind spots despite hidden agent identities (Sections 3.2 and 4.4).
- The eight-trajectory qualitative sample is purposive and cannot estimate mechanism prevalence; generated reasoning may be incomplete or post-hoc (Sections 3.1, 4.3, and 6).
- The observational design cannot separate training-data effects from prompting, scaffolding, search policy, stopping behavior, or environmental constraints (Sections 1 and 6).
- A 30-second command timeout may encourage bypass behavior after agents enter a path where proper backoff would require 60 seconds (Section 4.3).
- Refusing models are excluded rather than treated as stealth outcomes, so results apply to models that engage under their default safety configuration (Section 3.1).

**Analyst inferences:**

- Unequal per-model run counts and accumulated reruns may leave comparisons sensitive to provider reliability or differing run mixtures, although the authors normalize rates by each model’s valid-run count.
- Fail-closed handling of malformed judge outputs can introduce false failure labels; the reported 0.6% individual-verdict rate limits but does not eliminate this concern.
- The supplied text omits the numerical contents of several central tables and the complete hyperparameter table, restricting independent checking of task balance and model-level results.
- Purpose-built tasks with explicit safe and reckless paths support controlled comparison but may not reproduce the ambiguity, defender behavior, or operational constraints of live engagements.

## Open research questions

1. **Do LLM panel verdicts agree with offensive-security experts?** This remains open because only inter-model agreement is reported. A next experiment could obtain blinded labels from multiple practitioners and measure judge–human κ by stealth dimension.

2. **Which interventions reduce reckless solves without suppressing capability?** The paper identifies missing stopping rules and resource provenance as possible mechanisms but does not test them causally. A factorial experiment could compare explicit sufficient-proof stopping prompts, provenance labels, pre-execution gates, and an unchanged baseline.

3. **How much trajectory context is necessary for reliable and economical monitoring?** Only full-context judging is evaluated. A controlled ablation could compare rubric-only metadata, actions only, truncated histories, and complete trajectories on the same human-labeled cases.

4. **Does benchmark stealth predict real defensive visibility?** The paper proposes defensive transfer but does not evaluate operational detection systems. A next experiment could replay matched safe and reckless runs under instrumented WAF, SIEM, and EDR systems and compare alert rates with panel verdicts.
