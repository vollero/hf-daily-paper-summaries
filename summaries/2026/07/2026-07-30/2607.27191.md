# Can AI agents conduct open-ended AI research? Early evidence from two case studies

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.27191) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27191)


## One-sentence takeaway

In two expert-graded shadow evaluations, frontier agents autonomously completed substantial research engineering but produced papers that original authors decisively rejected, primarily because of weak experimental judgment, ineffective backtracking, poor use of feedback and resources, and instruction drift.

## Problem and motivation

The paper asks whether AI agents can autonomously conduct open-ended AI research, a capability central to forecasts of rapid or recursive AI progress. The authors argue that existing evidence is inadequate: verifier-based benchmarks reduce research to optimizing fixed metrics, while blind peer review of generated papers is noisy, potentially selective, and often lacks deep scrutiny. The identified gap is an evaluation that combines uncontaminated, open-ended questions with detailed expert assessment (Sections 1–2, pp. 2–6).

## Approach and contributions

- **Shadow evaluation:** An agent receives the central question from a high-quality unpublished paper, works without access to that paper’s findings, and is graded by its original authors as a conference submission (Section 2, pp. 5–6).
- **Two long-horizon case studies:** Opus 4.8 agents using OpenClaw addressed an LLM-persona problem and a TabPFN distribution-shift problem, with six days, a $3,000 API budget, GPU credits, a VM, web access, subagents, and AI-review tools (Section 3, p. 7; Appendix A, p. 24).
- **Trajectory-level diagnosis:** Log analysis identifies five recurring failure modes: poor research judgment, limited creative response to design shortcomings, ineffective project-level backtracking, weak resource awareness, and instruction drift (Section 5, pp. 14–17).
- **Robustness experiment:** A repeat of the TabPFN task with GPT-5.6 Sol and its Codex scaffold reproduced most failure modes, although its resource failure involved exhausting the token budget too early rather than underspending it (Section 6, p. 17).
- **Released evidence:** The authors report releasing expert reviews, survey responses, agent repositories, and run logs, subject to nondisclosure of detailed artifacts for one still-unpublished source paper (Section 2, p. 6; footnote 3, p. 4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Neither main run met the publication bar. | Personas received **2/6 (Reject)** and TabPFN **1/6 (Strong Reject)** from original-paper authors; reviewer confidence was **4/5** and **5/5**, respectively. | Table 1, p. 3; Section 4.1, p. 9 |
| Engineering execution was broadly successful. | Agents conducted literature reviews, managed GPU jobs, ran hundreds of experiments and robustness checks, obtained external reviews, and compiled complete LaTeX papers; the only unresolved environment issue was an OpenClaw bug patched by humans. | Section 4.5, p. 12 |
| Exploration ended earlier than planned. | Personas coalesced after **5 hours** despite budgeting **42 hours**; the TabPFN agent falsified six approaches within **14 hours** and did not revise its solution approach despite **110 hours** remaining under the original deadline. | Figure 2, p. 11; footnote 13, p. 16 |
| AI feedback detected problems but did not induce strategic revision. | Across **15 self-review rounds**, no draft received an acceptance; agents generally narrowed claims or added caveats instead of redesigning the work. Human and agent reviews identified overlapping concerns, but prioritized them differently. | Section 4.4 and Figure 3, pp. 11–12; Table 4, p. 13 |
| OpenClaw agents underused API budgets. | Personas spent **$1,130 of $3,000** and TabPFN **$1,235 of $3,000**; both ended with less than half of the API budget used. | Figure 1, p. 10; Section 5.4, p. 16 |
| The Codex robustness run showed a different resource failure. | GPT-5.6 Sol exhausted the **$3,000** token budget in just over **two days**, leaving nearly **100 hours**, and produced underpowered experiments, no novel contribution, misformatted figures, and no appendices. | Section 6, p. 17 |
| Submission instructions were violated. | Both main papers exceeded the **nine-page** limit by extending onto a tenth page; TabPFN used **36 vs. 69** references and Personas **16 vs. 52**, compared with the corresponding original papers. | Section 4.6, pp. 12–13 |
| No significant final-output reward hacking was found. | Review of raw calls and committed code found no concealment or misrepresentation; however, one run committed an access token, and subagents misstated results five times before the orchestrator caught them. | Section 4.7, p. 13 |
| Higher reasoning effort improved some output qualities. | Two non-reasoning pilot runs had poorer literature reviews and writing and were not sent for original-author review; this comparison was qualitative rather than a scored controlled ablation. | Section 4.8, p. 14 |

The evidence supports the authors’ narrower claim that the tested agents and scaffolds failed on these two open-ended, conference-level empirical research questions despite strong engineering performance. It also provides qualitative evidence that similar failures survived one model-and-scaffold change. It does **not** establish that AI agents generally cannot conduct research, isolate which component caused failure, validate AI reviewers on acceptable papers, or show that more time, better elicitation, or future systems would not change performance.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Small and selected sample:** The evidence comes from five runs—two pilots, two main runs, and one robustness run—covering only two empirical ML questions. Generalization to incremental, theoretical, or otherwise structured research is unknown (Table 3, p. 8).
- **Non-blind, subjective grading:** Reviewers knew the submissions were AI-generated and had authored papers addressing the same questions, potentially favoring their own framing or methods (Sections 2 and 7; Table 3, pp. 6, 8).
- **Researcher discretion and prior beliefs:** Task selection, scaffold design, interventions, and qualitative log interpretation involved substantial judgment. The authors disclose that some core-team members held skeptical priors about imminent recursive self-improvement (Section 8, p. 19).
- **Elicitation uncertainty:** OpenClaw context-reset failures occurred **14 times** in TabPFN and **5 times** in Personas. The Codex robustness scaffold received less optimization than the main scaffold, and the strongest Anthropic model was unavailable (Section 7.1, pp. 17–18).
- **Unequal human and agent effort:** Agents received six days, while original authors worked much longer and used more GPU time. The authors argue unused budgets and qualitative reviewer objections reduce this concern, but it remains a scope difference (Section 7.1, p. 18).
- **No positive-paper test for AI reviewers:** Since both generated papers were rejects, repeated AI rejection does not establish reviewer discrimination or calibration (Section 4.4, p. 11).
- **Analyst inference — limited causal attribution:** Because model, scaffold, task, budget allocation, interventions, and context failures varied without a factorial design, the study cannot cleanly assign failure to any one factor.
- **Analyst inference — pilot comparison is not a strong reasoning ablation:** The non-reasoning pilots lacked external expert scores, so the claimed benefit of higher reasoning effort rests on qualitative comparisons (Section 4.8, p. 14).

## Open research questions

1. **Does shadow-evaluation performance generalize across research types?** Two empirical ML questions cannot establish performance on theoretical, incremental, or differently scoped work. A next experiment could preregister a larger, stratified set of unpublished questions across research categories.

2. **Which interventions improve project-level backtracking?** Agents recognized many weaknesses but rarely restarted. A controlled experiment could compare standard scaffolds against explicit abstention, forced-restart checkpoints, and clean-context alternative teams while holding model, task, and budget fixed.

3. **Can AI reviewers distinguish strong from weak open-ended research?** This paper observes only rejected agent outputs. A next study could blind AI reviewers to a balanced collection of accepted-quality and rejected shadow submissions and measure calibration and ranking agreement with experts.

4. **How sensitive are results to time, compute, and elicitation?** The OpenClaw runs underspent while the Codex run exhausted its token budget early, leaving the optimal allocation unresolved. A factorial experiment could vary wall-clock time, inference budget, scaffold, and resource-monitoring support on repeated tasks.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported setup, scores, resource use, and stated failure modes because the supplied text is complete and provides detailed tables, figures, reviews, and limitations.

Material missing or unresolved information includes full per-run experimental outputs for the still-unpublished case, independent blinded regrading, a larger task sample, controlled model/scaffold/resource ablations, quantitative inter-rater reliability, and positive examples needed to validate the AI-review process.
