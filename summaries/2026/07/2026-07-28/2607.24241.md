# FilmBench: A Film-Grade Benchmark for Cinematic Video Generation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.24241) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24241)


## One-sentence takeaway

FilmBench evaluates text- and reference-conditioned video generation against professional cinematic-language criteria, finding strong expert-ranking agreement but persistent weaknesses in multi-shot construction, dynamic aesthetics, and camera craft.

## Problem and motivation

Existing video-generation benchmarks mainly use web- or template-derived prompts and generic criteria such as visual quality, text alignment, and temporal smoothness. The authors argue that these benchmarks can produce near-ceiling scores while overlooking professional requirements including shot scale, camera movement, composition, lighting, performance, editing, and audio continuity (§1).

FilmBench addresses this gap by evaluating whether generated videos follow director-level cinematic intent, using prompts tied to verified film clips rather than generic web content. It covers both text-to-video (T2V) and reference-to-video (R2V), with particular emphasis on realistic multi-shot shot lists (§§1, 3.1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Film-grounded benchmark:** FilmBench contains 1,169 prompts reverse-engineered from distinct award-winning film clips selected across 20 cinematic genres. Directors refine automatically drafted descriptions into structured prompts; 1,056 prompts are multi-shot (§3.1).

- **Two generation tasks:** The benchmark includes 515 T2V prompts and 654 R2V prompts. R2V covers scene, prop, and character references (§3.1).

- **Professional evaluation taxonomy:** It organizes evaluation into Instruction Following, Temporal Continuity, and Aesthetic Quality, decomposed into 12 components and 35 sub-metrics. R2V adds three reference-fidelity metrics (§3.2; Tables 1, 3–4).

- **FilmOps and automatic evaluation:** The authors release a modular suite of six cinematic-language operators, trained on film/TV data, plus weights and inference scripts. An evaluation agent combines these operators, expert annotation models, and a judge model (§§4.1–4.2; Appendix G).

- **Comparative evaluation:** Nine T2V and seven R2V systems are tested with one generated video per prompt, producing 4,635 T2V and 4,578 R2V scored records (§5.1).

These results establish that FilmBench’s aggregate model ordering is strongly aligned with the sampled experts and that its fine-grained metrics reveal distinct weaknesses hidden by an overall score. They do not establish sample-level evaluator accuracy, generalization to non-cinematic video, causal reasons for model differences, or superiority over prior benchmarks under a controlled head-to-head evaluation; the supplied text only contrasts its results with previously reported near-ceiling scores.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope limitation:** Rankings may not transfer to vertical short-form video, casual user-generated content, or other non-cinematic generation (§6).

- **Author-acknowledged evaluator weakness:** Human and automatic judgments diverge more for editing, audio coherence, and audio quality (§5.2).

- **Analyst inference — model-level validation:** Agreement is computed from rankings of model means, so high \(\rho\) does not demonstrate reliable scoring of individual videos or prompts (§5.2).

- **Analyst inference — source distribution:** Prompts originate from director-selected, award-winning films. This supports cinematic relevance but may underrepresent ordinary productions or cinematic styles outside the selected 20-genre and Chinese/international grouping (§3.1).

- **Analyst inference — generation variance:** Each system produces only one video per prompt under default settings, leaving seed sensitivity, prompt-format sensitivity, and best-of-\(n\) behavior unmeasured (§5.1).

- **Analyst inference — aggregation choices:** All applicable L3 metrics and the three L1 axes are equally weighted, while unavailable audio dimensions are excluded. Rankings could depend on these weighting and missing-value conventions; no alternative-weighting ablation is reported (§4.2).

- **Analyst inference — task comparisons:** T2V and R2V use different prompt sets, and every R2V prompt is multi-shot. Differences between their absolute scores therefore cannot be attributed solely to reference conditioning (§§3.1, 5.9).

- **Reproducibility gap in supplied text:** FilmOps training ranges, backbones, and release contents are described, but exact training splits, hyperparameters, evaluator prompts, costs, and complete per-operator numeric results are not included in the retrieved text (§4.1; Table 12 is referenced but its values are absent).

## Open research questions

1. **Can evaluator agreement improve for editing and audio?** These components have the lowest reported correlations. A next experiment could train specialized temporal/audio judges and compare sample-level correlations and ranking stability against the current evaluator.

2. **How stable are rankings across aggregation policies?** Equal weighting is a design choice without a reported sensitivity study. Recompute rankings using expert-derived weights, worst-dimension aggregation, and explicit penalties for unsupported audio.

3. **What causes the multi-shot deficit?** The paper localizes degradation to editing, framing, camera movement, and spatial coherence but does not isolate planning from rendering. A controlled experiment could hold visual content constant while varying only shot count, transition structure, and shot-list complexity.

4. **How well does FilmBench transfer beyond its curated film distribution?** The authors explicitly exclude non-cinematic settings. A next evaluation could apply the taxonomy, with adapted rubrics, to short-form, user-generated, documentary, and experimental-video subsets and revalidate human agreement.
