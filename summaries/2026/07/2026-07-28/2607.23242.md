# IndicTalk: A Large-Scale Persona-Based Multilingual Conversational Corpus for Indic Languages

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.23242) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.23242)


## One-sentence takeaway

IndicTalk is a planned public corpus of 1,328,604 synthetic, news-grounded, persona-conditioned conversations covering native-script and Romanized English code-mixing for nine Indic languages, with generally favorable—but limited—automatic, LLM-judge, and human quality assessments.

## Problem and motivation

Conversational resources for Indic code-mixing are scarce despite bilingual speakers routinely alternating between English and Indic languages in both native and Romanized scripts. Existing Indic code-mixed datasets largely address sentence-level discriminative tasks, while dialogue datasets tend to cover only Hinglish, few languages, translated conversations, or narrow domains. The authors identify the absence of a large, unified, event-grounded, multi-turn resource as a bottleneck for training, instruction-tuning, and evaluating multilingual conversational models (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Large multilingual corpus:** IndicTalk contains 1,328,604 six-to-eight-turn conversations across nine Indic languages and two script conventions, yielding 18 language varieties (Tables 5–6, pp. 5–6).
- **Automated construction pipeline:** Public news and blog documents are cleaned and summarized into six-to-eight factual English sentences by Sarvam-M. GPT-OSS-120B then generates separate persona-conditioned conversations for every target variety (Sections 3.1–3.4, pp. 3–4).
- **Persona and script diversity:** Dialogues use five persona categories—friends, family members, colleagues, experts, and student–teacher—and either native-script or fully Romanized code-mixing (Table 3 and Section 3.4, pp. 4–5).
- **Automatic filtering:** Native-script utterances must contain both target-script and Roman-script tokens, meet an unspecified minimum CMI, and exceed an unspecified token threshold. Romanized text receives script and length checks but no CMI filter (Section 3.5, p. 4).
- **Multi-method assessment:** The paper reports code-mixing statistics, mBERT pseudo-perplexity for native-script text, two LLM judges, and ratings from proficient human speakers (Sections 4.2–4.5, pp. 5–7).

These results establish that the retained synthetic conversations satisfy the authors’ structural filters and receive broadly favorable ratings on sampled evaluations. They do not establish usefulness for model training or downstream tasks, superiority to an existing conversational corpus, factual faithfulness to source documents, or equivalence to naturally occurring bilingual dialogue. The reported overall LLM-judge correlation of ρ=0.91 on p. 6 is also not reconciled with the script-specific correlations of 0.745 and 0.778 on p. 8.

## Limitations and validity risks

The authors acknowledge that synthetic conversations may omit pragmatic nuance, dialect diversity, and natural disfluencies. News and blog grounding biases the corpus toward formal, event-centric discussion. Human raters—2–3 graduate students per variety—may not represent regional dialects or Romanization conventions, while PPPL is unavailable for Romanized text (Limitations, p. 8).

Analyst inferences supported by the text:

- Human evaluation is narrow: only 10 conversations per variety, against tens of thousands of generated examples.
- GPT-OSS-120B both generates the corpus and serves as one judge. Gemini provides an independent judge, but no human-versus-model calibration at the conversation level is reported.
- Dialogue-model selection is based on development observations and illustrative examples rather than a quantified controlled comparison (Section 3.6 and Appendix E).
- The automatic filter thresholds `τCMI` and `τlen` are not numerically specified, limiting exact reproduction.
- No baseline corpus comparison, downstream training experiment, factual-grounding evaluation, filtering retention rate, inter-annotator agreement, or uncertainty intervals are reported.
- Native-script CMI is enforced during filtering, so high CMI partly reflects a construction constraint rather than an independently emerging property.
- Romanized CMI appears in Table 7 even though Section 3.5 says token-level identification is unreliable; the paper does not fully explain how those values were computed or validated.

## Open research questions

1. **Does IndicTalk improve conversational models?** No downstream training results are reported. Fine-tune matched models with and without IndicTalk and compare held-out response quality across all 18 varieties.

2. **How closely does the synthetic corpus resemble real bilingual conversation?** The authors explicitly note missing pragmatic, dialectal, and disfluency variation. Compare IndicTalk with consented natural conversations using human naturalness judgments and matched code-switching distributions.

3. **Are conversations factually faithful to their source documents?** Event grounding is central to the pipeline, but factual consistency is not directly measured. Annotate source–summary and source–dialogue entailment on a stratified multilingual sample.

4. **How robust is quality across Romanization conventions and speaker populations?** Current annotators may not cover regional variation. Recruit geographically and demographically broader raters, then report subgroup scores and inter-annotator agreement.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported corpus design and aggregate results because the supplied text includes the complete retrieved paper and its main tables.

Material missing or unclear information includes exact validation thresholds, generation/filter failure rates, duplicate detection, source licensing and provenance details, full PII-removal performance, annotator demographics and compensation, inter-annotator agreement, confidence intervals, conversation-level judge correlations, factuality measurements, downstream utility, controlled dialogue-generator comparisons, and resolution of the conflicting LLM-judge correlation reports.
