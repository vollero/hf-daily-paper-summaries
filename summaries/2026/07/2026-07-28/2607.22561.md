# Codifying the Judge: Scalable Evaluation via Program Distillation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.22561) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22561)


## One-sentence takeaway

PAJAMA distills an LLM’s pairwise evaluation logic into calibrated committees of executable programs, delivering competitive judgment accuracy at much higher throughput and selectively escalating uncertain cases to an LLM.

## Problem and motivation

LLM judges are widely used for preference labeling and reward-model supervision, but the authors identify four barriers: per-sample inference cost, latency and GPU demand, opaque decision logic, systemic stylistic biases, and the need to rerun an entire dataset after rubric changes. Existing weak-supervision and routing methods do not directly turn reusable judging logic into transparent executable evaluators. The proposed shift is to pay for LLM synthesis once, then execute and edit local programs at scale (§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Program distillation:** An LLM synthesizes Python scoring functions from ten code-expressible rubrics and ten validation examples; a similarity filter encourages diversity (§3.2, Appendix A).
- **Calibrated committee:** Each program’s scores are min–max normalized, converted to preference/abstention votes using validation-tuned thresholds, filtered by validation accuracy, and aggregated with a weak-supervision label model (§3.3).
- **Confidence-aware hybrid evaluation:** Uncovered or uncertain pairs are routed to an LLM using committee vote variance or aggregator posterior confidence (§3.4).
- **Reusable supervision:** Program verdicts label preference pairs for reward-model training without per-example proprietary-model calls (§4.3).
- **Editable evaluators:** A coding agent modifies exposed program logic to reduce selected evaluation biases (§4.4).

The results support the authors’ claim that program committees can offer a strong accuracy–throughput trade-off on the filtered textual preference tasks tested, and that their outputs can provide useful routing and reward-model supervision. They do not establish equivalence to proprietary judges generally: PAJAMA’s 78.11% average remains 9.57 points below GPT-4.1’s 87.68%, and the evaluation excludes ambiguous, mathematical, and coding cases. Several routing, cost, and RewardBench claims cannot be independently checked from the supplied text because their full numerical tables or operating points are absent.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Quality depends on the synthesis LLM’s ability to understand the task and generate effective programs; inherited errors or biases can degrade labels (Appendix E).
- **Author-stated:** The approach is best suited to readily evaluable candidates and may not generalize to complex mathematics or coding (Appendix E).
- **Author-stated:** Internal routing signals remain below an oracle router, leaving uncertainty detection unresolved (§4.2, Figure 5).
- **Analyst inference:** Filtering out ties, low-consensus cases, mathematics, and coding narrows the demonstrated validity to decisive, rubric-oriented textual preferences (Appendix B.1).
- **Analyst inference:** The same 500-example split supports threshold tuning, program selection, and aggregation; although validation-size sensitivity is tested, the supplied text does not report separate ablations for these components (§3.3, Appendix D.1).
- **Analyst inference:** The main study uses programs synthesized by one named model, one hardware setup, and CPU/GPU systems with different execution regimes, so portability of quality and throughput ratios is not established (§4.1, Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference:** Random labels fill committee abstentions in standalone evaluation. Coverage is generally high but falls to 89.49% on PandaLM, making results partly sensitive to this policy (Table 4).
- **Analyst inference:** Coding-agent calibration improves aggregate bias metrics, but rich-content bias win rate worsens; editing can therefore trade one robustness measure against another (Table 2).

## Open research questions

1. **How well does program distillation work for mathematics, coding, or other verifier-heavy tasks?** These domains were excluded. A next experiment could synthesize judges with execution or symbolic-verification tools and compare them with pure programs and LLM fallback.

2. **Can synthesis quality transfer across synthesis models and domains?** The main experiment uses Claude Opus 4.6 and dataset-specific examples. A controlled factorial study could vary synthesis model, rubric source, and target dataset while holding the aggregation pipeline fixed.

3. **Can richer committee signals close the oracle-routing gap?** Posterior confidence and vote variance remain below the oracle. A next experiment could train a calibrated router from program traces, abstention patterns, coverage, and feature-level outputs, then evaluate it under fixed escalation budgets.

4. **Does bias calibration preserve ordinary evaluation accuracy?** Table 2 measures bias robustness but does not report post-edit accuracy on the five standard preference datasets. Re-evaluating calibrated committees on both clean preferences and bias probes would quantify the robustness–utility trade-off.

## Confidence and missing information
