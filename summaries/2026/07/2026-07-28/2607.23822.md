# DriveDNA: A Large-Scale Multimodal Naturalistic Driving Dataset and Benchmark for Driving Style Identification

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.23822) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.23822)


## One-sentence takeaway

DriveDNA shows that learned vehicle-motion representations retain driver-specific information after controlling for vehicle model and driving context, but high re-identification scores—especially from video—can reflect route and contextual shortcuts rather than driving style.

## Problem and motivation

The paper studies whether stable, driver-specific behavior can be identified in naturalistic driving data. This matters for personalized driver assistance and behavior prediction, but everyday logs entangle driver identity with vehicle, route, and traffic exposure. The authors argue that existing datasets lack the scale, persistent identities, vehicle diversity, and explicit confound controls needed to separate realized driving behavior from these shortcuts (Section 1).

They operationalize driving style as stable patterns in realized vehicle motion—such as acceleration and curvature—under comparable contexts, rather than as raw control inputs or personality labels (Section 1).

## Approach and contributions

1. **DriveDNA dataset.** The authors release 4,121 decoded drives from 465 drivers and 115 vehicle models, comprising 975 hours of human-controlled 10 Hz telemetry with forward video. The public tier includes de-identified signals, frozen video embeddings, splits, code, and evaluation tools; raw blurred video is gated (Sections 3 and 7).

2. **Confound-aware benchmark.** Three core tasks test distinct aspects of style: few-shot driver re-identification, personalized future-motion prediction, and verification on 14,868 condition-matched pairs. Additional splits control vehicle model, cross-vehicle transfer, and missing channels (Section 4).

3. **Behavioral annotation assets.** The dataset supplies six scenario labels, eight weak behavioral primitives, and 276,248 rule-generated maneuver events across six classes, with human auditing. Verified lane changes and rejected hard negatives are released separately (Sections 3.3–3.4; Appendix D).

4. **Baseline and leakage suite.** Thirty configurations span classical descriptors, supervised and self-supervised time-series encoders, multimodal fusion, probabilistic prediction, and zero-shot foundation models. Vehicle, route, and condition probes quantify shortcut leakage under fixed, three-seed evaluation (Sections 4.3–5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Learned representations outperform classical descriptors for unseen-driver re-identification. | With 5-minute enrollment, supervised-contrastive Transformer AUROC is `.935 ± .005`, versus `.707` for classical descriptors; headline learned-model results average three training seeds on a fixed split. | Table 3; Sections 4.4 and 6.2 |
| Driver information persists after context matching. | On 14,868 pairs matched by vehicle model, scenario, speed range, and applicable headway range, the learned embedding achieves `.811 ± .006` AUROC; descriptors fall to `.550`. Performance spans `.78–.83` across six scenarios. | Table 4; Sections 4.1 and 6.3 |
| Video re-identification is strongly affected by contextual leakage. | Video-only AUROC drops from `.937` unmatched to `.675` condition-matched. Its representation predicts route at `347×` chance and vehicle at `64×` chance, versus `197×` and `56×` for CAN. | Table 4; Section 6.5 |
| Cross-vehicle identity transfer remains above chance but is less precise. | For 7 eligible drivers and 110 vehicle pairs, cross-vehicle verification gives AUROC `.750`, bootstrap 95% CI `[.64, .86]`, versus `.816` `[.74, .90]` within one vehicle. | Appendix G |
| Personalization mainly changes predictive distributions, not point estimates. | CVAE and MDN likelihood gains are `+6.9 ± 4.3` and `+0.10 ± 0.05` nats, respectively, while RMSE remains flat. At 5 seconds, the reported few-shot RMSE gain is `+0.8 ± 0.5%` for `k=5`. | Section 6.4 |
| Distributional fit improves modestly with personalization. | CVAE predictions move from `.430` to `.422` MMD and `.349` to `.339` Wasserstein-1; histogram KL has no consistent improvement. | Appendix H |
| Re-identification quality does not guarantee predictive utility. | Conditioning on the best re-identification embedding produces `−0.2%` prediction gain; the few-shot style vector gives `+0.4%` to `+1.4%` through FiLM but `−0.7%` through query injection. | Section 6.4; Appendix M |
| Temporal video features help more than per-frame appearance features. | Temporal features improve event-forecasting AUROC from `.842` to `.847` and contribute `+1.49 ± 0.32%` prediction gain; per-frame features reduce forecasting AUROC, including a 7.3-point reduction for DINOv3. | Section 6.5; Table 13 |
| Event annotations are mostly high precision, except automatic lane-change candidates. | Five sampled classes achieve `94.8%–99.6%` audited precision. Lane-change candidates achieve `61.2%`; 22,322 individually verified events are released. | Section 3.4; Table 7 / Appendix D |

These results support the authors’ claim that learned representations contain driver-associated information beyond coarse vehicle-model and matched-condition effects, and that personalization can improve distributional prediction for some drivers. They do **not** establish that the embeddings measure an intrinsic or causal notion of driving style: physical vehicle instances, routes, recording devices, and driver identity remain incompletely separable. Small average personalization gains and heterogeneous per-driver effects also limit claims of broad practical benefit.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Behavioral primitives are percentile-based weak labels, not ground-truth style or personality measurements; the event taxonomy is likewise rule-derived (Section 8.2).
- **Author-stated:** The sample is regionally skewed and restricted to volunteers using an aftermarket driving system, so it is not representative of all drivers (Section 8.2).
- **Author-stated:** Most drivers appear with only one vehicle, leaving driver and vehicle effects naturally entangled. Condition matching fixes vehicle model, not physical vehicle instance (Section 8.2; Appendix G).
- **Author-stated:** Pedal signals have incomplete coverage—gas in 40% and brake in 57% of drives—so the benchmark primarily models realized motion rather than actuation (Sections 3.2 and 8.2).
- **Author-stated:** Likelihood results are sensitive to evaluation sampling; freezing manifests changed an MDN gain from an apparent `+1.16` to `+0.068` nats for the same model (Appendix I).
- **Author-stated:** The cross-vehicle analysis is limited to 7 vehicle-controlled drivers, producing wide confidence intervals (Appendix G).
- **Analyst inference:** Condition matching reduces known confounds but cannot demonstrate their complete removal because it uses coarse bins and model-level rather than instance-level vehicle control.
- **Analyst inference:** Some baseline comparisons are less robust than headline results: foundation-model rows are single-pass evaluations, and the LoRA result is a single run and seed (Appendix L).
- **Analyst inference:** Personalization’s practical significance remains uncertain because average point-prediction gains are small and likelihood gains are positive for only 55% of evaluated unseen drivers (Section 6.4; Appendix H).

## Open research questions

1. **Can driver style be isolated independently of the physical vehicle?** Most participants use one car, so vehicle-instance effects remain unresolved. A next experiment could collect the same drivers across several shared vehicles and compare cross-over versus ordinary splits.

2. **Which drivers benefit reliably from personalization?** Likelihood gains are positive for 55% of unseen drivers and have a heavy-tailed distribution. A follow-up could predict benefit from support-set size, behavioral diversity, and representation uncertainty, then validate on held-out drivers.

3. **Can multimodal models use video without learning route identity?** Temporal video helps prediction, but video-only identity recognition exhibits strong route leakage. A plausible experiment is route-adversarial or location-balanced temporal-video training evaluated jointly on prediction, matched-context utility, and leakage.

4. **How well do findings transfer beyond this volunteer fleet?** The authors identify regional and selection biases. A next experiment could reproduce the frozen tasks and metrics on a prospectively sampled fleet with broader geography, demographics, and vehicle access.
