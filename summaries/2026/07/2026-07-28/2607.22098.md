# Reasoning Denoiser: Denoising Reasoning Traces for Hallucination Detection in Large Reasoning Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.22098) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22098)


## One-sentence takeaway

ReDe uses final-answer attention as annotation-free supervision to learn a step-embedding space that filters irrelevant and repetitive reasoning, consistently improving hallucination-detection AUROC across four tasks, two 8B reasoning models, and four detector families.

## Problem and motivation

Long reasoning traces can contain irrelevant steps and repetitions that dilute signals about whether the final answer is correct. The authors argue that existing confidence scores cannot reliably distinguish useful from noisy steps, while similarity filtering on raw embeddings struggles with repetitions because they remain semantically close to informative steps (Section 1, Figure 1). The resulting gap is a method specifically designed to denoise reasoning traces for hallucination detection rather than generation quality or efficiency.

## Approach and contributions

- **Noise characterization:** The paper distinguishes irrelevant steps from repetitive steps and analyzes how both impair hallucination detection (Sections 1 and 3).
- **Annotation-free supervision:** ReDe ranks training-time steps using attention from the final answer’s last token to each step (Section 4.1, Equation 1).
- **Representation shaping:** Perplexity-weighted step embeddings are passed through a lightweight two-layer projection while the underlying reasoning model remains frozen. A three-term objective compacts informative proxies, disperses noisy proxies, and separates the groups (Section 4.2, Equations 2–3; Appendix A.4).
- **Detector-agnostic filtering:** At inference, ReDe removes the steps with the largest within-trace \(k\)-nearest-neighbor distances in the projected space, without recomputing attention, and supplies the filtered trace to an existing detector (Section 4.3).
- **Empirical and theoretical analysis:** Experiments cover multiple tasks, model families, detector types, transfer settings, and ablations; Appendix E gives conditional bounds linking filtering errors to linear-probe risk under explicit assumptions.

These results establish broad empirical improvement within the tested benchmarks, models, splits, labeling pipeline, and detector implementations. They do not establish that attention is a causal explanation of reasoning, that filtering guarantees truthfulness, or that gains generalize to arbitrary models, domains, trace formats, or high-stakes deployments. Most comparisons use correctness labels produced by an external judge, although Appendix C.5 reports robustness to several alternative labeling methods.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated limitation:** ReDe only filters completed traces and does not suppress noisy reasoning during generation (Appendix D).
- **Author-stated limitation:** It is not a guarantee of truthfulness and may either trust hallucinations or suppress correct but unconventional reasoning; the authors retain human oversight for high-stakes use (Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference — access requirements:** The main setup requires white-box access to attention, hidden states, and token probabilities. The proxy-model experiment improves black-box results, but assumes a suitable accessible proxy and does not fully recover white-box performance (Appendix C.8, Table 19).
- **Analyst inference — label validity:** Main correctness labels come from Qwen3-32B rather than uniform human adjudication. Alternative-label experiments reduce, but do not eliminate, dependence on automated judgments (Section 5; Appendix C.5).
- **Analyst inference — annotation scope:** Step-label accuracy is validated only on Qwen3-8B traces from AIME 2024. The number of examples in the independently verified subset is omitted, limiting interpretation of the reported \(\kappa\) (Appendix A.3 and C.4).
- **Analyst inference — tuned removal rate:** Performance peaks at a 70% drop ratio and declines with stronger filtering; therefore, a fixed removal fraction can delete informative steps when the true noise prevalence differs (Section 5.2, Figure 6(b); Appendix E.4).
- **Analyst inference — theoretical scope:** The guarantees depend on assumptions including approximately label-uninformative noise, independent local-neighbor indicators, strong density separation, bounded step representations, additive trace summaries, and a linear probe. The paper does not establish these assumptions generally for real reasoning traces (Appendix E).
- **Analyst inference — statistical reporting:** Results are averaged over three seeds, but uncertainty is absent from several central tables and no significance tests are reported in the supplied text (Section 5; Tables 2–4).

## Open research questions

1. **Can denoising be integrated into generation without reducing answer quality?** ReDe only filters completed traces. A next experiment could train an LRM with the step-informativeness objective and compare generation accuracy, trace length, calibration, and detection AUROC against post-hoc ReDe.

2. **How well does the learned notion of noise transfer across model architectures and trace styles?** Cross-dataset and proxy-model results are promising, but human-validated selection accuracy is limited to Qwen3-8B on AIME 2024. A next experiment could annotate stratified traces from all evaluated models and domains, then report per-category precision and recall for irrelevant and repetitive steps.

3. **Can filtering adapt to each trace’s actual noise rate?** The fixed 70% drop ratio creates count-mismatch risk and performance falls when filtering becomes excessive. A next experiment could compare validation-tuned fixed ratios with calibrated, trace-specific stopping rules based on the \(k\)-NN score distribution.

4. **Does ReDe remain reliable when attention is unstable or unavailable?** Direct attention filtering underperforms projection learning, while black-box use requires a proxy. A next experiment could train alternative projections from hidden-state attribution or intervention-based signals and evaluate transfer under deliberately mismatched proxy and target models.
