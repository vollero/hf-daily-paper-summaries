# Rethinking Classifier-Free Guidance in On-Policy Diffusion Distillation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.24731) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24731)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors show that matching only classifier-free-guided predictions in on-policy diffusion distillation can hide compensating branch errors under asymmetric conditioning, and propose Positive–Direction Matching to improve control transfer and robustness to inference-time guidance changes.

## Problem and motivation

On-policy distillation (OPD) commonly matches the teacher’s and student’s classifier-free guidance (CFG) compositions while retaining separate positive and negative branches. This loss constrains only a weighted combination of the two branch errors, so infinitely many nonzero error pairs can satisfy it (Eqs. 5–7).

The authors argue that this ambiguity becomes harmful when the teacher’s negative branch contains privileged reference or control information unavailable to the student. In that setting, improving the positive branch may worsen the negative branch—termed **Negative Branch Asymmetry (NBA)**—and compensation learned at the training guidance scale may fail when the scale changes at inference (§§1, 3.1).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Failure-mode analysis.** The paper derives the branch-level under-identification of CFG-composed OPD and empirically contrasts shared versus privileged negative conditioning. NBA is diagnosed through decreasing positive-branch error alongside increasing negative-branch error (Eqs. 5–7; Fig. 2).

2. **Positive–Direction Matching (PDM).** PDM separately matches the positive prediction and the conditional direction \(v^+-v^-\). Its zero-loss solution requires both branch errors to vanish, eliminating cross-branch compensation (Eqs. 9–10).

3. **Branch-aware comparison objective.** Independent Branch Matching (IBM) directly supervises both branches, serving as a foil for separating the general value of branch-aware supervision from PDM’s particular parameterization (Eq. 11).

4. **Efficient video application.** For dense-to-sparse video control, the authors supervise only the first \(K\) student-visited states while completing the full rollout, reducing teacher-computation cost (Eq. 12; §3.3).

5. **Evaluation across asymmetries and modalities.** The study covers text rendering, reference-conditioned style transfer, and dense-to-sparse pose, depth, and scribble video control (§§4.1–4.3).

The evidence establishes the algebraic ambiguity of composed matching and provides consistent empirical signatures of NBA in the tested asymmetric settings. It also supports the authors’ claim that branch-aware supervision can improve matched-scale control and off-scale stability within their configurations. It does **not** establish that every privileged-conditioning task develops NBA, that PDM is theoretically superior to IBM, or that the qualitative style findings generalize beyond the small case study.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated limitation:** PDM and IBM have the same branch-level zero-loss solution under non-degenerate weights; PDM’s observed advantage is empirical and lacks a theoretical optimization explanation (§6).

- **Author-stated scope:** The analysis has not been extended to other guidance mechanisms or broader forms of teacher–student conditioning asymmetry (§6).

- **Author-stated evaluation caveat:** Reference-conditioned style transfer uses only 12 held-out prompts and qualitative inspection, so it is not an aggregate performance estimate (Appendix §7.1.2).

- **Analyst inference:** The main numerical bodies of Tables 1–5 are absent from the supplied text. Except for the three OCR values and supervision-time endpoints, effect sizes and metric-by-metric comparisons cannot be independently assessed.

- **Analyst inference:** The central shared-versus-privileged diagnosis is demonstrated through two image case studies and one video application. This supports a plausible mechanism but does not isolate privileged negative conditioning across many architectures or tasks.

- **Analyst inference:** Guidance and hyperparameter studies use pose-only models, whereas the main results use joint multimodal training; the authors explicitly state that these protocols are not directly comparable (§4.2.1).

- **Analyst inference:** Video control metrics depend on re-extracted annotations from DWPose, MiDaS, and a scribble annotator; the supplied text does not report human evaluation or annotator-error sensitivity (Appendix §7.1.5).

- **Analyst inference:** A project URL is present, but the supplied text does not establish whether code, checkpoints, or the derived benchmark are publicly downloadable.

## Open research questions

1. **Why does PDM outperform IBM despite their identical zero-loss branch solution?**  
   The paper identifies this as theoretically unresolved (§6). A next experiment could compare gradient alignment, conditioning, convergence speed, and branch-error trajectories under matched loss scales.

2. **Is privileged negative conditioning necessary or merely one sufficient trigger for NBA?**  
   Only a small number of shared and privileged configurations are tested. A controlled factorial study could independently vary positive-branch and negative-branch information asymmetry while holding the architecture and data fixed.

3. **How broadly does PDM transfer beyond standard CFG?**  
   Other guidance mechanisms are outside the paper’s scope. A next experiment could formulate analogous component-aware objectives for another guidance composition and test robustness under inference-time guidance changes.

4. **Can supervision-horizon selection be made adaptive?**  
   \(K=8\) is best among the evaluated video settings, but the text provides no general rule for choosing it. A next experiment could allocate teacher queries according to timestep-wise error or gradient contribution and compare cost–quality trade-offs with fixed \(K\).
