# Progress Reward Modeling for Robotic Learning: A Comprehensive Survey

> [← Daily summary](../2026-07-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.21655) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21655)


## One-sentence takeaway

The survey unifies robotic progress-reward research through model interfaces, reward-construction mechanisms, supervision pipelines, and evaluation goals, while emphasizing that useful downstream rewards are not necessarily faithful measures of task progress.

## Problem and motivation

Terminal success signals reveal only whether a task eventually finishes, offering weak credit assignment and no direct indication of advancement, stagnation, or regression during long-horizon execution (Abstract; Section 1). Progress estimation could improve policy learning, monitoring, planning, reranking, filtering, and failure recovery, but it is difficult because progress is latent, task-conditioned, history-dependent, and potentially non-monotonic (Section 1).

The authors identify a fragmented literature whose methods differ in observations, goal specifications, outputs, supervision, and evaluation protocols. Consequently, results based on calibration, temporal ordering, preferences, success detection, or policy performance are often not directly comparable (Abstract; Section 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Interface taxonomy:** Organizes models by current-state representation, goal specification, and output form, enabling architecture-independent comparison (Section 2; Figure 2).
- **Construction taxonomy:** Groups methods into frozen foundation-model scoring, temporal or relative learning, instruction-tuned progress prediction, and programmatic reward construction (Section 3; Figure 3).
- **Data and evaluation framework:** Classifies supervision by degree of human involvement and separates progress fidelity, robustness/generalization, and downstream utility evaluations (Section 4; Figure 4).
- **Research agenda:** Identifies coarse temporal resolution, fixed-rate progress assumptions, inference latency, and inadequate long-horizon memory as major unresolved limitations (Section 5).

This is a conceptual survey rather than a newly evaluated progress model.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations of current approaches:**

- Coarse frames, clips, bins, and stages can miss subtle physical changes such as alignment, unstable grasps, or early contact failures (Section 5).
- Timestamp-derived supervision encourages smooth progress even though real execution contains jumps, plateaus, stagnation, and regression (Section 5).
- large vision-language model latency can prevent reward inference at every control step (Section 5).
- Short windows or single observations may not retain completed, pending, or invalidated subgoals in long-horizon tasks (Section 5).
- Human labels are costly and subjective; automated labels inherit temporal assumptions, simulator abstractions, annotator-model errors, or implausible synthetic artifacts (Section 4.1).

**Analyst inferences supported by the supplied text:**

- The survey’s taxonomy is not quantitatively validated against alternative organizational frameworks.
- The text provides no systematic study-counting procedure, inclusion criteria, quality assessment, or numerical meta-analysis, so the completeness and relative prevalence of categories cannot be independently assessed.
- Comparisons remain qualitative: standardized head-to-head results across methods, datasets, and settings are not reported.
- Reproducibility of the literature synthesis is unclear because search and screening procedures are not described in the supplied text.

## Open research questions

1. **How can progress labels represent event-dependent jumps, plateaus, and regression?** Timestamp supervision assumes a smoother relationship than real task execution supports (Section 5). A next experiment could compare timestamp labels with event-anchored human labels on trajectories containing retries, reversals, and variable-speed execution.

2. **What compact memory is sufficient for long-horizon progress estimation?** Short windows may omit completed or invalidated subgoals (Section 5). A plausible experiment would compare memoryless, fixed-window, recurrent, and explicit subgoal-state models on repeated-action tasks.

3. **Can fine physical progress be inferred reliably from multimodal sensing?** Visual inputs may miss subtle contact, force, or grasp changes (Section 5). A next experiment could evaluate vision-only models against vision-plus-proprioception, force, and tactile variants using fine-grained event annotations.

4. **How should fidelity and control utility be jointly evaluated?** The survey notes that an effective policy reward may be poorly calibrated, while an accurate estimator may be unusable for control (Sections 4.2 and 4.4). A suitable experiment would measure calibration, ordering, latency, sample efficiency, and policy success for the same models under one standardized protocol.
