# Mitigating Gender Bias in English to Romanian Machine Translation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.08606) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08606)


## One-sentence takeaway

A hybrid system that predicts contextual gender with a fine-tuned LLaMA classifier and guides English-to-Romanian translation through inline tags improves benchmark gender accuracy by more than 40 percentage points over untagged MT, according to the authors.

## Problem and motivation

English often leaves gender implicit, whereas Romanian requires explicit gender and morphological agreement. Standard MT systems can therefore default to masculine forms or reproduce occupational stereotypes, yielding biased or grammatically inconsistent translations. The authors identify English–Romanian as an understudied, relatively low-resource setting; prior mitigation research largely targets higher-resource languages (Sections 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Hybrid pipeline:** A predefined and fuzzy-matched entity selector identifies occupations and role nouns. A LoRA-tuned, 4-bit LLaMA 3.2 1B classifier assigns feminine, masculine, or ambiguous gender from context; feminine and masculine predictions become inline tags consumed by a fine-tuned Helsinki-NLP English–Romanian Transformer (Section 5; Figure 2).
- **EnGen datasets:** Dataset 1 provides 11,472 balanced single-entity examples; Dataset 2 provides 996 more difficult multi-entity examples with distractors. They support two-stage curriculum training for contextual gender classification (Section 3; Figure 1).
- **EnRoGend dataset:** A pair-locked corpus of 1,974 tagged English–Romanian examples, balanced across 987 masculine and 987 feminine instances and covering 82 occupations or roles (Section 4).
- **Comparative adaptation study:** Full, partial, and LoRA MT fine-tuning are compared; full fine-tuning performs best in the reported experiment, while some partial configurations are competitive (Section 5.2; Table 5).
- **External diagnostic evaluation:** The pipeline is evaluated manually on Romanian translations of WinoMT and WinoGender, against raw MT and zero-shot GPT-5.2 (Section 6; Table 6).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Experiments cover only English-to-Romanian, although the authors suggest possible transfer to other morphologically gendered languages (Section 7).
- **Lexical coverage:** Entity selection depends on a predefined list with fuzzy matching, and EnRoGend covers only 82 occupations or person-related roles. Unlisted entities and broader constructions are not evaluated (Sections 4–5).
- **Binary translation control:** EnGen recognizes an ambiguous class that includes neutral or non-binary references, but EnRoGend contains only feminine and masculine tags. The paper does not establish controlled Romanian handling of ambiguous or non-binary inputs (Sections 3.1 and 4).
- **Manual benchmark evaluation:** WinoMT and WinoGender lack Romanian references here; one native speaker performs evaluation, followed by unspecified spot checks. Inter-annotator agreement and detailed judging procedures are not reported (Section 6).
- **Lenient correctness criterion:** Nonstandard forms such as “vânzătora” are accepted when gender and agreement are judged correct, so benchmark accuracy does not imply full lexical or orthographic correctness (Section 6).
- **Reproducibility:** GPT-5.2 is proprietary and evaluated with “default system settings,” which the authors acknowledge may prevent exact replication (Section 6).
- **Analyst inference:** The >40-point claim cannot be audited from the retrieved text because the numerical contents of Table 6 are missing.
- **Analyst inference:** LoRA’s reported MT underperformance applies only to the tested configurations; no parameter counts, complete scores, or capacity-matched comparison are supplied here, limiting the broader conclusion that adapters are insufficient.
- **Analyst inference:** GPT-assisted dataset creation followed by native-speaker filtering may introduce stylistic or template artifacts; the paper reports filtering but no diversity or artifact analysis (Section 3).

## Open research questions

1. **Can the pipeline represent ambiguous, neutral, or non-binary Romanian outputs?** EnGen predicts ambiguity, but EnRoGend trains only masculine/feminine control. A next experiment could add neutral or ambiguity-preserving targets and evaluate them with a dedicated Romanian test set.

2. **How well does the method generalize beyond the 82 listed occupations and roles?** Candidate selection is lexicon-based and training coverage is narrow. A next experiment could hold out entire entity types and test unseen occupations, inflections, names, and multiword roles.

3. **Which stage contributes most to the benchmark gain?** No oracle-tag, predicted-tag, or selector-recall ablation is reported in the supplied text. A next experiment could compare gold tags, predicted tags, tags with classifier errors, and raw MT to separate classification from translation effects.

4. **Does improved gender control preserve general translation quality?** Reported MT quality is measured on the controlled EnRoGend split, while benchmark evaluation focuses on gender correctness. A next experiment could evaluate the adapted model on a broad, independently translated English–Romanian corpus alongside gender diagnostics.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text describes the datasets, pipeline, splits, and several headline metrics clearly, but omits the numerical cells of Tables 4–6. Material missing information includes exact WinoMT/WinoGender accuracies by system and subset, complete hyperparameter results, LoRA and partial-tuning scores and parameter counts, entity-selector precision/recall, annotator agreement, benchmark spot-check protocol, general-domain MT evaluation, and error analyses for classifier-to-translator propagation.
