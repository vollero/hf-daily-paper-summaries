# UniSwap: Streaming Audio-Visual Identity Swapping for Talking Videos

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.11752) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11752)


## One-sentence takeaway

UniSwap jointly transfers appearance and vocal timbre in talking videos using a block-causal audio-video diffusion transformer, achieving stronger synchronization than evaluated cascades and stable one-minute identity preservation, though not real-time playback or best-in-class single-modality quality.

## Problem and motivation

The paper addresses joint identity replacement: changing both the visible person and their voice while preserving source motion, background, speech content, and audio-video timing. Existing visual-replacement and voice-conversion models handle separate modalities; cascading them provides no joint objective for coordinating converted speech with lip motion. Most diffusion-based replacement systems also require complete clips, limiting low-latency interaction (Introduction).

The central challenges are scarce cross-identity pairs aligned in performance and timing, and converting a slow bidirectional diffusion backbone into an efficient streaming generator.

## Approach and contributions

- **Unified replacement:** One audio-video diffusion transformer conditions on a source video/audio pair, reference portrait, and reference voice clip to generate both modalities jointly (Secs. 1 and 3).
- **Swap-and-reconstruct supervision:** Real clips become reconstruction targets; pose proxies suppress visual identity, off-the-shelf voice conversion alters timbre, and the original portrait and a 30% audio crop provide identity references (Sec. 3.1; Fig. 3).
- **Three-stage adaptation:** In-context pretraining learns joint replacement; conditional streaming adaptation introduces block-causal masking and KV caching; self-forcing DMD reduces inference from 30 to 3 denoising steps per block (Secs. 3.2–3.4; Fig. 2).
- **Memory-efficient distillation:** Teacher, generator, and critic use separate LoRA adapters on one frozen backbone, reducing reported peak memory from over 80 GB to 65.34 GB (Sec. 3.4.1).
- **Long-form position handling:** Feature-RoPE Decomposition combines bounded local coordinates, a persistent initial sink block, and reference re-anchoring to limit drift under rolling-cache inference (Sec. 3.4.2).

The evidence supports improved synchronization, competitive identity transfer, efficient blockwise generation, and stability over the evaluated one-minute horizon. It does not establish real-time operation, superiority in general visual or voice quality, robustness beyond the tested data and durations, or causal superiority over an equally sized jointly trained non-streaming alternative.

## Limitations and validity risks

The authors explicitly limit the system to single-speaker videos and report difficulty with multi-speaker scenes, occlusions, and complex interactions. Expression follows the audio automatically and cannot be independently or arbitrarily controlled (Sec. 10). They also acknowledge that 13.6 FPS is below the 25-fps playback rate (Sec. 4.3).

Analyst inferences:

- Training and short evaluation both draw on AVSpeech, although speakers are disjoint; broader cross-domain generalization is therefore uncertain (Sec. 4.1).
- The long benchmark contains only 20 web-crawled one-minute videos, so stability beyond one minute is untested (Sec. 4.1).
- Seed-VC both synthesizes training sources and supplies every visual baseline’s audio, potentially making comparisons sensitive to that converter choice (Sec. 4.1).
- Baseline and UniSwap timing units differ—whole clips versus blocks—so per-step efficiency is not directly comparable, as the authors note (Table 3).
- The supplied user-study text gives participant count and ranking but omits Table 6’s numerical ratings, preventing assessment of effect sizes or variability (Sec. 8).
- The text identifies a project page but does not establish public availability of code, weights, synthesized training tuples, or evaluation benchmarks.

## Open research questions

1. **Can joint replacement operate at or above playback speed without quality loss?** UniSwap reaches 13.6 FPS against 25-fps playback. A next experiment could profile cache, decoder, and denoising costs, then compare optimized three-, two-, and one-step variants at matched quality.

2. **How robust is the method in multi-person and occluded scenes?** These are explicit limitations. A stratified benchmark could vary speaker count, overlap, occlusion severity, and turn-taking while measuring identity assignment and synchronization per person.

3. **Does long-form stability persist beyond one minute?** Evaluation stops at one minute. Tests at 5, 10, and 30 minutes could track segment-wise DINO-S, synchronization, voice similarity, and accumulated artifacts.

4. **Can expression and identity be controlled independently?** Expressions currently follow audio rather than explicit controls. A next experiment could add expression conditioning and test whether expression edits preserve lip synchronization, identity, and linguistic content.
