# From Inaudible Inputs to Model Failures: Low-Frequency Safety Risks in LALMs

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.09158) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09158)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors show in receiver-side simulations that a fixed 5–20 Hz waveform can substantially disrupt six audio-language models with low measured audibility, while a spectral-shift detector plus clean rerecording partially restores performance.

## Problem and motivation

Large audio-language models (LALMs) can process frequencies users may not perceive, creating a mismatch between model-visible and human-audible evidence. The authors argue that prior work studies adversarial audio, semantic attacks, and general acoustic corruption, but has not adequately examined availability and reliability failures caused by fixed, utterance-independent low-frequency signals (Introduction; Related Work, pp. 1–2).

This matters because an unnoticed signal could corrupt speech recognition, translation, audio question answering, or emotion classification without alerting the user. The paper evaluates this hidden input channel and proposes a preliminary defense.

## Approach and contributions

- **Intermittent Low-Frequency Lockout (ILL):** A black-box red-teaming method that constructs one universal waveform offline from a reference speech corpus and reference LALM. Sentence Attention Scale Estimation determines active intervals from multiscale attention boundaries; Frequency Confusion Transfer maps corpus spectral-centroid transitions into a continuous-phase sequence of 5–20 Hz states. The resulting template is fixed across test recordings and target models (Method, pp. 2–4).

- **Distributional Requery Guard (DRG):** A plug-in defense that uses STFT-derived normalized spectral descriptors and two-cluster K-means to flag low-frequency distribution shifts. Flagged inputs trigger a second recording, and the LALM answers using content consistent across both recordings (Distributional Requery Guard, p. 4).

- **Cross-model, multi-task evaluation:** ILL and DRG are evaluated on six open- and closed-source LALMs across audio QA, ASR, speech translation, and emotion classification, with objective and human audibility measurements (Experimental Setup, pp. 4–6).

- **Diagnostic analysis:** Attention, representation similarity, and output-confidence measurements associate the attack with weaker use of acoustic evidence, but the authors explicitly do not claim a complete causal mechanism (Analysis and Limitations, pp. 6–7).

The results establish substantial task degradation under digitally composed, receiver-side perturbations across the tested models and datasets. Human ratings and ANR support low perceptibility for the tested playback conditions, but do not prove universal inaudibility. The evidence also does not establish field effectiveness: the study neither emitted ILL through a calibrated physical attack chain nor tested device- and environment-specific propagation. Internal measurements are correlational rather than causal.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations**

- The attack was not deployed in uncontrolled environments; experiments simulated the waveform received by the microphone rather than reproducing the complete loudspeaker–environment–microphone chain (Limitations, p. 7).
- Physical effectiveness depends on source capability, propagation loss, microphone response, analog filtering, automatic gain control, and the received signal’s noise floor (Appendix A, p. 10).
- The attention, representation, and confidence analyses do not establish a complete causal mechanism (Limitations, p. 7).
- DRG recovery depends on the quality of the second recording; persistent interference produced smaller gains than clean reacquisition (Defense Evaluation, pp. 5–6).

**Analyst inferences**

- Generalization is uncertain because evaluation uses 100 examples per dataset or subset, one reference model for constructing ILL, and six target models (Experimental Setup, p. 4; Appendix C, p. 10).
- The audibility study used student volunteers, consumer headphones or loudspeakers, fixed within-session volume, and digitally mixed stimuli; it does not cover broader populations, calibrated sound-pressure levels, or diverse real-world devices (Appendix F, p. 11).
- Forced requery was not uniformly benign: although 10 of 12 scores remained within 0.03 of standard inference, Qwen3 MMAU accuracy fell from 75% to 62% (Table 4, p. 6).
- Table 3 appears internally ambiguous: some CoVoST2-trained precision and recall entries are identical while the listed F1 differs, so those detector results require clarification.
- Code, waveform templates, participant allocation details, and release status are not given in the supplied text, limiting reproducibility assessment.

## Open research questions

1. **Does ILL remain effective through a real acoustic chain?**  
   The paper evaluates receiver-side waveforms, leaving device and room effects unresolved. A next experiment could use calibrated low-frequency sources and multiple microphones, distances, rooms, and frontend filtering configurations.

2. **Can defenses recover without requiring the user to repeat an utterance?**  
   DRG relies on independent reacquisition, and recovery weakens when the second recording is also noisy. A useful experiment would compare requery against single-recording spectral filtering, representation-level correction, and multi-microphone capture under persistent interference.

3. **Which model components causally produce the failure?**  
   Attention and confidence changes are correlational. Controlled interventions could filter selected encoder bands or patch clean versus attacked intermediate representations to test whether specific audio-encoder stages mediate the errors.

4. **How well do attack and detector generalization extend beyond the tested distributions?**  
   ILL uses one reference LALM and a small construction corpus, while DRG uses simulated clean/jammed training examples. Cross-device, cross-language, unseen-frequency-pattern, and naturally occurring infrasound tests could measure false positives and transfer robustness.
