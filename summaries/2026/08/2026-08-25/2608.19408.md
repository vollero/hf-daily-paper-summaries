# Beyond Imitation: Filtering On-Policy Distillation by Reasoning Progress

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.19408) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19408)


## One-sentence takeaway

R²-OPD improves mathematical-reasoning distillation by masking teacher-derived token supervision when its within-response ranking conflicts with independently estimated reasoning progress.

## Problem and motivation

Standard on-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student on its own trajectories using dense teacher–student distribution matching, but implicitly treats similarity to the teacher as evidence of reasoning quality. The authors argue that this proxy can penalize productive alternative reasoning and reward teacher-like spans that make little progress.

Outcome-level correctness cannot assign credit to intermediate steps, while directly mixing noisy process rewards with token-level KL losses is difficult because the signals have different scales and granularity. The identified gap is therefore a mechanism that uses reasoning progress to assess the reliability of teacher supervision without replacing OPD’s objective (Introduction; Process Reward Estimation).

## Approach and contributions

- **Progress estimation:** The response is divided using a fixed lexicon of reflective discourse markers. At each eligible boundary, eight student rollouts estimate the probability of reaching the verified answer; consecutive probability differences define segment-level process rewards (Eqs. 4–6; Appendix B.5).

- **Noise-reduced comparison:** Adjacent segments with same-sign nonzero progress are merged. Their process rewards telescope to endpoint differences, while token-level reverse-KL losses are averaged within each merged segment (Eqs. 7–10; Lemma 1; Proposition 2).

- **Conflict filtering:** Within each response, segments are ranked by progress. A conflict occurs when a higher-progress segment also receives a higher distillation loss. Segments with the largest positive inconsistency scores are masked from the normalized OPD loss rather than assigned a new reward (Eqs. 11–13; Algorithm 1).

- **Theoretical and empirical contributions:** The paper proves cancellation of internal boundary-estimation errors under merging and an \(O(1/L)\) variance bound for segment averaging under decaying covariance assumptions, then evaluates the method on two student–teacher pairs and three mathematical-reasoning benchmarks.

The results support the authors’ claim that selective filtering improves aggregate reasoning accuracy in the tested small-model mathematical settings and that merging is important for AIME performance. They do not establish that the method scales to larger models, transfers beyond verifiable mathematics, or that the detected conflicts correspond to objectively incorrect teacher guidance. The qualitative cases in Appendix C illustrate successful trajectories but are explicitly not causal evidence.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Process-reward estimation has nontrivial cost and variance because it uses multiple continuations at each boundary; reducing both is left to future work (Conclusion).

- **Author-stated:** Generalization to larger models and more diverse domains remains untested (Conclusion).

- **Author-stated:** Progress estimates can be affected by finite-rollout noise, verifier errors, and distribution shift (Related Works).

- **Analyst inference:** Filtering applies only when segmentation succeeds and the ground-truth answer appears in the response. Other trajectories pass through unchanged, so the effective intervention may cover a selective subset; its coverage rate is not reported (Process Reward Estimation; Algorithm 1).

- **Analyst inference:** Segmentation depends on a fixed discourse-marker lexicon and counts periods as a sentence approximation. Robustness to other writing styles, languages, or alternative segmenters is not evaluated (Appendix B.5).

- **Analyst inference:** The masking ratio is tuned through an ablation on the same three benchmark families, and aggressive masking sharply hurts AIME. This suggests material hyperparameter sensitivity (Figure 4).

- **Analyst inference:** Only two closely sized student–teacher configurations, one training corpus, one epoch, and mathematical benchmarks are tested; variance across random seeds and statistical uncertainty are not reported in the supplied text.

- **Analyst inference:** The paper ablates masking ratio and merging, but the supplied text reports no isolated ablations of ranking versus raw-score comparison, the discourse segmentation rule, rollout count, eligibility checks, or exclusion of the first segment.

## Open research questions

1. **Can progress estimates be made cheaper without losing filtering quality?** Eight rollouts are generated for every eligible nonterminal boundary. A next experiment could compare fewer rollouts, adaptive sampling, and a learned value estimator at matched compute.

2. **How robust is R²-OPD to segmentation choices?** Only fixed discourse markers with a minimum sentence interval are evaluated. A controlled comparison could test sentence-, parser-, model-, and token-window-based segmentation while measuring conflict stability and downstream accuracy.

3. **Does the method work beyond verifiable mathematics?** Current progress estimation relies on ground-truth answers and task-specific verifiers. A next experiment could evaluate domains with reliable non-mathematical verification or carefully benchmark learned evaluators against exact verification.

4. **Which trajectories receive filtering, and does eligibility bias the gains?** The paper does not report the proportions rejected by answer matching, segmentation, minimum-segment, or conflict checks. Logging these rates and evaluating stratified performance would reveal where the method actually changes training.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text provides detailed methodology, theory, settings, aggregate results, and selected ablations, but the rendered numerical contents of Tables 1–6 are absent. Consequently, per-benchmark baseline scores, complete transfer results, exact no-merging scores, eligibility rates, computational overhead, seed counts, uncertainty estimates, hardware requirements, and code/model release status are unavailable.
