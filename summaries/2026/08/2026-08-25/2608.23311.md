# Beyond the Stability-Exploration Dilemma: Environmental Regularization for LLM Policy Optimization

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.23311) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23311)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors propose ERPO, which replaces response-level Policy-KL with query-likelihood regularization and reference-based query weighting, reporting better mathematical-reasoning accuracy and greater decoding stability than GRPO without directly constraining response exploration.

## Problem and motivation

LLM policy optimization commonly uses Policy-KL to limit response drift, creating what the authors describe as a stability–exploration trade-off: stronger regularization stabilizes training but restricts response exploration, while removing it eliminates explicit drift control. The authors identify an overlooked input-side instability: although the training dataset is fixed, the likelihood that the evolving model assigns to each query can drift from the pre-RL model’s likelihood, increasing estimator variance and contributing to long-run instability (Sections 1 and 3.2; Figure 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Environment-Regularized Policy Optimization (ERPO):** Replaces Policy-KL with Query-KL, penalizing divergence between the current and pre-RL model-induced query distributions (Equations 4–7).
- **Reference-derived query weighting:** Reweights per-query updates using a clipped, dataset-static proxy based on cached reference-model query log-probabilities (Equations 20–28). The authors explicitly state that this is not an unbiased density-ratio estimator.
- **Structural decoupling:** Proposition 1 shows that the Query-KL gradient contains the query-likelihood gradient but not the response-policy score function, implying no *direct* regularization pressure on response probabilities (Section 4.2; Appendix H).
- **Drop-in implementation:** ERPO wraps GRPO, PPO, or REINFORCE-style estimators by modifying the outer query weighting and adding Query-KL; reference likelihoods are cached, and current likelihoods reuse an existing forward pass (Appendices F–G).
- **Stability-oriented evaluation:** The paper evaluates Pass@1, Pass@k, and Avg@k across decoding temperatures 0.1–1.5 and studies training runs extended to 1,000 steps (Sections 5.1–5.3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| ERPO improves aggregate reasoning performance over GRPO | Across six benchmarks and temperatures 0.1–1.5, the authors report up to 14.9% gain and 6.2% average improvement in Avg@32. Whether these percentages are absolute or relative is not specified in the supplied text. | Section 5.2; Figure 3 |
| Gains extend across reported metrics | ERPO is reported to improve Avg@32 by 6.2%, Pass@32 by 3.64%, and Pass@1 by 5.69% over GRPO across the benchmark evaluation. | Section 5.2; Table 1 |
| Query-KL replacement is the stronger ablated component | On MATH500 with Qwen-7B and eight rollouts, ERPO without query weighting scores 80.90 mean accuracy at temperatures ≤1.0 versus 68.80 for GRPO; corresponding high-temperature means are 38.00 and 12.50. | Table 2; Section 5.4 |
| Query weighting alone also helps | GRPO with only query weighting scores 76.14 at temperatures ≤1.0 and 23.79 at 1.2–1.5, compared with GRPO’s 68.80 and 12.50. | Table 2 |
| Benefits persist at larger model scale | For Qwen-32B with eight rollouts, ERPO scores 84.60 at temperatures ≤1.0 and 82.80 at 1.2–1.5, versus GRPO’s 81.62 and 57.20. | Table 2 |
| More rollouts especially improve high-temperature stability | With Qwen-7B and 16 rollouts, ERPO scores 66.25 at temperatures 1.2–1.5 versus GRPO’s 39.75; the corresponding ≤1.0 means are 77.82 and 75.22. | Table 2; Section 5.4 |
| ERPO reduces the train–evaluation gap | Across checkpoints 40–240, the average gap falls from 6.47% for GRPO to 3.14% for ERPO, described as an approximately 51% reduction. | Appendix C; Tables 4–5 |
| ERPO transfers to other RLVR estimators in limited tests | Applying the concept to DAPO and RLOO yields reported absolute improvements of 10.24% and 2.28%, respectively, at temperatures below 1.0. | Appendix E; Table 6 |
| ERPO delays but does not eliminate long-run collapse | Over training extended to 1,000 steps, GRPO first degrades at high temperatures after 400 steps and later across temperatures; ERPO deteriorates less but can still exhibit entropy spikes and loss of sampling capability. | Section 5.3; Figures 5–6 |

These results support the authors’ claim that ERPO outperforms the tested GRPO configurations on mathematical reasoning, particularly under high-temperature decoding, and that its components affect stability differently. They do not establish broad superiority outside math or Qwen models, nor prove that query drift causes the observed failures. The supplied rendering also omits Table 1’s cell values and Table 3’s numerical contents, preventing independent checking of several summarized claims.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Evaluation is concentrated on mathematical reasoning and Qwen-family models; transfer to dialogue, instruction following, code generation, and multilingual tasks remains untested (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** Query-likelihood estimation quality and cost may vary with model scale and data-selection mechanisms (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** The regularization coefficient was not exhaustively tuned (Section 5.4; Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** ERPO can still collapse during extended training (Section 5.3).
- **Author-stated:** The practical query weight is a clipped monotone proxy, not an unbiased estimate of the ideal importance ratio (Appendix I).
- **Analyst inference:** Assumption A1—preserving the pre-RL query distribution preserves generalization—is empirically motivated but not theoretically established, and could be harmful if the reference distribution is poorly matched to the target task.
- **Analyst inference:** Because the same parameters determine query and response likelihoods, the absence of a response score term proves no *direct* response regularization, but does not by itself show that QKL updates leave response behavior unchanged.
- **Analyst inference:** Evidence is mostly against vanilla GRPO; the supplied text does not show comparisons with other contemporary stabilization or data-reweighting methods.
- **Analyst inference:** Reproducibility is limited by missing numerical contents for Tables 1, 3, 4, 5, and 6, an incompletely specified K3 mini-batch surrogate, and no reported uncertainty or multi-seed analysis in the supplied text.
- **Analyst inference:** The reported 74.6% average Pass@1 for 16 rollouts is not directly reconcilable with the visible aggregate columns of Table 2 because the aggregation basis is not explained.

## Open research questions

1. **Does Query-KL generalize beyond mathematical RLVR?** This remains open because experiments cover math and Qwen models only. A next experiment could compare ERPO and Policy-KL on code generation, multilingual reasoning, dialogue, and instruction following using matched training budgets.

2. **Is the query-weight proxy necessary, or would a calibrated density ratio work better?** The implemented clipped inverse-NLL weight is explicitly biased. A next experiment could compare it with normalized sequence probabilities, learned density-ratio estimators, length-normalized likelihoods, and no weighting.

3. **Does controlling query drift causally prevent collapse?** The paper reports correlated Query-KL and stability trajectories but no intervention that independently varies drift. A next experiment could sweep Query-KL targets while holding response-side KL, reward statistics, and update magnitude fixed.

4. **How robust is ERPO to the choice of reference model?** A1 assumes the pre-RL reference is worth preserving, but this is not tested. A next experiment could use references from different pretraining, SFT, and prior-RL stages and measure both target accuracy and broader capability retention.
