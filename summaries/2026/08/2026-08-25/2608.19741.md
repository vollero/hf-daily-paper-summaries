# One Success Isn&#x27;t Reliability: Thinkingbox, a Sandbox and Benchmark for Agents in Stateful Business Workflows

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.19741) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19741)


## One-sentence takeaway

Thinkingbox shows that current agents can sometimes discover successful trajectories in stateful business workflows but remain unreliable across repeated attempts, with the strongest model reaching 65.36% pass@1 yet only 25.25% all-20-attempt success.

## Problem and motivation

Business workflows such as refunds, booking changes, insurance updates, and IT requests require multi-turn information gathering, policy compliance, coordinated tool use, and correct persistent state changes without collateral effects. The authors argue that response quality, valid tool calls, and clean termination cannot establish end-to-end completion because an agent may update the wrong record, omit a required action, or claim success after a failed call. Existing evaluation abstractions do not fully combine isolated stateful environments, simulated-user interaction, outcome checking, and repeated-trial reliability for non-code professional workflows (§§1, 3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Thinkingbox sandbox:** A reusable orchestration loop connecting an agent, a constrained simulated user, isolated MCP-compatible tools, execution tracing, side-effect extraction, and executable judges (§3; Figure 2).
- **Outcome-based evaluation:** Tasks pass only when all applicable checks over terminal state, side effects, and dialogue succeed. This permits alternative valid trajectories while detecting missing, incorrect, or extra persistent effects (§3, Equations 3–4).
- **Thinkingbox-bench:** A released set of 507 synthetic, manually reviewed workflows spanning five business domains; all tasks have backend-state checks, and 30 additionally assess a required final-response property (§§4.1–4.3; Appendix B.3).
- **Repeated reliability study:** Twelve proprietary and open-weight models are evaluated with 20 attempts per task using pass@1, pass@k, and pass^k, accompanied by evaluator ablations and deterministic trajectory diagnostics (§§5.1–5.4; Appendix C.5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| The strongest reported model, GPT-5.4, achieves 65.36% pass@1. | 507 tasks, 20 independent attempts per task, micro-averaged over tasks and trials. | Abstract; §1; §5.1; Table 4 |
| Occasional success substantially exceeds dependable repetition. | GPT-5.4 reaches 91.12% pass@20 but only 25.25% pass^20; pass@20 means at least one success, whereas pass^20 estimates success in all 20 independent attempts. | §1; Appendix D.1; Table 11; Figures 3–4 |
| Difficulty varies markedly by domain. | Across reported models, mean pass@1 is approximately 52% for retail, 35% for booking, 30% for neobank support, 27% for consulting, and 23% for auto insurance. | §5.3; Table 4 |
| Tool-usage failures dominate the diagnostic taxonomy. | Among failed trials, Tool Usage averages 77.5%, Wrong State Update 12.1%, Incomplete User Resolution 7.9%, and No State-Changing Action 2.5%. Labels are exclusive dominant signatures assigned by deterministic precedence, not unique causes. | §5.4; Table 5; Appendix D.3 |
| Weak completion proxies frequently accept failed executions. | Of 79,853 failed trials within 121,680 valid recorded trials over a common set of 12 models, 80.88% terminated cleanly and invoked a mutating tool; 67.24% also ended without an explicit terminal tool error. | Appendix D.2; Table 12 |
| Longer trajectories do not imply greater success. | GLM-5.1 averages 44.86 messages and 11.99 tool calls but underperforms GPT-5.4, which averages 29.80 messages and 11.05 calls. | Appendix D.3; Table 14 |

These results establish that, within this benchmark and protocol, state-based evaluation reveals failures that response- and action-level proxies miss, and repeated trials expose a large discovery–reliability gap. They do not establish performance on the general distribution of enterprise work, with real users, or under alternative simulators. The failure labels describe observable trace patterns rather than proven causal mechanisms.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Of 507 tasks, 477 ignore final-message content. An agent can therefore receive credit after making the correct backend change while inaccurately describing it to the user (Appendix A; Appendix B.3).
- **Author-stated:** Tasks are synthetic reconstructions derived from a confidential, non-public collection and are not claimed to represent enterprise-work prevalence or distribution (Appendix A; Appendix B.1).
- **Author-stated:** Every retained task has a single golden terminal state, excluding workflows with multiple defensible resolutions (Appendix A; Appendix B.5–B.6).
- **Author-stated:** A fixed GPT-5.4-mini simulator shapes all interactions and judges the 30 response-rubric tasks. It is cooperative, closed-world, never changes goals or invents facts, and permits at most ten follow-ups; same-family interaction effects favoring GPT-5.4 cannot be ruled out (Appendix A; Appendix C.4).
- **Author-stated:** Scores depend on harness conventions, including termination markers and turn, action, token, and timeout limits (Appendix A; Appendix C.4/C.6).
- **Author-stated:** The reported pass^k statistic is a biased plug-in estimator selected because the unbiased estimator becomes zero whenever observed successes are fewer than \(k\) (Appendix C.5).
- **Analyst inference:** Generalization across simulator backbones and communication styles is unresolved because no simulator-sensitivity experiment is reported in the supplied text.
- **Analyst inference:** Comparisons involving o3-pro require caution because Table 4 excludes 636 system/harness-error trials, producing varying denominators; complete per-model confidence intervals and table cells are absent from the supplied rendering.

## Open research questions

1. **How sensitive are rankings to the user simulator?** The paper fixes one GPT-5.4-mini cooperative simulator. A next experiment could rerun the benchmark with several simulator families and controlled variations in cooperativeness, disclosure, and goal stability.

2. **Can training reduce the gap between discovery and reliable repetition?** Thinkingbox supplies terminal rewards and per-check reward vectors, but the supplied text reports evaluation rather than training results. A next experiment could fine-tune agents on failure-targeted rewards and compare pass@1, pass@20, and pass^20 on held-out workflows.

3. **How would fuller communication evaluation affect scores?** Only 30 tasks judge final-response properties. A next experiment could add independently validated deterministic or multi-judge rubrics to a stratified subset of the other 477 tasks and measure score and ranking changes.

4. **Do the findings transfer to real, less cooperative users and multi-actor workflows?** Current tasks use synthetic records and allow only the agent to change world state. A next experiment could evaluate consented human interactions or controlled dual-action simulations where users can withhold information, revise goals, or perform required actions.
