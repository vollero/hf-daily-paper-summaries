# Tomatoes, Potatoes, and Onions: Questioning the Need for Faces in Face Presentation Attack Detection

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.21455) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21455)


## One-sentence takeaway

A CLIP-based detector trained only on print and replay attacks involving vegetables achieved 92.70% mean AUC across four face-PAD datasets, supporting the authors’ claim that transferable PAD cues can arise from recapture processes rather than facial content.

## Problem and motivation

Face presentation attack detection (PAD) is usually treated as a face-specific problem, requiring human-face datasets that introduce consent, privacy, and demographic-bias concerns. The authors question this premise: print and replay attacks produce display, paper, optical, and recapture artifacts that are not inherently facial.

The identified gap is the absence of a controlled test that removes faces entirely from downstream PAD training while preserving conventional acquisition and attack processes. The paper asks whether such face-free training transfers to attacks on faces and whether face-free examples complement ordinary face-PAD data (Sections 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Reframing PAD:** The paper proposes viewing much of face PAD as detection of presentation-instrument and recapture artifacts rather than facial semantics (Sections 1 and 5).

2. **TPO dataset:** The authors introduce and release a face-free dataset of tomatoes, potatoes, and onions. It contains 12,480 presentations from 78 physical objects, covering bona fide observations and balanced print/replay attacks under a factorial device, scale, viewpoint, and object protocol (Section 3; Table 1).

3. **Cross-object evaluation:** A FoundPAD-style CLIP ViT-B/16 model with LoRA is trained on vegetables and tested on four face-PAD datasets; the reverse face-to-vegetable direction is also evaluated. Controls include synthetic-face training, an ImageNet-pretrained ViT, mixed face/TPO training, attack-type and object subsets, and reduced frame counts (Sections 4–5; Tables 2–5).

4. **Representation analysis:** PCA/t-SNE embeddings and radial FFT statistics are used to examine whether transfer reflects cross-domain PAD organization or one universal frequency shortcut (Section 5.4; Figures 2–3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Face-free training transferred strongly to face PAD. | TPO-trained CLIP+LoRA achieved **92.70% mean AUC and 14.15% mean HTER** over M, C, I, and O, with video-level scoring. | Section 5.1; Table 2 |
| The representation prior mattered substantially. | With identical TPO downstream data, the ImageNet-21k ViT reached **67.81% mean AUC**, versus **92.70%** for CLIP+LoRA. The comparison does not control normalization or adaptation strategy. | Section 4; Section 5.1; Table 2 |
| TPO outperformed the synthetic-face control. | SynthASpoof-trained FoundPAD obtained **81.02% mean AUC and 27.56% HTER** on MCIO, versus TPO’s **92.70% and 14.15%**. | Section 5.1; Table 2 |
| Reverse transfer was uneven but generally above the zero-shot result. | Single-source face-trained models obtained **58.89–89.88% AUC** on TPO; CLIP zero-shot obtained **67.72%**. One face source therefore fell below zero-shot despite the paper’s broader wording. | Section 5.1; Table 2 |
| Adding TPO improved aggregate single-source transfer under a fixed budget. | Across 12 face-to-face protocols, mean AUC rose from **89.33% to 92.55%** and mean HTER fell from **17.54% to 13.97%** when some sampled face exposure was replaced by TPO. | Section 5.2; Table 3 |
| TPO also improved aggregate multi-source transfer. | Across six double-/triple-source settings, mean AUC rose from **92.11% to 96.96%** and HTER fell from **14.72% to 7.84%**. | Section 5.2; Table 4 |
| Attack-type diversity was more useful than either attack alone. | Both-PAI training produced **92.70% mean AUC**, compared with **86.14%** for print-only and **83.97%** for replay-only training. | Section 5.3; Table 5 |
| Reducing redundant frames caused little aggregate degradation. | Using 10% of frames yielded **92.97% mean AUC and 13.56% HTER**, compared with **92.70% and 14.15%** using all frames. | Section 5.3; Table 5 |
| A single global spectral pattern did not explain transfer. | Attack-minus-bona-fide FFT residuals differed in direction across six datasets: TPO gained high-frequency energy, several face datasets lost it, and M was near zero. | Section 5.4; Figure 3 |

These results establish strong cross-object discrimination within the tested RGB print/replay protocols and show that TPO can improve average cross-dataset performance under fixed optimization exposure. They do not establish that faces are unnecessary for all PAD attacks, that the method is operationally calibrated, or that face-free training guarantees demographic fairness. The observational embedding plots also do not identify the exact physical cues responsible for transfer.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Results cover only print and replay attacks in the visible spectrum. Transfer to 3D masks, depth, or infrared is unknown (Section 8).

- **Author-stated pretraining caveat:** Downstream adaptation is face-free, but CLIP was pretrained on Internet imagery that may include faces. The result supports reduced use of sensitive downstream data, not a fully face-free system (Section 8; Ethics and Privacy Statement).

- **Author-stated deployment caveat:** The experiments are not intended to demonstrate operational readiness (Ethics and Privacy Statement). HTER uses a target-set EER threshold, so target calibration is assumed (Section 4; Section 6).

- **Analyst inference — uncertainty not measured:** The paper reports seed 777 but no multi-seed variation or confidence intervals for the principal AUC/HTER comparisons. Robustness to training randomness is therefore not established (Section 4; Tables 2–5).

- **Analyst inference — imperfect causal ablation:** The CLIP/ImageNet comparison changes normalization and parameter-efficient versus full fine-tuning. The authors explicitly caution that it is not a single-variable ablation (Section 4).

- **Analyst inference — aggregate claims hide exceptions:** Face+TPO improves the averages, but not every individual AUC protocol improves—for example, I→O and O→I decline in Table 3, and O&C&I→M declines in Table 4. “Consistently” is therefore stronger than the per-protocol evidence supports.

- **Analyst inference — narrow instrument coverage:** TPO uses one office printer and two acquisition/display devices in controlled indoor conditions. The evidence does not establish robustness across broad printer, screen, camera, illumination, compression, or adversarial variation (Section 3).

## Open research questions

1. **Does face-free transfer extend to geometry-dependent attacks?**  
   This remains open because only 2D print and replay attacks were studied. A next experiment could construct face-free 3D presentation attacks and evaluate transfer to face-mask PAD.

2. **What minimum pretraining prior enables face-free PAD?**  
   CLIP greatly outperformed the ImageNet ViT, but the comparison confounds several training choices and CLIP may contain prior face exposure. A controlled experiment could compare encoders pretrained on audited data while holding normalization and adaptation strategy fixed.

3. **Which recapture factors actually drive transfer?**  
   FFT analysis rules out one simple universal spectral residual but does not isolate causal cues. A factorial intervention could vary printer halftoning, display pixels, reflections, compression, camera noise, and object reflectance independently.

4. **Does face-free training improve fairness or only remove demographic labels from training data?**  
   The fairness implication is explicitly theoretical in this paper. A next experiment could compare demographic error disparities of face-only, TPO-only, and mixed models on labeled face-PAD test sets.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported experimental setup and tabulated results because the supplied text includes the complete methods, principal tables, figures, and stated limitations.

Material missing information includes multi-seed uncertainty, statistical significance tests for performance differences, detailed per-sample demographic evaluation, broader printer/display/camera coverage, tests on 3D or non-RGB attacks, operational calibration without target labels, and an audited account of the visual content used in CLIP pretraining.
