# Task-CoEvolve: Efficient Harness Optimization via Adaptive Validation Task Selection

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.20169) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20169)


## One-sentence takeaway

Task-CoEvolve adaptively evaluates evolving LLM harnesses on historically discriminative validation tasks and estimates full-set scores from those partial evaluations, approaching full-search performance while substantially reducing evaluation cost.

## Problem and motivation

Automated harness optimization repeatedly modifies the code surrounding a fixed LLM and uses validation performance to choose among candidates. Existing methods typically evaluate every candidate on the entire fixed validation set, which is expensive—especially for long-running terminal tasks—and wastes effort on tasks that all candidates consistently solve or fail.

The authors identify a gap between work that makes candidate generation more efficient and the largely unstudied problem of reducing the number of tasks evaluated per candidate. Simple subsets are inadequate: a fixed subset risks overfitting, while independently resampled subsets have differing difficulty and therefore yield scores that are not directly comparable (Sections 1 and 3.3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Adaptive task selection:** Each task is weighted using the Bernoulli variance of its historical outcomes, a floor for never-solved tasks, and an uncertainty bonus for tasks with few observations. This emphasizes tasks near the current capability frontier while retaining exploration (Equation 2, Section 3.3).

- **Sampling-aware score estimation:** Task inclusion probabilities are estimated by Monte Carlo simulation and used to estimate full-validation-set performance. The method selects either a Hájek estimator or an anchored difference estimator based on the validation-pool structure observed before search (Equations 3–4, Section 3.3; Appendix A).

- **Co-evolving evaluation protocol:** A fresh weighted subset is drawn for each candidate, statistics are updated after evaluation, and the final harness is chosen by estimated full-set score. The procedure depends only on past success/failure outcomes, not task content or harness code (Sections 3.1 and 3.3; Figure 2).

- **Empirical contribution:** The method is evaluated on three online text-classification datasets and Terminal-Bench 2.1, including component ablations, estimator swaps, score-estimation analysis, cost measurements, and task-discriminability tracking (Sections 4–5 and Appendices A–E).

These results support the authors’ claim that adaptive partial evaluation can preserve much of full-search performance while reducing task executions, token usage, and time in the tested settings. The ablation also supports contributions from all three components, although resampling provides the largest measured increment.

The evidence does not establish universal superiority over full search: the 20% text-classification advantage is small and based on three runs, Terminal-Bench has no separate held-out test set, and partial-score ranking remains imperfect—even at 20%, the selected candidate was only 12th of 60 by full-validation score.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated limitation:** The number of tasks evaluated for each candidate is fixed in advance. The method cannot stop early for clearly inferior candidates or allocate extra evaluations to close comparisons (Section 6).

- **Author-stated scope boundary:** Harness optimization provides no benefit when the underlying model already leaves little benchmark headroom; full search found no improvement for DeepSeek-V4-Flash (Appendix C).

- **Estimator dependence:** The appropriate estimator varies with the validation-pool structure. The authors choose it using Phase-0 results and benchmark type, and estimator swaps substantially degrade or destabilize estimates (Section 3.3; Appendix A). A fully automatic, benchmark-independent selection rule is not demonstrated.

- **Weak ranking at very small budgets:** At 7%, estimated and true candidate scores have only 0.13 Spearman correlation. The authors attribute this to evaluating only two or three examples per dataset (Table 6; Section 5.3).

- **Terminal-Bench validity risk:** The same 89 tasks are used for both optimization and final reporting, giving full search an acknowledged selection advantage and preventing a held-out generalization test (Sections 4.3–4.4).

- **Analyst inference:** The text-classification superiority over full search may reflect reduced overfitting, as the authors hypothesize, but the supplied experiments do not isolate that mechanism through validation-size, independent-search-set, or repeated-test-set ablations.

- **Analyst inference:** Cost is not proportional to the number of sampled tasks. Variance weighting favors longer multi-turn tasks, so Task-CoEvolve uses more tokens per trial than uniform resampling; efficiency comparisons therefore depend on whether the budget is defined by executions, tokens, or time (Section 4.5).

- **Reproducibility risk:** Hyperparameters and major protocols are reported, and code is promised, but the supplied text does not confirm that the repository is currently available or provide complete implementation and environment details.

## Open research questions

1. **Can evaluation allocation adapt within a candidate?** The fixed subset size prevents early stopping and additional sampling for ambiguous comparisons. A next experiment could compare Task-CoEvolve with sequential confidence-bound or racing procedures under matched token and wall-clock budgets.

2. **Can estimator choice be learned automatically?** Performance depends strongly on choosing Hájek versus anchored difference estimation. A next experiment could evaluate a pre-registered selection diagnostic or a doubly robust estimator across validation pools with systematically varied mean success rates and task heterogeneity.

3. **Does adaptive sampling improve held-out generalization for terminal agents?** Terminal-Bench uses the search pool as the final evaluation pool, so generalization is unresolved. A next experiment could optimize on one task split and report performance on a disjoint, sufficiently powered terminal-task split.

4. **How should informativeness be balanced against execution cost?** Variance-weighted sampling selects discriminative but often expensive tasks. A next experiment could weight estimated information gain by predicted tokens, latency, or monetary cost and compare Pareto frontiers at matched final performance.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the paper’s stated method, experimental settings, and reported results because the supplied text includes the main sections, tables, and appendices.

Material missing or unresolved information includes statistical significance tests or confidence intervals for between-method differences; more than three text-classification runs; repeated Terminal-Bench runs or uncertainty estimates; a held-out Terminal-Bench split; complete code and environment details; sensitivity analyses for \(\ell\), \(\lambda\), Monte Carlo repetitions, and history-window length; and evidence across additional harness-optimization frameworks or task modalities.
