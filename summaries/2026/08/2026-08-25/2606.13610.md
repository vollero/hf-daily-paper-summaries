# One Polluted Page Is Enough: Evaluating Web Content Pollution in LLM Recommenders

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.13610) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13610)


## One-sentence takeaway

FORGE finds that search-augmented LLM recommenders can promote entirely fake products after minimal pollution of retrieved web evidence, while reasoning and simple skepticism prompts may worsen the failure and consensus filters sharply reduce legitimate recommendations.

## Problem and motivation

Search-augmented LLMs inherit part of their trust boundary from the open web, where commercially motivated actors can publish plausible fake reviews that reach recommendation pipelines through ordinary search ranking. Unlike training poisoning, closed-corpus RAG poisoning, or prompt injection, this attack preserves fluent, relevant, policy-compliant output and may promote a nonexistent brand without obvious malicious instructions or anomalous text (Introduction, Table 1).

The authors identify a measurement gap: existing benchmarks do not isolate whether an LLM will treat plausible but polluted live-web evidence as credible support for recommending a fake product.

## Approach and contributions

- **FORGE benchmark:** A controlled simulation of the query → live search → top-10 evidence → LLM recommendation pipeline. Retrieved pages are frozen, then selected documents are locally modified rather than contaminating the public web (§3; Figures 1 and 12).
- **Three pollution styles:** Entity replacement, promotional-passage injection, and full-document synthesis test attacks with different content structures (§3.2).
- **Broad evaluation:** The main experiment covers 225 products, 15 categories, five consumer scenarios, and 12 closed-source or open-weights LLMs, using greedy decoding (§3.1, §4.1).
- **Mechanistic analyses:** The paper studies retrieval rank, pollution dose, reasoning, prior brand agreement, and fabricated social proof (§4–5).
- **Defense assessment:** It evaluates skepticism prompting, filtering against model priors, and cross-document agreement (§6).
- **Released asset:** The authors state that they release the FORGE benchmark and evaluation harness, while keeping simulated polluted documents private (Abstract; Conclusion; Ethical Considerations).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| All evaluated models were vulnerable under top-3 entity replacement. | Across 12 models × 225 products, per-model fooled rates ranged from **13.3% to 73.8%**, averaging **42.7%**. | Table 2 |
| One highly ranked polluted page could suffice, while lower positions were much weaker. | On six open-weights models and 45 Digital Products cells per model, rank-1 replacement produced **2%–27%** fooled rates; ranks 2–10 were generally **0%–13%**, with most cells in the 0%–4% range. | Figure 4; Table 9 |
| Increasing pollution generally increased attack success. | With 1, 3, and 10 polluted top-10 pages, model-specific rates ranged respectively from **2%–27%**, **16%–64%**, and **44%–100%** on the six-model Digital Products subset. | Figure 5; Table 8 |
| Vulnerability differed strongly by category. | The 12-model category mean ranged from **22.8%** for Phone/PC to **81.7%** for Dining; the category effect was significant, Friedman \(\chi^2(14)=99.4\), \(p<10^{-14}\). | Table 2; §4.2 |
| Cross-model prior agreement predicted lower vulnerability. | Across 15 categories, evidence-free brand-set agreement correlated negatively with fooled rate: Pearson \(r=-0.65\), \(p<0.01\). | Figure 8; §5 |
| Disabling reasoning reduced vulnerability in two instrumentable models. | Matched 225-cell runs reduced fooled rate from **56.9% to 38.7%** for Qwen3.5-9B and **80.4% to 71.6%** for GLM-4.6V-Flash; McNemar \(p=2.8\times10^{-7}\) and \(1.7\times10^{-3}\). | Figure 3; Table 16, Appendix N |
| Fooled outputs showed more unsupported social proof. | A 14-phrase lexical scan fired **1.5–11×** more often in fooled than resisted outputs; qualitative cases contain claims absent from the polluted pages. | §5; Table 5, Appendix B |
| Full synthesis was the strongest tested attack overall. | On matched five-product subsets across all models and categories, average fooled rates were **78% A3**, **38% A1**, and **25% A2**; A3 exceeded A1 on 11/12 models. | Table 11, Appendix J |
| The skepticism prompt backfired on average. | Across 2,700 cells, fooled rate rose by **10.5 percentage points** overall; the closed-source mean change was **+24 pp**, versus **−3 pp** for open-weights models. | §6; Table 13, Appendix L |
| Consensus filters caught most fake brands but damaged utility. | D2 removed about **95%** of planted brands while discarding **62%–79%** of legitimate recommendations; D3 at \(\tau=4\) caught **89.9%** while discarding **52%–73%**. | Figure 11; Tables 13–14 |
| The broad category ordering replicated in English. | Across 360 English trials, mean rates were **43% smartphones < 58% skincare < 87% SF restaurants**; 8/12 models were within ±10 pp of their matched Chinese average. | Figure 7; Table 12, Appendix K |

These results establish substantial vulnerability within FORGE’s controlled, locally rewritten evidence bundles. The paired reasoning ablation supports a causal effect for the two tested open-weights models, but does not establish that reasoning universally increases vulnerability across architectures. Likewise, the English experiment supports transfer to three US-region categories, not comprehensive multilingual or geographic generality. Because FORGE does not inject content into the live web or re-run search after pollution, it measures LLM consumption of polluted evidence once retrieved—not an attacker’s end-to-end ability to obtain search visibility.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Attacks were not optimized, so the authors interpret measured effectiveness as a possible lower bound; domain-tailored or query-aware attacks were not tested (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** Rank-position and dose-response scans cover only Digital Products and six open-weights models; reasoning-trace analysis and the D2 defense also require open-weights instrumentation (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** The main evaluation is Chinese, and local-life data are restricted to Shenzhen. English replication covers only three categories and one US local setting (Limitations; Appendix K).
- **Author-stated:** Bundles are a static 2026-04 retrieval snapshot, so absolute vulnerability rates may change as web content and search rankings evolve (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** Anchor selection depends on an LLM/rule/human pipeline. A 300-slot pilot achieved 75.3% exact agreement and \(\kappa=0.752\), but category-level agreement ranged from 0.48 to 1.00 (Appendix A).
- **Author-stated:** Polluted documents are private for dual-use reasons, limiting full independent reconstruction even though the benchmark and harness are released (Ethical Considerations).
- **Analyst inference:** Single greedy decoding does not measure output variance or attack reliability under stochastic generation; the paper reports only one T=0 completion per cell (§4.1).
- **Analyst inference:** The A3-versus-A1 comparison does not isolate document realism: A3 simultaneously changes body content, brand density, real-brand corroboration, and URL path. The authors explicitly acknowledge this confounding (Appendix J).
- **Analyst inference:** The binary substring metric is well audited, but prefix matching still has demonstrated linguistic collisions. Their observed pooled controls are low—0.30% without evidence and 0% with clean bundles—rather than logically eliminating all false positives (§3.3; Appendix I).

## Open research questions

1. **Can retrieval-time credibility controls reduce attacks without suppressing useful long-tail recommendations?**  
   This remains open because the tested defenses are prompt-level or post-hoc and lose substantial legitimate utility. A next experiment could compare source-reputation weighting, domain diversification, and provenance-aware reranking on identical FORGE bundles, reporting both fooled rate and legitimate-brand survival.

2. **Which component makes full-document synthesis stronger?**  
   A3 changes several factors simultaneously, and the paper cannot separate removal of real-brand evidence from fake-brand density or synthetic style. A factorial experiment could independently vary brand density, retained real-brand mentions, body length, writing style, and URL changes.

3. **Does reasoning increase vulnerability across models and reasoning regimes?**  
   The causal ablation covers only two open-weights models with exposed toggles. Future work could test more models with matched reasoning budgets and compare unguided reasoning against explicit source-verification procedures.

4. **How stable are the findings under live, longitudinal retrieval?**  
   Frozen bundles exclude changes in indexing, SEO competition, and search ranking. A safe next study could take repeated clean retrieval snapshots across regions and time, then apply pollution locally to quantify temporal and ranking sensitivity without publishing fake pages.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported benchmark setup and numerical results because the supplied text includes the complete main paper and detailed appendices.

Materially unavailable or unresolved information includes independent reproduction results; access to the private polluted documents; full multilingual and multi-region evaluations; stochastic-decoding robustness; end-to-end live-search attack success; and broad reasoning-on/off evidence beyond the two tested models.
