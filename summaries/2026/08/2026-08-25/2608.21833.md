# GameXpert-Bench: How Far Are Coding Agents from Expert Game Development?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.21833) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21833)


## One-sentence takeaway

GameXpert-Bench finds that current coding agents create playable game foundations and follow explicit requirements more reliably than they autonomously discover defects, verify runtime behavior, or preserve functionality during cumulative refinement.

## Problem and motivation

The paper addresses how to evaluate coding agents across the user-facing game-development lifecycle, where gameplay logic, rendering, interfaces, audiovisual elements, controls, and state transitions must work together in an executable artifact. The authors argue that successful builds, plausible screenshots, and final-artifact scores cannot reveal whether runtime behavior works or how agents handle later diagnosis, repair, and refinement. Their identified gap is that prior game benchmarks generally evaluate a final product or an isolated development stage, rather than generation, repair, and multi-turn optimization together (Introduction; Table 1, pp. 2–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Lifecycle formulation:** From qualitative analysis of complete human–agent trajectories, the authors derive three recurring stages: generation, diagnosis and repair, and optimization (Section 3.1, pp. 4–5).

- **Unified benchmark:** GameXpert-Bench operationalizes these stages as GameGen, GameFix, and GameOpt, all grounded in evidence from executable artifacts rather than source code alone (Sections 3.2–3.3, p. 6).

- **Three evaluation collections:** GameGen provides 97 single-request tasks across 11 genres; GameFix provides 100 tasks per run from 50 human-verified games with 19–27 reversible bug injections each; GameOpt provides 17 six-turn chains containing 102 requests and 701 acceptance criteria (Sections 4.1, 5.1, and 6.2; pp. 6, 13, 18).

- **Stage-specific verification:** Generation combines runtime event checks and human ratings; repair uses deterministic Fail-to-Pass and Pass-to-Pass probes; optimization uses evidence-grounded final-product criteria and regression penalties (Sections 4.3, 5.3, and 6.3).

- **Empirical diagnosis:** Evaluations of current agents reveal a recurring gap between producing plausible implementations and delivering rich, verified, regression-free games (Conclusion, p. 22).

The evidence supports the authors’ claim that explicit implementation is generally more reliable than autonomous discovery, runtime verification, and regression-free integration within these benchmark settings. It does not establish general performance across all game engines, professional production pipelines, longer interactions, or unconstrained human collaboration. Associations—such as that between automated functional and human-perceived GameGen scores—are correlational, not causal (Figure 8, p. 12).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope limitation:** GameFix’s 19–27 simultaneous defects are a deliberate long-horizon stress test, not a claim about typical real-game bug counts (Section 5.2, p. 13).

- **Author-stated access limitation:** The 50 GameFix Gold Games are confidential and remain internal, restricting independent reproduction of that track despite deterministic grading (Section 5.1, p. 13).

- **Author-stated GameOpt confound:** Turn order and design dimension are not adequately separated—audio is always turn six—so the late-turn rebound does not establish long-context stability (Section 6.4, p. 21; Table 19, p. 35).

- **Author-stated judgment boundary:** Thirty-three GameOpt criteria are proxies; implementation evidence for such criteria does not prove aesthetically superior experience (Section 6.2, p. 19).

- **Analyst inference:** GameGen’s shared rubrics are partly derived from events observed across evaluated models. Although humans curate them and every artifact receives the same rubric, the supplied text does not report an ablation against rubrics derived independently of model outputs (Section 4.3, p. 7).

- **Analyst inference:** GameOpt uses final-product-only scoring, so intermediate regressions that are repaired before the sixth turn are not measured directly (Section 6.3, p. 19).

- **Analyst inference:** The supplied text gives detailed authoring checks for GameFix probes, but no equivalent independent validation study of GameGen’s automated evaluator or GameOpt’s evidence-grounded judge is reported.

## Open research questions

1. **Does repair performance transfer to more realistic bug loads?** The benchmark intentionally co-injects 19–27 bugs, so performance on one or a few naturally occurring defects remains open. A next experiment could stratify identical mutation operators into 1-, 5-, 10-, and 20-bug tasks.

2. **Can turn retention be separated from request difficulty?** GameOpt’s turn positions and dimensions are confounded. A counterbalanced experiment could permute all six dimensions across longer chains while holding requests and starting snapshots fixed.

3. **How much do model-derived GameGen rubrics affect rankings?** The shared rubric pools events observed across evaluated models, and no rubric-construction ablation is reported. A follow-up could compare rankings under expert-authored, specification-only, and cross-model-derived rubrics.

4. **Which interventions improve autonomous defect discovery?** GameFix identifies discovery, verification, value recovery, planning, regression control, and stopping as interacting abilities, but does not isolate causal contributions. Controlled agent variants could add one mechanism at a time—such as mandatory runtime testing or coverage-based stopping—and measure STRICT and Cliff changes.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the benchmark design and reported numerical results because the supplied text includes the main methods, tables, figures, and substantial appendix material.

Material missing or unavailable includes public access details for the confidential GameFix games; full annotation and inter-rater reliability statistics for GameGen human scores; judge agreement or calibration statistics for GameOpt; complete execution budgets and hardware/runtime costs; statistical uncertainty for most leaderboard differences; and ablations isolating rubric construction, weighting choices, harness effects beyond the two reported GPT comparisons, or individual agentic abilities.
