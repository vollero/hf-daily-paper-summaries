# MameLoshnLM: Yiddish Language Model and Evaluation Benchmark

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-07.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.05850) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05850)


## One-sentence takeaway

Continued pretraining of Llama 3.1 8B on a curated, Yiddish-dominant corpus produces an open 8B model that leads comparable open models on the authors’ multi-task benchmark and better captures Yiddish-specific lexical and morphological patterns.

## Problem and motivation

Yiddish has a rich literary record and roughly one million speakers but limited, unreliable digital resources. The authors identify gaps across the entire language-model pipeline: little verified pretraining text, noisy multilingual corpora, scarce native-authored evaluation data, and no prior comprehensive modern LM development effort for Yiddish. Their audit finds that less than half of mC4’s nominal Yiddish split comes from validated native sources, illustrating why simply using web-scale multilingual data is inadequate (Sections 1 and 3.1; Appendix D).

## Approach and contributions

- **Oytser corpus:** More than 915M words from nine contemporary web-native and literary sources, including over 12,000 OCRed books and more than 0.3M native web documents (Section 3.2; Table 2).
- **Kashes benchmark:** Nine tasks covering translation, linguistic analysis, NER, commonsense QA, question generation, and paraphrase detection. It includes Kashes-mt, a new 5,287-pair, native-authored Yiddish–English translation set (Section 4; Table 3; Figure 1).
- **MameLoshnLM:** Llama 3.1 8B continued-pretrained for one epoch on approximately 5.7B tokens, using a Yiddish-dominant mixture supplemented with English to limit catastrophic forgetting (Section 5).
- **Corpus and linguistic analyses:** An audit of mC4’s Yiddish split and targeted probes of Hebrew/Aramaic-origin vocabulary and Yiddish morphology (Section 8; Appendices D–E).
- **Language-mixture experiment:** Two variants test whether German, Hebrew, Polish, and Russian improve adaptation; results are limited and inconsistent relative to the Yiddish-dominant mixture (Section 7.2; Appendix F).

The evidence supports the authors’ claim that targeted continued pretraining improves both benchmark performance and specific aspects of Yiddish lexical and morphological competence relative to comparable open baselines. It does not establish native-level proficiency, general superiority outside Yiddish, or that corpus quality alone caused every improvement: the intervention combines curated data, continued pretraining, and a particular language mixture.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope constraints:** Native Yiddish general-understanding benchmarks are unavailable, so PIQA, WikiQA, and PAWS-Wiki use machine-translated Aya data; MameLoshnLM’s main exceptions occur on PIQA and PAWS-Wiki (Sections 4 and 7.1).
- **Author-stated mixture finding:** Related-language transfer is specific to the tested CC100 mixtures and training budget; it produced only limited, inconsistent gains (Section 7.2).
- **Resource and licensing constraints:** Training requires about 207 H200 GPU-hours, and the released model is non-commercial because of source agreements (Section 5; Ethics Statement).
- **Privacy residual risk:** Forum data were de-identified and inspected, but the authors report only that sampled final data contained no readily linkable explicit personal information, not that re-identification is impossible (Section 3.2; Ethics Statement).
- **Analyst inference — benchmark breadth:** Nine tasks are broad for Yiddish, but many datasets are small or domain-specific, and three general-understanding tasks are translated; broader interactive generation and real-user evaluation remain untested.
- **Analyst inference — causal attribution:** There is no reported same-size experiment that cleanly separates the effect of corpus curation from corpus quantity or literary-versus-contemporary composition.
- **Analyst inference — audit uncertainty:** Machine-translation detection partly relies on URL patterns, locale siblings, and manual judgments. Appendix D acknowledges possible false positives and labels native/MT shares as lower bounds.
- **Analyst inference — probe coverage:** Lexical analysis depends on one LK lexicon and morphology on the YiTB test set, so these probes measure important but incomplete dimensions of native-like Yiddish.

## Open research questions

1. **Which parts of Oytser drive the gains?** The paper combines historical books, edited web text, scripture, and informal forums without reporting source-removal ablations. A next experiment could train matched-token variants that omit each source group in turn.

2. **Does the model help Yiddish speakers and scholars in realistic workflows?** The paper evaluates benchmark tasks rather than interactive or end-to-end use. A next study could measure expert accuracy and time savings on archival search, annotation, or historical newspaper extraction.

3. **Can native-authored Yiddish replace translated general-understanding tests?** PIQA, WikiQA, and PAWS-Wiki rely on translated material. Native speakers could author culturally and linguistically grounded reasoning and paraphrase sets, followed by human validation and cross-model evaluation.

4. **Can related-language mixing help without diluting Yiddish tokens?** The tested mixtures change the target-language token share. A controlled experiment could hold Yiddish tokens, total tokens, and optimization steps fixed while independently varying each related language.
