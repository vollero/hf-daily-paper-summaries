# On-Policy Delta Distillation for Multilingual Math Reasoning

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-07.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.05802) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05802)


## One-sentence takeaway

On Qwen3 math benchmarks, OPD 2’s teacher-versus-base delta signal outperforms standard on-policy distillation across English, Korean, and Japanese, while English-only training transfers accuracy but often fails to preserve the requested response language.

## Problem and motivation

On-Policy Distillation (OPD) supplies token-level teacher feedback on student-generated trajectories and is presented as a potentially data- and compute-efficient alternative to sequence-level reinforcement learning. Prior OPD research focused largely on English, while multilingual work used self-distillation rather than a stronger teacher; consequently, evidence for teacher-based OPD in Korean and Japanese was limited (§1).

The paper asks whether OPD 2 remains superior to OPD across languages, reduces English–Korean reasoning disparities, and transfers from English without degrading target-language generation (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Multilingual evaluation of OPD 2:** The authors compare standard OPD with OPD 2 on Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-8B students, using Qwen3-30B-A3B-2507 as teacher, across English, Korean, and Japanese math benchmarks (§3.1).
- **Delta-based supervision:** Instead of rewarding the teacher–student log-probability gap, OPD 2 uses the gap between a post-trained teacher and its corresponding base model, intended to isolate capabilities acquired during post-training (§2.1, Eq. 4). Reward centering and an original-OPD-based conditioning criterion are also used, though their formulations are omitted.
- **Language-gap analysis:** The study measures how multilingual OPD and OPD 2 affect English–Korean accuracy differences across seven benchmark groupings (§3.3).
- **Accuracy-versus-language distinction:** An English-only training comparison shows that cross-lingual accuracy transfer can occur while outputs shift toward English, motivating response-language evaluation alongside accuracy (§3.4, Table 1).

The evidence supports consistent gains within the tested Qwen3 models, languages, training recipe, and math benchmarks. It does not establish generalization to other model families, domains, teachers, or languages. Numbers in §3.2/§3.4 and Appendix A use different benchmark averages, so superficially different values should not be compared without preserving their benchmark sets.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** The experiments cover only mathematical reasoning in English, Korean, and Japanese, with two Qwen3 student sizes and one Qwen3 teacher (§3.1).
- **Incomplete method specification:** The paper omits the formulations for OPD 2 reward centering and its conditioning criterion, referring readers to earlier work (§2.1). This supplied text also does not provide the training hyperparameter values beyond 100 optimization steps and equalized examples (§3.1).
- **Analyst inference — limited causal isolation:** The study compares OPD and the complete OPD 2 recipe, but reports no ablations separating the delta signal from reward centering and conditioning; their individual contributions therefore remain unclear.
- **Analyst inference — language metric coverage:** Target-language rates are reported only for PolyMath, and thinking-mode language is measured only after `</think>` even though the paper says intermediate reasoning is primarily English (§3.4, Table 1). The metric therefore does not establish target-language reasoning throughout the full trajectory.
- **Analyst inference — comparability risk:** Main-text and appendix averages aggregate different benchmark sets (§3.2, §3.4, Appendix A), which requires care when interpreting seemingly inconsistent scores.
- **Analyst inference — limited robustness evidence:** No variance, confidence intervals, repeated-run results, or statistical significance tests are supplied.

## Open research questions

1. **Does OPD 2 generalize beyond Qwen3 and East Asian languages?** The study uses one model family and three languages. A next experiment could repeat the controlled OPD/OPD 2 comparison across unrelated model families and a typologically broader language set.

2. **Which OPD 2 component produces the multilingual gains?** Delta rewards, centering, and conditioning are always combined. A factorial ablation could remove each component separately while holding rollouts, teacher, and training budget fixed.

3. **Does transferred accuracy reflect target-language reasoning rather than English-mediated reasoning?** The paper measures only final-answer language for thinking models and notes that their hidden reasoning is primarily English. A next experiment could score language use and reasoning validity at each generated step, alongside final-answer accuracy.

4. **How much multilingual data is needed to preserve language without sacrificing transfer?** Only balanced 1:1:1 multilingual and English-only datasets are compared. A mixture-ratio sweep could measure the accuracy–language-preservation frontier for Korean and Japanese.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported claims because the supplied text includes methods, settings, and detailed numerical summaries.

Material missing information includes the actual numeric cells of Tables 1–10, complete hyperparameters, language-identification procedure, decoding settings, run-to-run variability, significance testing, component ablations, contamination checks, compute cost, and evaluations beyond Qwen3, mathematics, and the three studied languages.
