# OSReward: Instituting Standardized Evaluation for Cross-Platform Computer-Use Reward Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-07.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.28609) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28609)


## One-sentence takeaway

OSReward shows that VLM judges of computer-use trajectories are systematically lenient on failed runs, then uses this diagnosis to train open 9B and 35B reward models that substantially improve failure detection at 30–60× lower estimated cost than frontier judges.

## Problem and motivation

Computer-use evaluation, data curation, and reinforcement learning require deciding whether an agent trajectory actually fulfilled its instruction. Human-written verifiers cover limited, predefined tasks and cannot inspect static trajectories without a live environment, while human annotation is too costly to scale (§1).

The authors identify two gaps: prior judge evaluations are generally confined to one platform and often reuse trajectories or potentially noisy verifier labels; and existing judges reportedly offer no reliable, open, affordable reward signal for large-scale training (§§1–2.2).

## Approach and contributions

- **OSReward benchmark:** 1,019 human-gold trajectories collected specifically for judge evaluation across web, Android, Ubuntu, and Windows, using four agent-backbone families and both GUI-only and GUI+CLI workflows. Three annotators label each trajectory, with disagreements adjudicated by senior reviewers (§§3.1–3.4; Figs. 3–5).

- **Diagnostic variants:** OSReward-Hard contains 284 re-verified difficult cases, mostly originating from annotator disagreements; OSReward-Multi adds human alignment and efficiency labels to successful trajectories (§3.4).

- **Standardized judge study:** Twenty-seven VLM judges receive the same prompt, the last five states by default, and associated action/reasoning text. Evaluation uses accuracy, success recall, failure recall, and balanced accuracy, supplemented by input, decoding, cost, and error analyses (§§4–5).

- **Open training assets:** OS-Shepherd-100K comprises about 100K reasoning-annotated judgment samples selected through high agreement among strong judges. The authors train OS-Shepherd-9B and OS-Shepherd-35B-A3B with supervised fine-tuning followed by GRPO targeted primarily at recoverable false-success errors (§§6.1–6.2; Appendix D).

The evidence establishes performance under a common protocol on a carefully reviewed benchmark and shows that the training recipe moves two Qwen3.5-derived models away from extreme leniency. It does **not** establish that benchmark-verifier agreement on OSWorld, WebArena, or AndroidWorld equals ground-truth accuracy; the authors explicitly describe those results as agreement with potentially imperfect verifiers (§7.1). Cost results are estimates based on API prices or comparable-model market rates, not controlled hardware measurements (§5.4; Appendix E.1).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** External-benchmark evaluations use human-written verifiers that may themselves be wrong, so they measure agreement rather than true accuracy (§7.1).

- **Author-stated:** OSReward-Multi is exploratory, applies only to successful trajectories, and contains highly skewed alignment labels; the sole alignment-0 case was removed as a safety concern (§3.4; Appendix B.4).

- **Author-stated:** The training corpus cannot afford benchmark-style human annotation and instead uses high-agreement VLM ensemble labels. Shared judge errors may therefore survive the filter (§6.1).

- **Author-stated:** Training-scale instruction collection relies partly on synthesized instructions—about 25% of combined Ubuntu/Windows instructions and 10% of web instructions (§6.1).

- **Author-stated:** The default protocol exposes only the last five states. On external OSWorld trajectories longer than 16 steps, reported pooled accuracy falls from 0.76 to 0.57 while false-positive rate rises from 0.20 to 0.37 (Appendix E.5; Fig. 18).

- **Analyst inference:** OSReward-Hard is intentionally enriched for disputed and failed cases, so its raw accuracy is diagnostic rather than representative of an unknown real-world deployment distribution (§3.4).

- **Analyst inference:** The same mined RL set and training recipe are used for both OS-Shepherd sizes, but the reported final transfer results do not isolate the RL stage from SFT, as the authors acknowledge (§7.2).

- **Analyst inference:** Gold labels are strong but not infallible: re-verification corrected 18 earlier labels, demonstrating that even the multi-stage process contained initial errors (Appendix B.2).

## Open research questions

1. **Can judges verify long trajectories without relying mainly on agent narration?** The last-five-state protocol loses evidence on long runs, while additional trailing screenshots show no consistent aggregate benefit. A next experiment could compare learned state selection, full-history visual memory, and artifact-aware retrieval under equal token and compute budgets.

2. **Can false-success bias be reduced without sacrificing success recall?** OS-Shepherd relocates its operating point toward higher failure recall, with some loss of success recognition (§7.2). A controlled experiment could train confidence-calibrated or class-conditional objectives and report both recalls across deployment-relevant base rates.

3. **How much noise remains in agreement-filtered training labels?** The training labels are not human-gold, and judges herd on difficult examples (§§5.3, 6.1). A next study could human-audit stratified samples across agreement levels, platforms, trajectory lengths, and failure types.

4. **Can fine-grained alignment and efficiency ratings be calibrated?** Judges rank these labels better than they emit usable rating levels, and alignment labels are highly imbalanced (§4.5; Appendix B.4). A next experiment could collect more low-alignment successes and compare ordinal calibration, pairwise ranking, and rubric-specific training.
