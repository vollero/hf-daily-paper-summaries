# Position: AI Agents in Scientific Teams Should Be Studied as Human-Agent Systems

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.14667) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14667)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors argue that scientific AI agents should be evaluated as parts of human–agent teams, because current systems rarely support sustained collaboration and preliminary evidence suggests that agents do not reliably seek useful human feedback without explicit prompting.

## Problem and motivation

Research on “AI Scientists” primarily emphasizes autonomous task completion, typically leaving humans to set goals, approve intermediate outputs, or review final artifacts. The authors argue that this neglects the social and interactive character of science and makes the agent—not the human–agent pair—the unit of analysis (Sections 1–2).

This gap matters because poorly designed collaboration may amplify hallucinations, bias, homogenization, accountability failures, cognitive offloading, and pressure on scientific institutions (Section 3). Conversely, the case studies suggest that expert judgment and agent implementation capacity can be mutually beneficial, although they do not establish complementarity through controlled comparisons (Section 4).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Human-feedback review:** The authors organize a representative collection of AI Scientist systems by feedback channel—artifact-level, discovery-phase-level, research-plan-level, and asynchronous steering or interruption—and by feedback type, granularity, and phase (Section 2; Table 1).

2. **Preliminary agent experiment:** On 10 AstaBench End-to-End Discovery development tasks, they test whether a GPT-5-mini ReAct agent seeks feedback when given an `ask user` tool, feedback encouragement, or mandatory per-action questioning (Section 2.5; Table 2).

3. **Risk and synergy analysis:** They synthesize reported risks and analyze two published scientist–agent case studies to illustrate how expert oversight and agent execution may augment one another (Sections 3–4).

4. **Conceptual framework and evaluation proposals:** They define team utility as collaboration advantage minus collaboration disadvantage and propose staged evaluation plus proxy measures such as Review Time, Intervention Density, and Simulated Oracle Synergy (Sections 5–6).

The paper directly establishes only a small preliminary result about feedback-seeking behavior in one agent configuration and a structured qualitative account of existing systems. It does not demonstrate that HAS designs outperform humans or agents alone, that the proposed utility model predicts outcomes, or that the case-study collaborations achieved formal complementarity. Several broader risk statistics are drawn from cited studies rather than generated by this paper.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated limitations:** The AstaBench experiment uses only 10 computationally intensive development tasks. The authors describe it as a deep rather than broad evaluation (Section 2.5).

- **Author-stated limitations:** Rigorous “centaur” comparisons among human–agent teams, humans alone, and agents alone are expensive, complex, and difficult to reproduce for open-ended scientific work (Section 6).

- **Author-stated limitations:** The two cases illustrate augmentation; they do not prove that the team exceeded the best member working alone (Section 4).

- **Analyst inference:** The experiment studies one GPT-5-mini ReAct harness, uses automatic continuation rather than substantive human responses, and relies on three LLM judges. The supplied text reports no human validation of those classifications, uncertainty estimates, or repeated-run variance (Table 2; Section 2.5).

- **Analyst inference:** Mandatory per-step questioning changes both interaction frequency and likely task execution burden, so Table 2 does not isolate whether the lower score arises from excessive questions, automatic responses, prompt changes, or another factor.

- **Analyst inference:** The system review is described as “representative,” but no search protocol, inclusion criteria, coverage analysis, or independent annotation agreement is supplied; its frequency claims should therefore be read as qualitative.

- **Analyst inference:** Review Time, Intervention Density, and Simulated Oracle Synergy are proposed proxies, not empirically validated measures of collaboration cost or advantage (Section 6).

## Open research questions

1. **Can an agent learn when human input is decision-relevant without asking at every step?** Table 2 shows almost no spontaneous questioning but excessive interaction under enforcement. A next experiment could compare learned or uncertainty-triggered question policies against fixed, optional, and mandatory policies using real human responses.

2. **Do human–agent scientific teams outperform both members alone?** The case studies demonstrate plausible augmentation but lack matched controls. A controlled centaur study could assign comparable discovery tasks to experts alone, agents alone, and expert–agent teams, measuring output quality, time, and coordination cost.

3. **Which feedback forms best prevent scientific errors?** Corrective requests represented only 4% of questions under enforced interaction, and the study did not test whether feedback corrected failures. A factorial experiment could vary feedback type, timing, and granularity while tracking factual, methodological, and code-level errors.

4. **Do the proposed proxy metrics predict meaningful scientific synergy?** Review Time, Intervention Density, and Simulated Oracle Synergy remain unvalidated. A next study could compare them with expert-rated cognitive load and controlled team-performance gains across several scientific domains.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the paper’s position, taxonomy, experiment design, and reported Table 2 values; **medium** for broader conclusions about the AI Scientist literature because the review methodology is incompletely specified.

Material missing information includes the literature-search and selection protocol, complete system-level annotations beyond the representative table, exact AstaBench tasks, prompts and agent settings, run-to-run variability, judge prompts and agreement, human validation, statistical uncertainty, error-level task outcomes, code or data availability, and controlled human-only or agent-only comparisons.
