# Accuracy and Order Sensitivity Diverge Under Label-Free Strategies

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.11947) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11947)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across six language models and two MCQ benchmarks, removing or reducing option-order influence did not reliably improve accuracy, because withholding options, losing comparative context, and matching failures introduced distinct costs.

## Problem and motivation

Multiple-choice accuracy can conflate model knowledge with sensitivity to option ordering, weakening its validity as an evaluation measure. Existing mitigations may require logit access or repeated calls—up to \(k!\) for full permutation—so the authors investigate prompt-level, label-free alternatives that could work with closed APIs. The identified gap is that prior work often treats bias reduction and accuracy improvement as aligned, without directly examining cases where they diverge (§1; §2, “Positioning of this work”).

## Approach and contributions

- The authors evaluate two strategies: two-stage prompting, which generates an answer without options and then uses an LLM to match it to the displayed choices; and independent hypothesis scoring, which scores each option separately from 0–100 (§3.4).
- They compare these strategies with direct MCQ prompting, cyclic permutation, and—where logits are available—PriDe, using six models on 1,000 sampled MMLU questions and 1,000 ARC-Challenge questions (§3.1–§3.5).
- A \(2\times2\) diagnostic grid crosses hidden versus visible Stage-1 options with embedding versus LLM matching, isolating option withholding as the principal bottleneck (§3.6; §4.6).
- They jointly analyze end-to-end accuracy, conditional accuracy, recall standard deviation (RStd), and per-question flip rate, showing that aggregate position balance, direct order sensitivity, and accuracy are distinct properties (§3.8; §4.2–§4.4).
- The experiment code, prompts, and evaluation scripts are reported as publicly available under the MIT license (§3.10).

These results support the authors’ central empirical claim that accuracy and positional robustness can diverge under the tested strategies. They do not establish that positional bias is harmless, that cyclic permutation improves underlying knowledge, or that option withholding universally causes failure. In particular, cyclic also aggregates several predictions, while the proposed under-specification mechanism was not directly tested by question type (§5; “Limitations”).

## Limitations and validity risks

Author-stated limitations:

- The evaluation covers six models, two English benchmarks, mostly four-option questions, and fixed 1,000-question samples; generalization to larger models, other distributions, or more choices is unknown.
- Most conditions were run once. No identical-order controls were included, so provider nondeterminism may inflate flip rates, especially for API models.
- The prompt ablations did not permit Stage-1 reasoning, and no separate or stronger matcher model was tested.
- PriDe used only one calibration-set size.
- Low scoring coverage in semantic-matching cells and Gemini two-stage cells means their flip-rate and RStd estimates concern selected subsets and may overstate position blindness.
- Gemini–ARC independent hypothesis was excluded after only 313/1,000 successful questions because of provider failures.
- Three ARC questions had only three genuine options, while some cloud prompts rendered a placeholder fourth option.
- The proposed explanation that hidden-option questions become under-specified relies partly on prior work rather than direct stratified evidence from these experiments.

Analyst inference: comparisons between methods with different call budgets are not purely procedural comparisons—baseline uses one call, two-stage two, and cyclic and independent hypothesis \(k\) calls (§5). Consequently, cyclic’s advantage cannot be attributed specifically to debiasing, and the paper does not isolate whether it comes from permutation coverage, ensembling, or additional inference opportunities.

## Open research questions

1. **Which question types become under-specified when choices are hidden?** The paper proposes this mechanism but does not test it directly. A next experiment could annotate answerability without options and stratify the hidden-versus-visible accuracy gap by that annotation.

2. **Can reasoning-enabled free-text generation or a stronger independent matcher rescue two-stage prompting?** Neither prompt ablation permits Stage-1 reasoning, and the same model performs matching. A factorial experiment could vary reasoning allowance and matcher identity while holding Stage-1 outputs fixed.

3. **How much observed flipping is true order sensitivity rather than inference nondeterminism?** The study lacks repeated identical-order controls. Repeating each fixed ordering several times would permit decomposition of within-order variability and between-order effects.

4. **Why does cyclic permutation outperform position-invariant isolated scoring?** Both require approximately \(k\) calls, but cyclic wins in 10/11 valid comparisons. Controlled variants could compare majority voting, score averaging, and access to the full option set under a matched call and token budget.
