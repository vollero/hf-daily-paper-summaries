# MegaParts: Scaling Part-Aware 3D Object Generation to 300 Parts via Token-Efficient Autoregressive Modeling

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.14783) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14783)


## One-sentence takeaway

MegaParts combines adaptive-length part geometry tokens with a long-context autoregressive model to generate structured 3D objects containing up to 300 parts, outperforming reported autoregressive and diffusion baselines on the paper’s benchmarks while incurring substantial sequence-length-dependent cost.

## Problem and motivation

Part-aware 3D generation supports localized editing, articulation, and structural control, but existing approaches reportedly focus mostly on objects with fewer than 50 parts and become expensive as detailed part geometry increases token length, memory use, and computation (Sections 1 and 2.2). Autoregressive models offer a scalable alternative to diffusion, but naïvely encoding every part with a long fixed token sequence creates a fidelity–efficiency tradeoff: compression loses geometry, while detailed encoding produces prohibitively long sequences (Section 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Adaptive part tokenizer:** A fully causal VQ-VAE concentrates geometric information in token prefixes. Stochastic prefix truncation and alternating encoder/decoder optimization encourage stable, coarse-to-fine representations (Sections 3.1–3.2).
- **Rate–distortion token allocation:** Each part receives a token budget selected from prefix lengths using Chamfer distance, normal inconsistency, bounding-box IoU, and a length penalty (Section 3.3).
- **Structured autoregressive generation:** A fine-tuned Qwen3-8B serializes object and part bounding boxes followed by each part’s shape tokens, supporting text, object-box, and part-box conditioning (Sections 3.4 and A.1).
- **Long-context scaling:** Staged training extends context from 8,192 to 32K, 128K, and 256K tokens, enabling objects with up to 300 parts (Section A.3).
- **Large training pipeline:** The authors curate nearly 10M part meshes for tokenizer training and approximately 440K part-aware assets for generator training, including 100K private assets (Section A.2).

The evidence supports improved reconstruction, benchmark generation scores, structural adherence, and scaling relative to the particular reported baselines and hardware. It does not establish modality-neutral superiority: the box-conditioned baselines use different auxiliary inputs, and the authors caution that part metrics penalize fused geometry and primarily measure adherence to a prescribed decomposition. Nor does “up to 300 parts” imply reliable generation for every such object—the reported success rate for the 200–300 range is 0.77, and feasibility depends more directly on token length.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Long-context autoregressive training and inference remain expensive, especially for complex objects (Section 6).
- **Author-stated:** The framework models geometry only, excluding textures, materials, semantic annotations, and physical or functional part relationships (Section 6).
- **Author-stated:** Even objects below 300 parts may exceed memory because sequence length depends on part complexity; additional memory or distributed inference may be necessary (Section C.1).
- **Author-stated:** Box-conditioned comparisons are matched on layout but not on auxiliary modality—MegaParts uses text, FullPart an image, and XPart a point cloud (Sections 4.3 and C.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Reproducibility is limited by 100K private training assets, an unspecified repair acceptance threshold, and incompletely reported tokenizer hyperparameters such as rate–distortion weights (Section A.2; Equations 7–10).
- **Analyst inference:** The 300-part boundary partly reflects preprocessing: meshes above 300 connected components are merged until the cap is met, so performance beyond this imposed representation limit is untested (Section A.2).
- **Analyst inference:** The human study’s reliability is difficult to assess because the number and characteristics of raters and votes are not reported in the supplied text (Section C.4).

## Open research questions

1. **Can sequence cost be reduced without losing fine part geometry?** Cost remains the main bottleneck, with 6,735 seconds of autoregressive generation in the 200–300-part range. A next experiment could compare hierarchical, speculative, or multi-token decoding at matched geometry metrics and memory.
2. **How well does the method scale beyond the 300-part preprocessing cap?** Components are currently merged to at most 300. An experiment could retain higher-count objects and measure success, quality, memory, and latency as both part count and token length vary.
3. **Can appearance and physical semantics be generated jointly with geometry?** The current output lacks textures, materials, articulation parameters, and functional relations. A next experiment could add structured appearance and joint tokens and evaluate geometry, material fidelity, and articulation validity separately.
4. **How dependent are results on private data and automated decomposition?** The generator uses 100K private assets, while connected components serve as a part proxy. Training controlled variants on public-only data and on semantic versus connected-component decompositions would isolate these effects.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported architecture, evaluations, and stated limitations because the supplied text includes the main paper and supplementary material.

Material missing information includes the private dataset contents and license, release status of code/models/data, rate–distortion and loss weights, repair thresholds, complete VQ-VAE training schedule and compute budget, baseline retraining details, statistical uncertainty, and human-study participant counts and demographics.
