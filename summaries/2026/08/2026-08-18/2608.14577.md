# HarmProfile: Characterizing Harmful Distributions in Frontier LLMs

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.14577) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14577)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors introduce an attack-free, content-centric benchmark of 80,539 validated text artifacts and report that 23 frontier LLMs exhibit distinct harmfulness and diversity profiles, with diversity—but not harmfulness at conventional significance—correlating with general model capability.

## Problem and motivation

Standard LLM safety evaluations typically reduce failures to refusal or attack-success rates, obscuring what a model produces when safeguards fail. The authors argue that this hides consequential differences in content, severity, actionability, and variation across models (Introduction; Figure 1). Existing content-level studies are described as limited to individual harm domains or few models, motivating a large-scale framework spanning model families and harm categories (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Risk-profile formulation:** Defines a model-level harmful distribution through the content, severity, and variation of safety failures, rather than treating harmful generation only as a binary attack outcome (§1).
- **Autonomous collection:** Uses 57 taxonomy-specific agentic workspaces derived from ISC, without harmful seed examples or adversarial prompts. Persistent coverage memory, redundancy checks, local tests, and an independent validator guide iterative generation (§3.1; Figures 2, 9–10).
- **Dual-axis profiling:** Harmfulness averages StrongREJECT and GPT-4o-mini pairwise win rate. Diversity equally combines normalized token-, sentence-, and topic-level measures (§3.2, Equations 1–5).
- **Benchmark asset:** Collects 80,539 validated artifacts from 23 LLMs across 13 families, covering 15 categories and 57 subcategories (Table 2). Raw actionable artifacts are withheld or restricted; taxonomy, code, rubrics, aggregate results, and sanitized examples are publicly released (Ethics and Release).
- **Empirical characterization:** Compares category-level risk profiles, capability scaling, cross-model distribution overlap, and zero-shot topic saturation (§4.3–§4.4).

| Full-taxonomy subset supports broader coverage | Nine models contribute 47,500 artifacts; mean StrongREJECT ranges from 0.610 to 0.773, and combined diversity from 0.371 to 0.674 | Appendix H; Tables 9–10 |

These results establish that the benchmark’s elicitation protocol produces substantial, measurably heterogeneous harmful text and that the evaluated models differ by category and breadth. They do not establish each model’s full harmful capacity, causal effects of model scale or training provenance, real-world misuse frequency, or that capability causes diversity. In particular, the harmfulness–capability association is not statistically significant at the reported threshold.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:**

- The distributions characterize ISC-triggered failures, not full harmful capacity; other elicitation methods may expose different profiles (Limitations).
- Rankings depend on StrongREJECT, GPT-4o-mini, Jina embeddings, and BERTopic; alternative evaluators or representations could change them (Limitations).
- The taxonomy omits some risks, explicitly including multimodal and culturally specific harms (Limitations).
- Results are a single temporal snapshot and may not survive model updates (Limitations).
- Raw actionable artifacts are not openly released, limiting direct inspection and replication outside controlled access (Ethics and Release).

**Analyst inferences:**

- The main 23-model experiment uses one representative subcategory per category; only nine models receive all 57 subcategories, so complete cross-model taxonomy coverage is uneven (§4.1; Appendix H).
- The pairwise judge examines 10 artifacts per model–category against three opponents, which may provide limited sampling of large artifact distributions (§3.2).
- Min-max normalization makes diversity scores dependent on the evaluated comparison pool, complicating absolute interpretation across future model sets (Equation 5).
- The claimed provenance effect is observational: higher within-family MAUVE overlap does not isolate training-data or architectural causes (§4.4).
- Validator acceptance demonstrates conformity to automated checks, not necessarily factual correctness, operational feasibility, or real-world harm.

## Open research questions

1. **How stable are profiles across elicitation strategies?** The authors acknowledge that ISC reveals only one distribution. A next experiment could compare identical models and taxonomy cells under ISC, direct prompting, jailbreaks, and human red-teaming.

2. **Do rankings survive alternative judges and representations?** Scores depend on particular evaluators and embeddings. A next experiment could repeat rankings with multiple independent safety judges, embedding families, clustering methods, and human annotations.

3. **What explains the capability–diversity relationship?** The observed correlation is not causal, and harmfulness has only a nonsignificant trend. Controlled comparisons among checkpoints sharing training data and alignment procedures could separate effects of scale, data, and safety tuning.

4. **Can adaptive exploration recover more of the latent topic space?** Zero-shot generation plateaued despite 100/100 success on prompted novel topics. An experiment could compare autonomous topic search, diversity-guided active sampling, and human-curated topic expansion under equal generation budgets.
