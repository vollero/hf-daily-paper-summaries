# R^3-Bench: LLMs Struggle with Resource-Rational Reasoning under Shared Budgets

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.16033) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16033)


## One-sentence takeaway

R³-Bench finds that frontier LLMs routinely fail to realize problem-solving competence demonstrated in isolation when six tasks compete for one shared computation budget, although its oracle measures offline empirical headroom rather than achievable online performance.

## Problem and motivation

Most reasoning and agent benchmarks give each problem an independent budget, so they cannot test whether a model allocates limited computation effectively across competing tasks. The authors argue that realistic workloads create opportunity costs: spending tokens or tool actions on one task leaves fewer resources for others. They address the missing evaluation of whether models can translate demonstrated single-problem competence into suite-level reward under shared budgets (Sections 1–3; Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Shared-budget benchmark:** R³-Bench constructs 50 six-problem contests in each of mathematics, competitive programming, and abstract reasoning. Every contest contains three Easy, two Medium, and one Hard problem, with difficulty determined by average standalone output length from three reference models (Sections 3.1; Figure 2).
- **Two evaluation regimes:** Tool-free models share an output-token budget; agents share a counted-action budget in an interactive shell. Strong and moderate pressure correspond to 20% and 80% of each model’s calibrated unbudgeted resource use (Sections 3.2–3.3; Appendix G).
- **Competence–allocation comparison:** Five independent single-problem runs at each of five budget levels define empirical response curves. These support an observed-cost equal-allocation replay and an offline multiple-choice-knapsack oracle constrained by the contest budget (Section 3.4; Appendix E).
- **Behavioral diagnostics:** Exhaustive single-annotator trajectory labeling examines coverage, strategy updates, and causes of oracle-selected misses (Section 3.4; Appendix K).
- **Scheduling intervention:** Two fixed online constraints—initial coverage and coverage plus a verification gate—test whether lightweight scheduling can recover performance under strong agentic pressure (Section 5; Appendix M).

The evidence establishes a widespread discrepancy between shared-budget contest performance and an offline replay of the same models’ sampled isolated-problem successes. It also associates the discrepancy with order effects, limited strategy revision, and pressure-dependent failure categories.

It does **not** establish that the oracle score is attainable by an executable scheduler, that allocation is the sole causal source of every gap, or that the fixed scheduling interventions generalize beyond the tested models, domains, and pressure setting.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated oracle limitation:** The oracle is optimal only over five sampled budget levels and five repeats per level. It inherits sampling error, may miss finer allocations, and is not a theoretical upper bound or executable policy (Appendix E).
- **Author-stated replay limitation:** Equal allocation uses the cheapest observed realized cost from attempts possibly launched with larger caps; it does not show that success would reproduce under a fresh hard cap of one-sixth of the budget (Appendix E).
- **Author-stated context diagnostic scope:** The target-in-suite experiment is a robustness diagnostic, not a causal decomposition, and covers only two models with explicit target cues and the largest single-problem budgets (Appendix O).
- **Annotation validity:** Although all 18,000 trajectories were annotated under an evidence-based rubric with model identity withheld, every trajectory received only one annotation; inter-annotator agreement is therefore unavailable (Appendix K).
- **Judging risk:** Mathematics relies on a model-based equivalence judge, while parser, artifact, or downstream judge failures receive zero. The supplied text reports no independent validation statistics for that judge (Appendices C–D).
- **Protocol asymmetry:** Thinking models receive an uncharged trace-only finalization stage, whereas models without exposed reasoning channels are evaluated in one stage and pay for final-answer tokens. A 300-call audit found no re-solving, but the interfaces remain structurally different (Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference — intervention attribution:** Scheduler trajectories are independent samples, and Strategies A and B combine constraints; thus Table 3 does not cleanly isolate the causal contribution of coverage versus verification (Section 5; Appendix N.4).
- **Internal reporting ambiguity:** The abstract says equal allocation exceeds moderate-pressure tool-free contests for three of six models, whereas Section 4.2 names four models. The supplied text does not resolve this discrepancy.

## Open research questions

1. **Can a learned online policy approach the empirical oracle without privileged response curves?** The fixed directives win only in some domains. A next experiment could train a policy using visible progress, feedback quality, and remaining budget, then evaluate it on held-out models and suites.

2. **How stable are oracle gaps under denser sampling?** Five repeats at five budget levels leave sampling uncertainty. A next experiment could increase repetitions and budget-grid resolution, reporting confidence intervals and oracle-selection stability.

3. **Which runtime signals make cross-task probing worthwhile?** Both schedulers help Code consistently but can hurt mathematics and abstract reasoning. A next experiment could manipulate feedback availability within each domain and measure the marginal value of probing.

4. **Does shared-budget behavior transfer to larger or heterogeneous workloads?** Evaluation is restricted to homogeneous six-problem suites. A next experiment could vary suite size and mix domains while preserving matched isolated-problem controls.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported benchmark design and descriptive results because the retrieved text includes the full paper, main tables, and methodological appendices.

Material uncertainties remain: no confidence intervals for most headline comparisons; no inter-annotator agreement because trajectories were singly annotated; no independent accuracy study for the mathematics equivalence judge; no resolved explanation for the three-versus-four equal-allocation discrepancy; and no direct evaluation of a learned, transferable allocation policy.
