# Understanding Cognition-Induced Risks in Agentic AI Systems

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.15304) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.15304)


## One-sentence takeaway

This survey organizes agentic-AI risks by expanding cognitive scope—physical, social, and self-referential cognition—and argues that these levels respectively threaten human agency, autonomy, and control while motivating corresponding safeguards.

## Problem and motivation

Agentic LLM systems increasingly participate in open-ended reasoning, planning, communication, and consequential human workflows. The authors argue that conventional task-bounded safety analysis insufficiently addresses the human and societal risks created as agents engage with the environment, other actors, and their own internal states. They therefore examine how expanding cognitive scope may erode human agency, autonomy, and control (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Risk taxonomy:** A three-level framework comprising physical cognition, social cognition, and self-referential cognition, ordered from environmental processing to a self-inclusive representation of the world (Figure 1; pp. 1–2).
- **Risk synthesis:** A literature-based mapping from those levels to risks involving human agency, human autonomy, and human control, respectively (Sections 3–5, pp. 2–7).
- **Mitigation agenda:** Strategies including AI-content detection, containment, complementary human–AI collaboration, depersonalization, access restrictions, multi-level safeguards, exclusion of survival-oriented objectives, metacognition monitoring, and human oversight (pp. 3–7).
- **Consciousness discussion:** An interpretive application of the C0–C1–C2 taxonomy, under which the authors characterize current agents as primarily C0 with primitive C1-like functionality and no evidence of genuine C2 self-awareness (p. 6).

This is a conceptual survey rather than a new experimental study, model, or benchmark.

The evidence supports a broad taxonomy grounded in diverse prior observations, and identifies recurring human-centered risk signals. It does not validate the taxonomy through original experiments, establish prevalence across deployed systems, quantify most effects, prove causality for reported associations, or show that the proposed mitigations work together in practice.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:**

- The scope excludes performance, robustness, and bias risks, concentrating on risks arising from cognitive engagement (p. 7).
- The review primarily uses publicly available literature and may miss industrial practices and internal reports (p. 7).
- Reference limits prevented exhaustive coverage; the authors selected at least one representative work for each risk and mitigation category (p. 7).

**Analyst inferences supported by the text:**

- Evidence strength is heterogeneous: the synthesis combines participant studies, behavioral agent evaluations, conceptual arguments, corporate reports, news reporting, and a Wikipedia reference. The paper provides no formal quality assessment of these sources.
- Many claims omit models, experimental controls, effect sizes, uncertainty, and detailed settings, limiting independent interpretation from this paper alone.
- No systematic search procedure, inclusion criteria, evidence-coding method, or framework-validation study is reported.
- Several proposed mitigations are recommendations rather than interventions evaluated by this paper.
- The progression from physical to social to self-referential cognition is conceptually motivated, but the text does not establish that the levels are exhaustive, mutually exclusive, or empirically ordered.
- The consciousness discussion depends on functional analogy and explicitly acknowledges that current self-reports may imitate language rather than demonstrate self-identity (pp. 5–6).

## Open research questions

1. **Can the three cognitive levels be measured reliably?** The paper defines them conceptually but supplies no operational criteria. A next experiment could develop behavioral and representation-level measures for each level and test their discriminant validity across multiple agents.

2. **Do the proposed safeguards reduce risk without merely relocating it?** Most mitigations are not evaluated here. A controlled agent study could compare sandboxing, response auditing, permission limits, and human checkpoints—individually and jointly—on task success, policy violations, and unauthorized resource access.

3. **Which patterns of human–LLM use cause lasting changes in agency or autonomy?** Several cited findings are associative or incompletely specified. A longitudinal randomized study could vary interaction duration, task type, anthropomorphism, and required human deliberation while measuring independent reasoning and offline social behavior.

4. **Can metacognitive monitoring distinguish functional self-representation from deceptive or imitative self-description?** The paper identifies this ambiguity but offers no validated detector. A next experiment could compare verbal self-reports with internal-state probes and out-of-distribution behavioral tests under controlled interventions.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text is complete enough to characterize the paper’s framework, claims, scope, and recommendations, but many empirical details reside only in cited sources.

Materially unavailable information includes the review’s search and selection protocol, source-quality assessment, complete experimental settings behind cited findings, most effect sizes and uncertainty estimates, operational definitions for cognitive levels, ablations or validation of the taxonomy, and empirical evaluation of the proposed mitigation strategies.
