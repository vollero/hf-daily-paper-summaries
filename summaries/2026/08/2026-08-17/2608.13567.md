# Modular Cognitive Architecture Emerges in Large Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-17.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.13567) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13567)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across 46 tasks and six instruction-tuned LLMs, the authors find that strongly task-attributed MLP neurons overlap and causally transfer disruption much more within four neuroscience-motivated cognitive domains than across them, supporting—but not proving—a brain-like modular organization.

## Problem and motivation

The paper asks whether functional specialization—distinct systems for language, formal reasoning, social reasoning, and physical reasoning—is peculiar to biological brains or can independently emerge in artificial systems optimized for next-token prediction. Existing LLM studies had identified specialized neurons or broad language-versus-reasoning divisions, but had not jointly tested fine-grained overlap, segregation, and causal relevance across multiple reasoning domains (Introduction, pp. 3–4).

This matters because convergence between systems shaped by different optimization pressures could indicate that modularity is a useful computational organization rather than solely a consequence of biological constraints. The stronger claim that modularity is a fundamental property of intelligence remains an author interpretation, not something directly established by these experiments (Discussion, pp. 10–12).

## Approach and contributions

- **Large, cognition-structured task battery:** The authors assemble 46 next-token-prediction tasks: 8 language, 20 formal-reasoning, 9 physical-reasoning, and 9 social-reasoning tasks. Each uses minimally different input pairs whose correct continuations differ (SI §§1.1, 2, pp. 19–34).

- **Neuron-level circuit localization:** For every task, attribution patching scores each feed-forward MLP neuron using its activation difference between the original and alternative inputs multiplied by the gradient of the output logit difference. The main analysis selects the top 0.1% of positively attributed neurons (Figure 1; SI §§1.3–1.4, pp. 21–23).

- **Modularity measurements:** Pairwise Jaccard overlap tests whether task-critical neuron sets are more shared within neuroscience-defined domains than across domains. Hierarchical clustering separately tests whether the overlap matrix recovers the four predefined groups (SI §1.4).

- **Causal validation:** Across 2,070 ordered source–target task pairs excluding the diagonal, source-task neurons are replaced by their target task’s alternative-input activations; the resulting target-task accuracy drop measures shared causal dependence (Results, pp. 8–10; SI §1.5).

- **Cross-model and robustness analysis:** Experiments cover six public-weight instruction-tuned models from four families and 24B–123B parameters, with controls for attribution thresholds, ablation methods, semantic prompt similarity, and a low-performing GPT-2 baseline (SI §§4–10).

Together, these results establish a robust association between the authors’ four task-domain labels and both neuron-set overlap and intervention sensitivity in the tested models. The interventions strengthen the case beyond purely correlational activation similarity.

They do not establish that LLM modules are anatomically or computationally identical to human brain networks, that modularity caused the models’ capabilities, or that next-token training necessarily produces this organization. No LLM-to-human neural-response comparison is conducted here; the alignment is based on task groupings motivated by prior neuroscience.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated mapping uncertainty:** Many tasks have not been directly tested with human neural measurements. Their assignment to a brain network is therefore a “well-motivated hypothesis,” especially for broad social-reasoning mappings (SI §1.1, pp. 20–21).

- **Performance-conditioned sample:** Items are retained only when a model answers both minimal-pair variants correctly, and tasks require at least 60% both-correct performance for inclusion. This is appropriate for localizing successful computation but limits conclusions about the model’s behavior on errors or the original unfiltered distributions (SI §1.1).

- **Restricted model population:** The main models are all large, autoregressive, instruction-tuned LLMs with 24B–123B parameters. Base models, smaller capable models, multimodal systems, and different architectures are not systematically compared (SI §1.2).

- **Restricted circuit definition:** Analysis focuses on feed-forward MLP neurons evaluated at the final prompt token. Attention heads, edges, other token positions, and distributed representations outside the selected top-positive neurons are not included (SI §1.3).

- **Threshold dependence in effect size:** Significance persists from top 0.05% to 5%, but within/cross overlap falls from 5.4× to 1.4× as the neuron set expands. Thus, the strongest modularity is concentrated near the top of the attribution ranking (SI §5.1).

- **Uneven robustness coverage:** Threshold and ablation-method robustness for causal effects is reported only for Qwen2.5-32B, not all six models (SI §§5.2, 6).

- **Weaker social-domain causal evidence:** Both directional social-reasoning ablation tests miss the stated significance threshold despite having the predicted direction (SI §4.2).

- **Analyst inference—causal origin remains unresolved:** Ablation shows that localized neurons contribute selectively to performance; it does not test whether modularity prevents interference, improves learning, or arose for the reasons proposed in the Discussion.

- **Analyst inference—asset reproducibility is not fully assessable:** The text says code, data, and full prompt configurations are available, but it does not provide repository details, licenses, compute requirements, random seeds, or complete numerical tables in the retrieved content.

## Open research questions

1. **Does modularity cause better learning or generalization?**  
   The paper observes modularity only after training and proposes interference reduction as an explanation. A next experiment could train matched models with regularizers that promote, leave unconstrained, or discourage domain segregation, then compare generalization and cross-domain interference.

2. **When does the organization emerge during training?**  
   Only final instruction-tuned checkpoints are analyzed, with GPT-2 serving as a low-capability comparison rather than a developmental trajectory. Applying the same battery to successive checkpoints of one model family could relate capability acquisition to changes in overlap and ablation specificity.

3. **Do LLM-derived modules predict human brain responses task by task?**  
   Many task-to-network assignments are hypotheses rather than direct neural mappings. An fMRI experiment using the same task items could test whether LLM overlap predicts cross-task response similarity or network recruitment in humans.

4. **How do specialized systems exchange information?**  
   The study localizes MLP neurons but does not trace communication between language and reasoning systems. Edge-level or token-resolved causal tracing on tasks requiring multiple domains could test the proposed early-language-to-later-reasoning hierarchy.
