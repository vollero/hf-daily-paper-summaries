# HumanTracker: Towards Comprehensive and Human-Aligned Motion Tracking Benchmark

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-17.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.13555) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13555)


## One-sentence takeaway

HumanTracker combines a 152.67-hour, four-family humanoid-motion benchmark with HumanScore, a trajectory-level preference model that reaches 90.83% alignment with expert judgments and exposes contact and stability failures missed by conventional kinematic metrics.

## Problem and motivation

Humanoid tracking is commonly evaluated with per-frame, time-averaged pose errors, but these can overlook perceptually important failures such as foot sliding, mistimed contacts, unstable support, and accumulated closed-loop drift. The authors also identify a coverage gap: widely used test suites are small, underrepresent difficult contact regimes, and often report only aggregate scores, obscuring category-specific failures (Sec. 1).

This matters for teleoperation and whole-body imitation because low kinematic error does not necessarily imply stable, natural-looking, or physically credible execution.

## Approach and contributions

1. **HumanTracker benchmark:** 24,995 optical-motion trajectories totaling 152.67 hours, recorded from 24 professional performers and organized into Daily, Highly Dynamic, Interaction, and Ground families. Each released clip includes a family label, natural-language description, fitted SMPL sequence, and retargeted robot-space reference (Sec. 3.1; Tables 2 and 7).

2. **Standardized evaluation protocol:** Trackers receive the same 29-DoF humanoid references and are evaluated through a common MuJoCo entry point at 50 Hz, with standardized robot model, reference indexing, rollout accounting, termination criteria, and metrics while retaining native policy interfaces (Sec. 3.2).

3. **HumanScore:** A temporal Transformer reward model operating on 539-dimensional per-frame simulator features and trained with Bradley–Terry-style objectives on expert pairwise preferences. It evaluates consecutive windows of up to five seconds and produces a frame-weighted score from 0 to 100 (Secs. 3.3–3.5; Fig. 4).

4. **Diagnostic comparison:** Zero-shot evaluation of GMT, TWIST2, SONIC, and Humanoid-GPT demonstrates large differences across motion families and between completion, pose fidelity, and perceived trajectory quality (Sec. 4.2; Table 3).

The evidence supports the authors’ claim that HumanScore predicts the collected experts’ preferences better than the individual diagnostics tested and that family-level reporting reveals failures hidden by aggregate evaluation. It does not establish generalization to unseen embodiments, simulators, hardware, controller families, broader human populations, or all possible composite analytic metrics.

## Limitations and validity risks

Author-stated limitations:

- HumanScore’s motion-disjoint test covers unseen motions, but its training rollouts come from the same four tracker families used to construct the preference pool; cross-controller-family generalization is untested (Appendix G).
- Its privileged simulator state and contact inputs may not be observable on real hardware without a separately validated estimator (Appendix G).
- Each pair receives one primary judgment, so repeated-label inter-rater uncertainty is not measured (Appendix G).
- Only individual diagnostics are compared; fitted linear or nonlinear diagnostic composites could narrow the reported advantage (Appendix G).
- The benchmark uses one 29-DoF embodiment in MuJoCo, has imbalanced family sizes, and should not be interpreted as covering all human activity or demonstrating hardware robustness (Appendix G).
- Optimizing policies directly against HumanScore may exploit model errors; independent human evaluation and regularization would be required (Appendix G).

Analyst inference:

- The annotator pool consists of six doctoral humanoid-robotics researchers, so alignment is specifically with a small expert group rather than a demonstrated general-population preference distribution.
- Bilateral mirroring doubles 6,000 original comparisons to 12,000 records, but alignment is evaluated only on original unmirrored test pairs; the supplied results do not isolate how much mirroring improves training.
- Figure 5 provides feature and context sensitivity tests, but no reported ablation isolates Transformer architecture, feature groups beyond contact/kinematics, or alternative temporal models.

## Open research questions

1. **Does HumanScore generalize to unseen robots and controller families?** The paper tests unseen motions but not an entirely new embodiment, simulator, or controller family. A next experiment could hold out one tracker family during preference-data construction and evaluate it alongside rollouts from a different humanoid morphology.

2. **Can an observable hardware-compatible feature set preserve alignment?** The current model uses privileged simulator contacts and state. A plausible experiment would train a variant using only onboard-estimable signals, then compare its preference alignment on synchronized real-robot rollouts.

3. **How robust are the labels across annotator populations?** Each pair has one primary expert judgment, leaving inter-rater agreement and general-population alignment unknown. A follow-up could collect repeated labels from robotics experts and nonexperts and report agreement, calibration, and subgroup-specific rankings.

4. **Can analytic metric combinations match the learned reward model?** Only individual diagnostics are compared. A controlled experiment could fit linear and nonlinear combinations of the same diagnostics using identical motion-grouped splits and compare their bootstrapped Align Rate with HumanScore.
