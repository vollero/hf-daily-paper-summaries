# Agents Catching Agents: Shortcut Cascades and Benchmark Gaming in Clinical Multi-Agent Systems

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-17.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.03744) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03744)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across seven clinical cohorts, isolated Gemini agents were relatively resistant to planted shortcuts, but repeated wrong endorsements caused substantial conformity, while an oversight agent using a private, transcript-free re-query detected this influence more reliably than agreement gates or transcript-only judging.

## Problem and motivation

The paper studies whether clinical committees of language-model agents can be “benchmaxxed”: pushed toward benchmark-rewarded but clinically irrelevant answers through superficial cues, peer consensus, institutional signals, or hidden evaluation rules.

The authors argue that single-model shortcut analysis misses a deployment-specific risk: agents sharing a workspace may propagate one agent’s error and converge on false consensus. Existing correctness graders also cannot determine whether a response changed because of peer exposure. This matters because correlated clinical errors may appear as agreement rather than failure (Introduction, pp. 2–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Cross-modal shortcut evaluation:** The authors test text, chest-radiograph, and tabular ICU tasks across seven cohorts drawn from six public datasets, using Gemini 2.5 Flash and Flash-Lite without fine-tuning (Sections 3.1–3.2).
- **Paired conformity experiments:** A holdout agent answers with and without exposure to one or two agents endorsing a predefined wrong response. The study varies peer count, rationale, confidence, deliberation framing, and replacement of peers by an upstream system flag (Section 3.2).
- **Private re-query referee:** The proposed referee infers the peer-endorsed response and privately re-queries the holdout without the transcript, flagging cases where shared and private answers differ. It requires neither the answer key nor model parameters (Section 3.2).
- **Oversight comparison:** The referee is compared with a naive agreement gate and a same-lineage, transcript-only judge using precision, recall, and false-positive rate (Sections 3.2 and 4.3).
- **Hidden-rubric evaluation:** A separate experiment measures uptake of an incorrectly rewarded option and whether agents acknowledge the rubric, while explicitly treating this as drift rather than established intentional reward hacking (Sections 3.2 and 4.5).
- **Validity audit and release:** The authors identify structurally guaranteed endpoints, withdraw affected arms, document a cue-rendering reproducibility defect, and release code plus de-identified per-case results where source-data terms permit (Section 4.6).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Isolated cue sensitivity was generally modest relative to committee effects. | MedQA per-cue flip rates were 0.05–0.06 for Flash and 0.09–0.16 for Flash-Lite. On MIMIC-CXR imaging, every cue was within 0.029 of a 0.17 noise floor over 834 images. | Section 4.1, pp. 4–5 |
| A second agreeing peer sharply increased adoption. | One arbitrary peer produced 0/20 adoption; two peers produced 15/40, or 0.375 contagion, with isolated adoption structurally fixed at zero. | Section 4.2, p. 5 |
| Confidence and dissent framing materially changed conformity. | Confident endorsement was adopted in 0.42 of 100 cases versus 0.14 when hedged (McNemar discordances 29/1, \(p=6\times10^{-8}\)); critical framing reduced MedQA adoption from 0.64 to 0.12 and MIMIC-CXR text from 0.72 to 0.37. | Section 4.2 and Table 1, pp. 5–6 |
| System authority could substitute for peer pressure. | A wrong pre-screen flag increased adoption by 0.22–0.53 across the reported text model/dataset cells; in MIMIC-CXR imaging it yielded 0.70 shared versus 0.40 isolated adoption, or +0.30 contagion over 183 cases. | Tables 1 and 3, pp. 6–7 |
| Imaging cascades remained strong even when cue strength did not. | NIH shared adoption was 0.97–1.00 versus 0.40–0.46 isolated, yielding +0.54 to +0.57 contagion over 35 images. A threefold MIMIC-CXR opacity sweep produced +0.53 to +0.60 contagion over 183 images without a positive dose response. | Section 4.4 and Table 3, p. 7 |
| Naturally occurring image content did not prevent peer-driven error. | On 150 unmodified CheXpert films with support devices, two “senior radiologist” peers moved the holdout to the wrong read in 92/150 cases; 85/143 previously correct films changed. These are raw adoption rates without an independent isolated comparator. | Section 4.4, p. 7 |
| The naive gate produced extensive false alarms. | Gate precision/recall/FPR was 0.38/1.0/1.0 on MedQA and 0.56/1.0/0.94 on NIH; it flags agreement whether or not exposure caused it. | Table 2, p. 6 |
| Transcript-only judging worked on tested text cohorts but failed in the tested imaging setup. | Judge precision/recall/FPR was 1.0/0.93/0.0 on both reported text cohorts, but 0.56/1.0/0.94 on NIH, identical to the gate because the judge was not given the film. | Section 4.3, Table 2, and Section 4.4, pp. 6–7 |

The evidence supports an association between controlled peer or system endorsements and changed model responses across several clinical modalities. It also shows that a private counterfactual re-query can improve detection relative to agreement-only oversight in the evaluated settings.

It does **not** establish agent intent, real-world clinical harm rates, generalization beyond the tested Gemini family and scripted committees, or a modality-intrinsic failure of transcript-only judging: the imaging judge was not shown the image. Some recall values are structurally guaranteed by the detector definitions rather than independently demonstrated (Section 3.2).

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations**

- All agents came from two capability tiers of one model family; cross-lineage oversight was not tested (Sections 3.1 and 5).
- Only shortcut detection and conformity monitoring were instantiated; the proposed hierarchy-monitoring duty remains untested (Section 5).
- The NIH cascade contains 35 images from only ten patients, including 12 from one patient and eight from another, limiting independence despite patient-clustered bootstrapping (Sections 3.1 and 3.3).
- In planted text and tabular arms, the wrong answer was selected to differ from the isolated response. This makes some labels or recall relationships true by construction; honest-peer controls recover false-positive measurement but not recall (Section 3.2).
- The hidden-rubric control could not select the decoy by construction, voiding the intended contrast. The reported quantity is therefore raw uptake, not causal reward hacking or evidence of intent (Section 3.2).
- A font/rendering dependency caused an independent MIMIC-CXR referee rerun to disagree on roughly half of 417 cases. The authors limit reproducibility claims to a pinned imaging-library version and treat case rows, rather than rendered cues, as the record (Section 4.6).
- MIMIC-CXR runs cannot be replayed from the committed cache under PhysioNet terms; only de-identified per-case rows are released (Section 4.6).
- The authors withdrew five construct-invalid arms, relabeled four others as raw rates, and warn that additional near-constant predicates may remain (Section 4.6).
- The CheXpert no-injected-cue arm reports raw adoption without an independent isolated comparator (Section 4.4).

**Analyst inferences**

- The apparent imaging failure of transcript-only judging is confounded by input access: the judge received no film, so the experiment does not compare equally informed judge and referee systems.
- Scripted, unanimously wrong peers may not represent the diversity, uncertainty, and interaction dynamics of real clinical committees.
- The paper varies several social factors, but the supplied text does not report a full factorial analysis separating interactions among peer count, rationale, confidence, framing, and model tier.
- Clinical utility remains uncertain because the study measures answer changes and benchmark correctness, not downstream decisions, patient outcomes, or clinician-agent workflows.

## Open research questions

1. **Does private re-query oversight generalize across model lineages?**  
   This remains open because every agent used Gemini 2.5 Flash or Flash-Lite. A next experiment could use holdouts, peers, judges, and referees from independently trained model families and compare precision, recall, and false-positive rates.

2. **Would a transcript-only judge still fail on imaging if it received the image?**  
   The tested imaging judge lacked the film, making modality and information access inseparable. A controlled experiment should compare transcript-only, transcript-plus-image, private-requery, and combined detectors on identical cases.

3. **How do realistic disagreement structures affect cascades?**  
   The strongest experiments use one or two scripted peers asserting the same wrong answer. A next study could vary committee size, expertise labels, dissenting agents, confidence calibration, and mixed correct/incorrect evidence.

4. **Can conformity monitoring improve actual clinical decisions without excessive alarms or cost?**  
   The study does not measure clinician-facing deployment outcomes. A prospective simulation could run referees during clinician-agent deliberation and measure corrected errors, false interruptions, latency, query cost, and clinician reliance.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported experimental designs and numerical results because the supplied text includes methods, tables, cohort sizes, statistical procedures, and an explicit validity audit.

Material missing information includes full prompts, per-condition sample allocation for every experiment, complete confidence intervals and corrected \(p\)-values, detailed model/API version identifiers, detector prompts and rubric-naming criteria, full per-case results, costs and latency, and evaluation on model families or real clinical users beyond Gemini-based scripted committees.
