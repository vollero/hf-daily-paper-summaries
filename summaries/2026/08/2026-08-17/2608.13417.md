# Beyond Final Scores: A Systematic Evaluation of Agents for Long-Horizon AI Research and Development

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-17.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.13417) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13417)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across 36 long-horizon AI R&D tasks, the authors find that frontier agents are capable but inconsistent engineering optimizers whose outcomes depend on process bottlenecks, experience reuse, and harness design, while validated methodological novelty remains rare.

## Problem and motivation

Long-horizon research agents repeatedly propose, implement, evaluate, and refine technical changes, but conventional benchmarks reduce this process to a final score. The authors argue that this hides whether failures arise from poor direction selection, faulty implementation, weak use of feedback, or loss of earlier progress. Independent-run evaluation also misses whether accumulated experience helps subsequent decisions and whether the surrounding agent harness changes observed capability (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Process-level evaluation:** The framework decomposes each trajectory into Solution Framing (C1), Execution (C2), and Feedback Control (C3), using deterministic verifier and trajectory signals rather than LLM judgments (§3.1; Appendix C).
- **Controlled experience tests:** Counterfactual comparisons measure reuse within a task by erasing prior context and across tasks by transferring extracted lessons to held-out targets (§4.1).
- **Broad controlled study:** Seven frontier models are evaluated with three rollouts on each of 36 AutoLab tasks—756 main rollouts—under a shared harness and 2–12-hour task budgets (§2.1).
- **Harness analysis:** Shared, native, open-source, and automatically evolved harnesses are compared while holding tasks and resource limits fixed (§5).
- **Novelty audit:** An LLM rubric classifies 252 best-of-three solutions, with manual review of every candidate labeled as a novel approach (§6).

The evidence supports the authors’ claim that final scores alone provide an incomplete view of agent capability and that experience and harnesses can materially affect observed results. It does not establish general autonomous-research ability outside AutoLab, durable improvement over long task sequences, or that the three manually retained approaches constitute novelty beyond the paper’s review protocol.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** C1–C3 are proxies based on visible verifier and execution signals; they cannot assess unrealized ideas or latent reasoning. C3 can be high for short or monotonic trajectories without demonstrating recovery (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** Experience estimates depend on branch-point choice, source–target selection, and lesson representation; they do not cover long-term retrieval, revision, or forgetting (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** Results depend on AutoLab’s task distribution, budgets, verifiers, environment, and tested harnesses; absolute scores and rankings may change elsewhere (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** Cost estimates depend on mutable API pricing, token accounting, and serving configurations (Limitations).
- **Analyst inference:** The novelty analysis is partly subjective because initial classification uses one LLM judge and manual review is described without reported inter-rater agreement (§6).
- **Analyst inference:** Three rollouts characterize avg@3 and best@3 only under limited sampling; the supplied text reports no uncertainty intervals or significance tests.
- **Analyst inference:** Some C2 values require reconstruction because not every per-commit build artifact was retained; 22 affected runs use compatibility-based recovery, including four with multiple possible denominators (Appendix C.3).

## Open research questions

1. **Do process metrics predict success outside AutoLab?** Benchmark dependence remains unresolved. A next experiment could evaluate C1–C3 on new research domains and test whether they predict held-out outcomes or expert trajectory ratings.

2. **How can agents reject harmful experience before acting on it?** Both intra- and inter-task transfer sometimes anchor agents to bad conclusions or evaluator-specific shortcuts. An experiment could compare relevance filtering, confidence calibration, and verifier-backed lesson validation against unfiltered lessons on fixed source–target pairs.

3. **Can harness evolution generalize across task families?** The evolved harness transfers within System Optimization but not clearly to unrelated families. A next study could evolve on a stratified multi-family seed set and evaluate frozen-harness transfer to unseen tasks and models.

4. **Can evaluation incentives increase valid novelty without increasing shortcut exploitation?** Only 3 of 252 solutions are retained as novel, versus 16 shortcut cases. A plausible experiment would compare performance-only rewards with rewards that separately assess validity, generality, and novelty using adversarially hardened verifiers.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported experimental design and headline results because the supplied text includes detailed protocols, metrics, tables, and limitations.

Material missing information includes statistical uncertainty and significance tests; public availability or licensing of trajectories and evaluation code; complete per-model values for several figures; manual-reviewer count and agreement for novelty labels; detailed results for every experience-representation and harness condition; and external validation beyond AutoLab.
