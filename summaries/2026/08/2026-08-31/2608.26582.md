# J-Zero: Unified Challenger--Solver--Judge Co-Evolution from Zero Data

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-31.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.26582) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26582)


## One-sentence takeaway

J-Zero co-trains task generation, response solving, and preference evaluation without external task data, producing sustained gains on both objectively scored and open-ended language benchmarks.

## Problem and motivation

Zero-data self-play can improve language models in verifiable domains because objective answers supply dependable rewards, but open-ended tasks require a learned Judge. The authors argue that a frozen Judge eventually stops distinguishing improvements beyond its existing evaluation capability, causing self-play to plateau. The identified gap is a closed-loop method in which the evaluator itself improves without deriving preference labels from its own scores or external supervision (§1–§2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Three-role co-evolution:** A Challenger generates difficult tasks, a Solver learns to answer them, and a Judge co-adapts to evaluate their evolving outputs (Figure 1).
- **Adversarial policy training:** Challenger and Solver are alternately optimized with GRPO. Challenger rewards combine Solver difficulty, task-diversity penalties, and format validation; Solver training selects tasks with high response-score dispersion (§3.1, Equations 1–9).
- **Judge-independent preference construction:** The Judge is trained with Bradley–Terry loss on two structurally ordered pair types (§3.2, Equations 10–13):
  - *Role asymmetry:* prefer the answer-oriented Solver’s response over the Challenger’s response.
  - *Subtask amplification:* prefer a decomposed-and-recombined response over the Solver’s one-shot response.
- **Unified empirical evaluation:** The method is tested at two model scales on 11 verifiable and three unverifiable benchmarks, with ablations and iteration-level analysis (§4–§5).

The results establish that, under the reported checkpoint-selection rules and model sizes, J-Zero outperformed the selected zero-data baselines and that Judge adaptation accounts for part of the gain. They do not establish indefinite self-improvement, superiority over methods requiring external supervision, or robustness beyond the evaluated models and benchmarks. Open-ended results also depend on automatic judges rather than direct human evaluation.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Experiments cover only base Challenger/Solver models up to 8B and an 8B Judge; larger models and post-trained reasoning models with long chains of thought remain untested (§6).
- **Author-stated:** The Judge is an off-the-shelf classifier-based reward model trained with BT loss, so the system is not initialized entirely from one generative model (§6).
- **Author-stated:** Subtask-amplification labels are unreliable early: the intended response wins only 21.1% at iteration 1 and remains below half for three iterations (§5.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Role-asymmetry and amplification provide heuristic rather than guaranteed ordering. Their external validation uses another LLM judge, not human labels, and ties are discarded (§5.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Training stops when a domain average begins to decline and reports the preceding checkpoint; this selection rule may favor methods differently and does not reveal variance across independent runs (§4.1). Run counts, uncertainty intervals, and significance tests are not supplied.
- **Analyst inference:** Automatic judges evaluate all three unverifiable benchmarks, introducing evaluator dependence despite the use of official protocols otherwise (Appendix A.1).
- **Analyst inference:** The ablation isolates Judge freezing and removal of each preference source, but the supplied text does not report ablations for score-dispersion filtering, decomposition/composition quality, pair proportions, or Challenger reward components.

## Open research questions

1. **Does improvement continue beyond ten iterations?** J-Zero had not plateaued within the reported budget, but longer-term stability is unknown. Run substantially longer trials while tracking downstream scores, Judge calibration, task diversity, and reward exploitation.

2. **Can unreliable early amplification labels be filtered or scheduled?** Their chosen side loses most comparisons during the first three iterations. Compare fixed equal mixing against delayed introduction, confidence filtering, and iteration-dependent pair weights.

3. **Does the method scale to stronger or differently trained models?** Only 4B/8B base policies and an 8B discriminative Judge were tested. Repeat the controlled comparison with larger models and post-trained reasoning policies, including compute-normalized results.

4. **Can a generative Judge co-evolve safely?** The authors identify this as necessary for realizing all three roles from one generative initialization. Compare classifier and generative Judges using ranking accuracy, critique usefulness, downstream performance, and susceptibility to reward hacking.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the method description and reported results because the supplied text includes the main equations, tables, appendices, and limitations.

Material missing information includes independent-run variance, confidence intervals or significance tests, full numerical trajectories from Figures 2–4, exact generated training data, code/model release status, human evaluation of open-ended outputs and constructed preferences, and experiments beyond ten iterations or 8B scale.
