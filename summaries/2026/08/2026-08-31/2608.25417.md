# Paint What You See: Benchmarking Dexterous Visual Tool Use in Multimodal Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-31.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.25417) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25417)


## One-sentence takeaway

EASEL shows that current multimodal agents can make coarse initial visual edits but struggle to sustain precise, feedback-driven tool actions, while trajectory supervision improves reconstruction modestly without resolving long-horizon correction failures.

## Problem and motivation

The paper studies “dexterous visual tool use”: repeatedly translating fine-grained visual evidence into explicit action parameters that directly determine an evolving visual result. Existing agent benchmarks often tolerate imprecise clicks, test visual operations without persistent output, or delegate execution to specialized segmentation or generation models; consequently, they do not isolate precision-critical, closed-loop control (Introduction; Figure 1).

This matters for applications where small parameter errors directly affect the outcome and later actions must respond to those errors. The authors use painting as a scalable proxy because every stroke changes the observable state, references require no manual annotation, and trajectories can be generated automatically (Section 1, pages 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **EASEL benchmark:** A controlled 2D environment whose primary task is incremental reference-image reconstruction. It contains 110 reconstruction samples across four categories and five semantic tasks covering region outlining, handwriting, and constrained path planning (Sections 3.1–3.2; Table 1).

- **Parameterized closed-loop interface:** At every step, an agent observes the target and current canvas, then emits a quadratic Bézier brush stroke. Reconstruction exposes 13 continuous path and appearance parameters; semantic tasks fix appearance and retain six path coordinates (Sections 3.1 and 3.4; Figure 2).

- **Result and trajectory evaluation:** Final outputs are scored alongside intermediate behavior using Final Similarity, Similarity@50%, trajectory AUC, Best Similarity, and Final–Best Gap. Semantic tasks use task-specific outline, recognition, and path-progress metrics (Section 3.3).

- **EASEL-Data and EASEL-9B:** The authors convert 11,000 automatically generated 250-step painting trajectories into 440,000 next-action examples, divided into early-reconstruction and completion curricula; 44,000 examples include generated chain-of-thought supervision. They fine-tune Qwen3.5-9B to test whether this supervision improves closed-loop action (Section 4; Figure 3).

- **Broad model study:** Twenty-five multimodal agents are evaluated, revealing early saturation, post-peak degradation, and task-specific failures that final reconstruction scores alone obscure (Sections 5.1–5.2).

These results establish that the tested agents perform poorly under EASEL’s particular canvas, tool schema, budgets, and metrics, and that early-trajectory supervision improves one 9B base model. They do not establish that painting performance transfers to real software, robotics, or the motivating medical/CAD applications. Nor do they isolate whether the dominant bottleneck is visual perception, planning, action quantization, rendering dynamics, or context management.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope limitation:** EASEL trades breadth for experimental control and uses a 2D canvas as a proxy for broader dexterous visual action (Section 6). Proxy validity outside this environment is therefore unresolved.

- **Author-stated remaining weakness:** Even after curriculum training, long-horizon feedback correction remains limited; most improvement comes from early-stage supervision (Sections 5.1 and 7).

- **Analyst inference — narrow semantic sample:** Each semantic capability is represented by only one or two tasks, for five tasks total, so broad claims about annotation, handwriting, or planning should be treated cautiously (Section 3.2; Table 1).

- **Analyst inference — selective semantic reporting:** Table 4 includes only five models with “nontrivial and differentiated” behavior; near-zero models are omitted. This prevents a complete 25-model semantic comparison (Section 5.2).

- **Analyst inference — metric dependence:** The primary similarity is a hand-weighted combination of SSIM, RGB L1, and Edge IoU. The text says alternative-weight sensitivity appears in supplementary material, but those results are unavailable here (Section 3.3).

- **Analyst inference — limited training ablation:** The reported curriculum comparison covers the base, C1-only, and C1+C2 variants, but does not isolate C2-only training, the contribution of the 44,000 chain-of-thought samples, coordinate quantization, or individual fine-tuning choices (Sections 4–5; Table 3).

- **Analyst inference — generated evaluation content:** Most nonprocedural benchmark categories are generated with GPT-image-2. The supplied text does not analyze generator artifacts, content diversity, or possible model familiarity with this visual distribution (Section 3.2).

## Open research questions

1. **What prevents sustained improvement after the first few actions?** The trajectories show early plateaus and degradation but do not causally separate perception, planning, memory, and action-control errors. A next experiment could provide oracle actions or oracle state summaries one component at a time and measure which intervention extends improvement.

2. **Which curriculum ingredients produce the observed 6.3% relative gain?** C1 explains most of the improvement, but the effects of C2, chain-of-thought supervision, replay, and coordinate quantization are not fully isolated. A factorial ablation could vary each component while holding training volume constant.

3. **Does EASEL performance predict success in broader precision-critical environments?** The paper motivates medical annotation, CAD, diagram authoring, and embodied manipulation without evaluating them. A follow-up could correlate EASEL trajectory metrics with performance on matched tasks in real editing software or embodied simulators.

4. **Can agents learn reliable stop, undo, and recovery policies?** Reported models exhibit repetition, harmful continued action, draw–undo loops, or failure to react to collisions. An outcome-supervised or reinforcement-learning experiment could explicitly reward retained best state, valid recovery, and timely submission.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the benchmark design and reported main-table results because the supplied text includes the core methods, equations, tables, and discussion.

Material missing information includes the supplementary task specifications, alternative metric-weight analysis, full renderer details, complete training hyperparameters, per-instance variance or uncertainty, repeated-run statistics, the full semantic results for all 25 models, dataset licensing details, and empirical validation on applications outside the 2D canvas.
