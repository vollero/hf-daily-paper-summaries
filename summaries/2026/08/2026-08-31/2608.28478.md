# Blind Men and the Elephant: Probing the Epistemic Myopia of LLMs under Long-Tail Divergent Knowledge

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-31.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.28478) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28478)


## One-sentence takeaway

ElephantBench shows that leading LLMs usually recall at least one account of a disputed long-tail fact but recover all verified accounts on only about half of its closed-book questions.

## Problem and motivation

Standard factual QA assumes one canonical answer, so it cannot reveal whether an LLM’s parametric memory retains multiple source-supported accounts of the same fact. Existing long-tail QA mainly tests rare-fact recall, while conflict benchmarks commonly provide evidence at inference time and therefore test evidence-conditioned reasoning rather than memory completeness. The authors address this gap with a closed-book evaluation of “epistemic myopia”: recalling one account while omitting another verified account (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **ElephantBench:** A 1,094-question English benchmark built from naturally occurring disagreements in a low-scoring partition of the RePro web corpus. Each item asks for every verified account of one subject–attribute pair, with source documents hidden during evaluation (§§3.1, 4.1).
- **Graph-based construction pipeline:** Knowledge-point clustering and entity/event matching reduce candidate document pairs; an LLM then classifies candidate relations as support, conflict, or neither. Conflict-centered subgraphs drive QA generation (§§3.2–3.3).
- **Multi-stage validation:** Generated answers are checked against source documents, independently verified through public web sources, and reviewed by humans (§3.3).
- **Diagnostic scoring:** Responses receive complete, partial, or failed recall labels, plus conditional completeness \(K=C/(C+P)\), separating fact retention from multi-account completeness (§3.4).
- **Corpus-grounded analysis:** The study relates outcome patterns to majority- and minority-account document frequencies, explicitly treating these frequencies as observational exposure proxies rather than causal measurements (§5.1; Appendix G).
- **Released evaluation assets:** The paper states that data, provenance, code, and construction documentation will be released under Apache 2.0 (Appendix K).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| No individual model completely recalled much more than half of the benchmark. | Across all 1,094 closed-book questions, Kimi-K3 obtained the highest \(C\), 52.38%, with \(P=45.25\%\), \(F=2.38\%\), and \(K=53.65\%\). | Table 1; §4.3 |
| Strong models generally remembered at least one account but frequently omitted another. | Kimi-K3, Gemini-3.1-Pro, and GPT-5.5 had \(F=2.19\%–2.65\%\), but \(C=50.18\%–52.38\%\) and \(P=45.25\%–47.44\%\). | Table 1; §4.3 |
| Scaling improved recall, but much of the gain remained partial. | Within Qwen3.5, scaling from 2B to 397B increased \(C\) from 1.65% to 32.27% and reduced \(F\) from 81.35% to 8.50%, while \(P\) rose from 17.00% to 59.23%. | Figure 4; §4.3 |
| Reasoning effects varied by model and size. | Reasoning increased \(C\) by 13.99 points for GPT-5.6-Sol and 12.89 for GPT-OSS-120B; for Qwen3.5 it changed \(C\) by −0.64 and −0.37 points at 2B and 4B, versus gains of 0.73–4.39 points from 9B to 397B. | Figures 5–6; §4.3 |
| Majority- and minority-side exposure had different observational associations. | A \(+1\sigma\) increase in majority exposure was associated with \(P+14.18\) and \(F-10.17\) points; the same minority-exposure increase was associated with \(C+15.13\) and \(P-15.41\) points, holding the other side fixed. | Table 4; §5.1; Appendix G |
| Models exhibited complementary but shared blind spots. | A greedy oracle over all 32 configurations raised \(C\) from 52.4% to 81.2% and reduced \(F\) from 2.4% to 0; 206 questions, or 18.8%, remained partial for every configuration. | Figure 9; §5.3 |
| Question formulation showed no detected aggregate effect. | Across eight frontier models, the named-entity versus clue-based mean \(C\) difference was 0.65 points, 95% CI [−1.05, 2.31], \(p=0.470\). | §5.4; Figure 13 |
| Automatic judging broadly agreed with human labels. | Across four frontier models, exact agreement was 90.13%–93.36%, Cohen’s \(\kappa=0.815–0.877\), and the mean \(C\) difference was 1.44 points. | Table 2; Appendix D |
| Temperature did not materially alter the tested results. | Four models evaluated on all 1,094 questions at \(T=0\) and \(T=1\) had \(|\Delta C|\leq0.46\), \(|\Delta K|\leq0.77\), and all McNemar \(p\geq0.53\). | Table 3; Appendix E |

The evidence supports the authors’ claim that incomplete multi-account recall is common in this benchmark and that scale or reasoning does not universally remove it. It does not establish that corpus exposure causes model behavior, that all differing accounts are true, or that the findings generalize to all long-tail knowledge, languages, models, and disagreement types.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** RePro document frequencies are only proxies because the evaluated models’ actual training corpora and sampling policies are undisclosed; exposure results are associative, not causal (Limitations; Appendix G).
- **Author-stated:** Although the benchmark spans 1,094 questions and 22 fields, it cannot represent the full breadth of long-tail knowledge or source disagreement (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** The 32 models do not cover later systems, every model size, or every reasoning configuration (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** Independent public support does not guarantee that a reported account is correct; source error or misinformation can remain (Ethics Statement).
- **Analyst inference:** Construction and scoring depend substantially on LLM decisions. Human review and judge-agreement testing mitigate this risk, but the reliability audit covers only four frontier models (Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference:** The dataset is English-only and has no train/dev/test split, limiting multilingual conclusions and complicating repeated benchmark use or method development (Appendix K).
- **Analyst inference:** The text reports 1,094 questions, 405 matched pairs in Figure 2, and describes generating matched forms, but it does not fully explain how unmatched questions arise or how dependencies between paired formulations are handled in all analyses.
- **Analyst inference:** Conditional perplexity is presented as a promising proxy through stratified patterns, but the supplied text gives no correlation coefficient or threshold-based predictive evaluation (§5.5; Appendix J).

## Open research questions

1. **Would balancing minority-account exposure causally improve complete recall?** The current regression is observational. A controlled pretraining or continued-training experiment could vary exposure to each account independently and measure \(C/P/F\).

2. **How well does ElephantBench generalize across languages and corpora?** Its released form is English and derives from one low-scoring corpus partition. Repeating the pipeline on multilingual and domain-specific corpora could compare disagreement types and recall gaps.

3. **Can inference methods recover shared blind spots without external evidence?** No tested configuration completely answered 206 questions. An experiment could compare targeted uncertainty elicitation, self-consistency, or multi-agent recall against the same closed-book protocol.

4. **How reliable is perplexity as a substitute for generation-and-judge evaluation?** The paper reports aligned trends but not predictive calibration. A next experiment could test rank correlation, classification accuracy, and compute savings across all evaluated model families.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the benchmark design and reported findings because the supplied text includes the main paper and relevant appendices.

Material missing or unclear information includes the full dataset records, complete construction code, precise candidate and rejection counts at every validation stage, detailed annotator disagreement resolution, regression uncertainty estimates and diagnostics, quantitative validation of the perplexity proxy, confidential accelerator/device-hour usage, and a complete explanation of the relationship among 1,094 questions, 405 matched pairs, and underlying conflicts.
