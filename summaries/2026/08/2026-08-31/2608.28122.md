# Agentic Artifact Creation: Systems, Evaluation, Principles, and Opportunities

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-31.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.28122) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28122)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors frame agentic artifact creation as stateful, feedback-directed construction of deliverables and argue that dependable systems require explicit commitments, bounded control, actionable verification, and revalidation after edits.

## Problem and motivation

Direct generation can produce drafts and components but is less dependable for complete deliverables whose content, layout, behavior, and other acceptance criteria interact. Failures may emerge late, be difficult to localize, or require broad regeneration (Introduction §1.1; Figure 1).

The authors identify an artifact-centered gap in prior surveys: existing reviews primarily organize models, agent architectures, workflows, domains, or evaluation methods rather than treating the delivered artifact, its editable state, and its acceptance criteria as the main unit of analysis (§§1.3, 2.4; Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Definition and functional model:** Defines agentic artifact creation as an episode in which an AI system materially constructs or revises a deliverable, carries state across decisions, and uses intermediate observations to redirect subsequent work (§3.1). The functional architecture connects an operational representation, construction policy, and runtime verification (§3.2; Figure 4).
- **Coded literature corpus:** Reviews 259 works available through August 20, 2026—230 qualifying systems and 29 construction benchmarks—and codes them by role, artifact family, subtype, cross-cutting facets, and provenance (§1.3; Figure 2).
- **Cross-family taxonomy:** Compares textual, 2D visual, audio, video, spatial, and behavioral artifacts according to their dependencies, observation modes, representations, edit interfaces, policies, and verification mechanisms (§4; Tables 2–3).
- **Evaluation framework:** Separates evaluation of delivered artifacts, construction trajectories, and agentic systems, while distinguishing evidence channels, evaluators, metrics, and protocol conditions (§6; Figure 8).
- **Principles and research agenda:** Synthesizes four design principles—externalize commitments, define control boundaries, make feedback actionable, and revalidate affected state—and uses them to motivate unresolved research directions (§7; Figure 10).

The evidence establishes the breadth and organization of the authors’ coded literature sample and supports qualitative claims about fragmented evaluation coverage, feedback localization, and cross-family construction challenges. It does not establish that the proposed architecture or four principles causally improve artifact quality: the supplied text reports a survey synthesis rather than controlled experiments validating those principles. Some numerical findings are inherited from reviewed benchmarks or a leaderboard snapshot rather than produced by the survey itself.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated corpus reproducibility limitation:** Database-specific query exports, per-source yields, and pre-reconciliation labels were not retained, although resulting coding and limitations were released (§1.3).
- **Author-stated coverage limitation:** The 2026 corpus is partial through August 20, and the authors report sparse standalone benchmarks for audio, video, and spatial creation (§6.4; Figure 2).
- **Author-stated evaluation gaps:** Trajectories, use outcomes, and validity beyond tested cases are less consistently covered; calibration, evaluator disagreement, tools, budgets, stopping rules, and costs are inconsistently reported (§6.4).
- **Author-stated conceptual qualification:** Decision interdependence, observability, and repairability form a qualitative lens rather than causal factors or a universal ranking; the principles are not presented as a mandatory pipeline (§7; Figure 10).
- **Author-stated judge risk:** Learned judges may share generator preferences or blind spots, so multiple learned evaluations need not provide independent evidence (Abstract; §§6.2, 8.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Because corpus retrieval and reconciliation artifacts were not fully retained, independent reconstruction of the inclusion process may be difficult.
- **Analyst inference:** Table 4 deliberately selects three protocols per family and therefore supports structured comparison, not prevalence estimates across the full corpus (§6.4).
- **Source limitation:** The supplied paper text is truncated, omitting substantial material between §8.4 and the references. Conclusions, further challenges, and any additional limitations in the missing pages cannot be assessed.

## Open research questions

1. **Do explicit commitment graphs improve global coherence enough to justify their overhead?**  
   This remains open because the paper presents commitment externalization as a principle, not a controlled result (§§7.1, 8.1). A next experiment could compare direct generation, fixed structured state, and adaptive commitment graphs on held-out revisions, measuring requirement retention, rework, and coordination cost.

2. **Which verifier combinations provide genuinely independent diagnostic evidence?**  
   Shared generator–judge blind spots remain a stated concern (§8.2). A factorial experiment could combine rule checks, specialist models, independent LM judges, and expert review, then measure defect detection, source localization, repair success, and post-repair regression.

3. **Can selective revalidation preserve correctness while reducing cost?**  
   The authors describe versioned dependency and evidence graphs as a candidate abstraction but say cross-family evidence is sparse (§7.4). A next experiment could inject controlled changes into several artifact families and compare full revalidation with dependency-based selective checks.

4. **How should system-level reliability and update stability be benchmarked?**  
   Only three representative protocols assess properties beyond task capability, each covering one property (§6.4). A longitudinal benchmark could rerun fixed tasks across system versions and controlled perturbations, reporting variance, regressions, matched-success costs, and rollback effectiveness.
