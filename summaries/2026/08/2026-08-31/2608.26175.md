# Lost in Compression: A Controlled Cross-Lingual Audit of Extractive Prompt Compressors

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-31.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.26175) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26175)


## One-sentence takeaway

English-supervised extractive prompt compressors lose substantially more useful context outside English at aggressive compression rates, whereas deterministic controls and a natively multilingual query-aware compressor largely avoid this transfer gap.

## Problem and motivation

Extractive prompt compression reduces inference cost by deleting context tokens, but compressors are usually trained and evaluated in English. This matters because non-English text already incurs a reported 1.3–1.8× token premium on the paper’s parallel corpus, potentially combining higher token cost with greater compression-induced quality loss (Introduction; Figure 6).

The identified gap is the absence of a controlled cross-lingual audit that holds semantic content, target-model token budget, and evaluation items constant while separating compressor behavior from task difficulty, tokenizer effects, and target-model behavior (Sections 1 and 3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Controlled audit:** The authors evaluate fully parallel items in ten languages and five scripts, matching controls to achieved budgets in the target model’s tokenizer. Utility is normalized between full-context and no-context accuracy, with paired bootstrap uncertainty (Section 3, Equation 1).
- **Broad compressor comparison:** Four learned systems/checkpoints—English-supervised LLMLingua-2 variants and Kompress-v2, plus multilingual XProvence—are compared with four deterministic strategies across eleven target models (Sections 3–4).
- **Transfer-gap characterization:** The study varies compression rate and tests whether the English/non-English gap replicates across compressor backbones, supervision regimes, and target models (Section 4).
- **Task and context stress tests:** It evaluates distractor-heavy long contexts and MultiEURLEX topic classification to test whether failures depend on distributed answer-bearing content (Section 5; Appendix C).
- **Translation arbitrage:** A small experiment compares native compression with translating contexts into English before compression (Section 6).
- **Released evidence:** The authors report releasing code, more than 25,000 cached compressions, and over 178,000 main-arm evaluation records (Reproducibility section).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| LLMLingua-2’s transfer gap grows sharply with compression. | On 300 paired Belebele items per language, 8/9 non-English languages have significant gaps at keep-rate 0.33 on both primary models; English retains 57–62% normalized context utility, Lithuanian 10–24%, and Chinese approximately none. | Abstract; Table 1; Figure 1 |
| Safe budgets are markedly shallower outside English. | Using \(U\geq0.8\), the reported safe budget is about 2× compression for English but approximately 1.3× or less for the other nine languages. | Section 4 |
| Budget-matched deterministic methods show no comparable systematic gap. | At keep-rate 0.5 on GPT-5.4-mini, deterministic mean gaps range from −0.20 to −0.09 with no significant language cases, versus +0.27 and 7/9 significant for LLMLingua-2. Claude shows +0.26 and 9/9 for LLMLingua-2, while deterministic methods have at most 2/9 significant at that rate. | Section 4; Figure 2 |
| English-only evaluation can reverse method rankings. | At keep-rate 0.33, LLMLingua-2 leads TF-IDF by 12–14 accuracy points in English, but its non-English advantage ranges from −5 to +7 points; TF-IDF wins in several languages on both primary models. | Section 4 |
| The pattern replicates across English-supervised backbones. | At keep-rate 0.5, mBERT LLMLingua-2 has a mean gap of +0.31 with 7/8 significant languages, compared with +0.23 and 6/8 for XLM-R; Chinese is excluded because mBERT expands rather than compresses it at one requested rate. | Section 4, “Replication with an mBERT backbone” |
| Multilingual supervision is associated with a much smaller gap, but calibration matters. | XProvence v1 has mean gap −0.03 on GPT at threshold 0.5 with 0 significant languages; v2 at aggressive threshold 0.5 empties 92% of Chinese contexts and produces gaps of +0.16 on GPT and +0.29 on Claude. | Section 4; Figure 3 |
| The LLMLingua-2 gap persists across target models. | Across eleven targets, mean gaps at keep-rate 0.5 range from +0.26 to +0.40; Chinese is worst for every model. Chinese-vendor targets show Chinese gaps of +0.70 to +0.82. | Section 4; Figure 4; Table 6 |
| Distractor-heavy contexts amplify failures. | With 150 long-context items, GPT utility is at or below no-context for Lithuanian, Latvian, and Polish at keep-rate 0.33; Claude retains 0.56 utility in English versus 0.14–0.33 elsewhere. | Section 5; Figure 5 |
| The effect is task-dependent. | On 24 MultiEURLEX documents per language, a title-only 5% budget obtains micro-F1 0.47–0.51 versus 0.45–0.48 for full documents. | Section 5; Table 7 |
| Translate-then-compress is promising but preliminary. | With GPT-5.4-mini and 60 items per language, approximately 0.18× native token cost matches or beats native compression at 0.33× in 3/5 languages: LT +10 accuracy points, FI +6.6, and ET +5. | Section 6; Table 2 |

The evidence supports a replicated association between English-centered compression supervision and disproportionate non-English degradation, including rate-control and empty-output failures. The controls make task difficulty, target tokenizer budget, and target-model choice less plausible as sole explanations.

It does **not** causally isolate supervision language from every correlated design choice: XProvence is query-aware and therefore operates under an easier setting, while Kompress and XProvence lack re-matched deterministic controls at their achieved budgets. The mechanism involving grammatical cohesion remains provisional rather than established.

## Limitations and validity risks

Author-stated limitations:

- Ten languages and five scripts remain narrow; six languages are Indo-European, and no non-Uralic agglutinative language is included (Section 7).
- Long-context, MultiEURLEX, and translation experiments cover only six European languages; the translation arm uses only 60 items per language (Sections 3, 6–7).
- FLORES-derived passages may contain contamination and translationese. Because they originate in English, the authors suggest—but do not prove—that native text could exhibit a larger gap (Sections 6–7).
- XProvence is query-aware, preventing a like-for-like absolute-quality comparison with task-agnostic compressors (Section 7).
- No multiplicity correction is applied across several hundred intervals, so isolated significant results may be false positives (Sections 3 and 7).
- Contexts are at most approximately 3k tokens, much shorter than many production RAG contexts; instructions are always English (Section 7).
- Refusal behavior differs by target model and is a confound for degraded-context evaluation (Section 4).
- The proposed morphology mechanism lacks causal confirmation; the case-marking probe is preliminary and its question design and survival metric have acknowledged weaknesses (Section 7; Appendix D).

Analyst inferences:

- The claim that supervision data “drives” the gap is stronger than the design can conclusively establish because supervision regime is entangled with query awareness, training datasets, calibration, and segmentation.
- Translation arbitrage cannot yet establish production superiority because translation quality, latency, licensing, and native-source performance are incompletely tested.
- Some baseline behavior is script-dependent: random deletion uses whitespace units, which correspond to larger chunks in Chinese (Appendix E).

## Open research questions

1. **Does the gap persist on natively authored multilingual text?** Translationese may make non-English passages unusually English-like. A next experiment could repeat the parallel audit with native-source documents and independently produced translations.

2. **Which linguistic structures are actually destroyed by compression?** The morphology explanation is based on diagnostics and a preliminary Lithuanian probe. A next experiment could use role-balanced minimal pairs with morpheme-level survival analysis across case-marking, agglutinative, and isolating languages.

3. **Can one compressor jointly achieve multilingual calibration and fine-grained Chinese segmentation?** XProvence v1 is conservative, while v2 exhibits all-or-nothing Chinese pruning. A next experiment could replace its sentence segmentation, calibrate thresholds per language, and measure both achieved-rate error and utility.

4. **Do results scale to production-length contexts and localized instructions?** The study stops near 3k tokens and fixes instructions in English. A next experiment could test 30k–300k-token multilingual RAG contexts with instructions localized to each language.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported experimental design and headline results because the supplied text includes methods, settings, metrics, limitations, and extensive result summaries.

Material missing information includes the numerical cells of Tables 1–7, complete confidence intervals, per-language raw accuracies, detailed prompt templates, hardware and monetary cost accounting for all compressors, native-source multilingual results, production-length evaluations, and definitive causal ablations separating supervision language from query awareness, segmentation, and calibration.
