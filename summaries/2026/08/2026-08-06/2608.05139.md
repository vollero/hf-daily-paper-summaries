# Toward Skill-Native LLMs: Skill Entropy for Benchmarking and Training Long-Horizon Reasoning

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.05139) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05139)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors introduce a reference-model-derived measure of skill-switching difficulty, use it to build a nine-domain long-horizon benchmark, and show that adding an entropy-matching reward to RL substantially improves cross-skill performance on several small language models.

## Problem and motivation

Long-horizon tasks often require a model to change reasoning skills between dependent steps while preserving prior outputs. The authors argue that strong performance on isolated skills does not measure this ability: models can solve component skills individually yet fail when those skills are composed.

Existing evaluations reportedly lack a task-level measure of how difficult particular directed skill transitions are. The paper therefore addresses both measurement—isolating skill-switching difficulty from single-skill competence—and training—providing explicit supervision for selecting an appropriate skill at each step (Sections 1–3).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Skill entropy:** A directed transition score defined as a smoothed ratio of reference-model single-skill accuracy to two-step cross-skill accuracy. Task entropy averages these scores over consecutive transitions (Equations 3–4). For tractability, the full skill-pair table is approximated through skill-to-domain and domain-to-skill factors (Equation 7).

2. **Skill2-Bench:** A benchmark of 300 held-out tasks, each containing 2–10 dependent steps, covering 558 labeled skills in nine verifiable or open-ended domains. Tasks are divided into low-, medium-, and high-entropy strata (Table 1; Sections 3.2–3.3).

3. **Skill-switching diagnosis:** Evaluation of eight frontier and four open-source models finds lower accuracy in cross-skill contexts and generally lower performance at higher entropy. The authors associate many failures with carrying the previous step’s skill or answer modality into the next step (Table 2; Figure 5).

4. **Skill-Entropy RL:** Models emit a skill label before every answer. GRPO optimizes a weighted combination of mean step correctness and agreement between the entropy ranks of predicted and gold skill sequences (Equations 5–6).

5. **Training-data reuse:** The authors label reasoning steps in OpenR1-Math with skills and apply the same reward to an existing dataset, reporting gains across six math evaluations (Section 4.4; Table 11).

|---|---|---|
| Cross-skill context reduces accuracy. | Across the 12 evaluated models, the reported average-domain difference between single- and cross-skill settings ranges from −4.3 to −12.6 points; the five displayed verifiable domains are coding, logic, math, planning, and science. | Table 2; Section 3.3 |
| Higher entropy generally predicts lower performance. | On the balanced 300-task test set, most models score lower on high- than low-entropy tasks. The evidence supports the authors’ claim that cross-skill context is a measurable source of difficulty and that their reward improves average results over matched GRPO on the tested models. Because the experiments do not manipulate skill choice independently of answer quality, Figure 5 establishes association rather than proving that wrong skill selection causes the accuracy loss. The reported results also do not establish that the entropy formulation is universally model-independent or superior outside the evaluated task-generation and skill-bank framework.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated approximation:** Direct evaluation of roughly \(3.1\times10^5\) ordered skill pairs is replaced by a multiplicative skill–domain factorization (Appendix B.4). This may obscure variation between skills within the same domain.

- **Reference-model dependence:** Entropy is defined using Claude-opus-4.7. Alternative-model overlaps exceed 80%, but task-rank correlations can be as low as 0.517, indicating meaningful ordering variation (Tables 7–8).

- **Small entropy-human study:** Human validation uses four annotators and only nine tasks, with 63% model–annotator agreement (Table 9). This is limited evidence for human-perceived switching difficulty.

- **Synthetic benchmark dependence:** Tasks are rewritten or generated by LLMs and filtered by a verifier. The supplied text does not identify the proposer/verifier models, report rejection rates, or provide an independent audit of whether every step truly requires the preceding answer.

- **Open-ended scoring risks:** Three domains rely on rubric-based LLM judgments. Human agreement is encouraging (Table 10), but does not remove judge bias. The text also conflicts on protocol: Section 3.3 names Claude-opus-4.7 as judge, whereas Appendix D.5 says the evaluated model itself serves as judge for the training evaluation.

- **Analyst inference—reward underidentification:** The entropy reward compares scalar entropy ranks rather than exact skill identities (Equation 6). Different predicted skill chains could therefore receive high reward if their aggregate entropy is similar; no ablation against exact skill-sequence matching is supplied.

- **Analyst inference—limited statistical reporting:** Main training tables provide point estimates without confidence intervals or multiple-run variance, so the stability of improvements across random seeds is not established.

- **Analyst inference—resource intensity:** The main setup uses 8 H100 GPUs, extensive reference-model sampling, LLM-generated traces, and embedding-based label matching (Tables 12; Appendix B.3–B.4). No compute or monetary cost comparison is reported.

## Open research questions

1. **Does entropy rank matching outperform direct skill-label supervision?**  
   This remains open because the paper compares against answer-only GRPO but not an exact skill-sequence reward. A next experiment could hold data and compute fixed while comparing entropy-rank, exact-label, transition-wise, and combined rewards.

2. **How accurate is the skill-to-domain factorization?**  
   Equation 7 is adopted for tractability without comparison to directly estimated skill-pair entropy. A stratified sample of ordered skill pairs could be evaluated directly and compared with the factorized scores and resulting task rankings.

3. **Does the method transfer to naturally occurring long-horizon workflows?**  
   Most core tasks are synthesized, while external benchmarks show only modest average gains. A next experiment could evaluate realistic multi-step tasks with human-annotated skill transitions and no LLM rewriting.

4. **What component causes the training gains?**  
   The method combines structured skill emission, SFT traces, entropy shaping, and answer supervision. A factorial experiment separating explicit skill tags, teacher traces, embedding matching, and entropy reward would clarify their individual effects.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported definitions, benchmark construction, and tabulated results; **medium** for broader validity because the benchmark and training pipeline depend heavily on synthetic tasks and model-based annotation.

Material missing or ambiguous information includes proposer and verifier identities, filtering rejection rates, training-run variance and significance tests, full compute/cost accounting, direct validation of the skill-to-domain approximation, an exact-skill-reward baseline, complete external evaluation prompts, and a consistent statement of which model judges open-ended outputs during post-training evaluation.
