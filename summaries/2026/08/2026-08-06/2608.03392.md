# Self-Evolving Coding Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.03392) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03392)


## One-sentence takeaway

This survey defines self-evolving coding agents and organizes prior work by what evolves, when adaptation occurs, and which software-specific evidence drives it, while emphasizing that trustworthy evolution requires evaluation beyond short-term benchmark success.

## Problem and motivation

Coding agents operate in changing repositories and repeatedly encounter tests, compiler errors, runtime traces, CI results, and reviews, yet their models, prompts, tools, memory, and control flow commonly remain fixed after deployment. This prevents them from accumulating project-specific experience and may cause repeated mistakes.

The authors identify a gap between broad surveys of self-evolving agents and the distinctive requirements of software engineering: repository-level context, executable artifacts, iterative debugging, concrete correctness constraints, and maintainability concerns are not adequately captured by generic agent taxonomies or evaluation practices (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Conceptual definition:** A self-evolving coding agent persistently updates its behavior or internal components from previous coding attempts and software-specific feedback, rather than merely retrying a task (§2.3).
- **Object-centered taxonomy:** The survey groups methods by five primary evolution targets: agent frameworks, memory, skills and tools, model-side components, and workflow or collaboration topology (§3; Figure 2; Table 2).
- **Orthogonal analytical dimensions:** It distinguishes task-time, post-task, and stage-wise evolution, alongside outcome, environmental, and trajectory-derived evidence (§4).
- **Evaluation synthesis:** It organizes relevant tasks, benchmarks, and metrics while arguing for assessment of correctness, cost, maintainability, robustness, safety, and transfer beyond the evolution setting (§5–§6).
- **Literature asset:** The authors provide a public paper collection; the supplied text does not describe its selection protocol or completeness (Abstract).

No quantitative effect sizes, aggregate study counts, or meta-analysis are reported in the supplied text. The evidence establishes a conceptual synthesis of the literature and recurring evaluation patterns; it does not demonstrate that self-evolving agents consistently outperform static agents or that any one evolution target is superior.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ stated concerns:** Dynamic agents are difficult to reproduce across runs, repositories, tools, and model versions, while benchmark-guided evolution risks leakage, contamination, and overfitting (§6).
- **Authors’ stated concerns:** Tests, compilers, generated tests, learned verifiers, and CI feedback can be incomplete or misleading; erroneous feedback may persist in future memories, skills, policies, or scaffolds (§6).
- **Authors’ stated concerns:** Memory and skill stores may become stale, redundant, repository-specific, or contaminated by failed trajectories; evolving multi-agent structures may increase cost and instability (§6).
- **Authors’ stated concerns:** Short-horizon benchmark scores do not adequately measure maintainability, security, reviewability, efficiency, or long-term reliability (§6).
- **Analyst inference:** The supplied text does not state a systematic search strategy, inclusion criteria, screening procedure, study count, or quality appraisal. Consequently, the coverage and representativeness of the taxonomy cannot be independently assessed.
- **Analyst inference:** Table 2’s contents are not rendered in the supplied text, limiting verification of how individual papers were classified.
- **Analyst inference:** The taxonomy’s categories overlap, as the authors acknowledge, but no formal coding rules or inter-rater reliability evidence is supplied; assignments may therefore involve subjective judgment.
- **Analyst inference:** Because the paper is a survey without aggregated quantitative comparisons, it cannot establish causal benefits of persistent adaptation over retrying, retrieval, or ordinary post-training.

## Open research questions

1. **How can evolution be distinguished from benchmark-specific adaptation?**  
   The survey identifies contamination and repeated tuning as unresolved threats (§6). A next experiment could evolve agents on one private repository set and test frozen variants on temporally and organizationally separated repositories, including static-agent controls.

2. **Which feedback signals remain reliable under incomplete tests?**  
   The paper notes that passing local checks may conceal regressions, safety problems, or poor maintainability (§§2.3, 6). A next experiment could inject controlled test omissions and misleading diagnostics, then compare test-only evolution with multi-signal validation using static analysis, held-out tests, and review.

3. **How should evolving memories and skills be maintained over long periods?**  
   Staleness, redundancy, and failed-trajectory contamination remain unresolved (§6). A longitudinal experiment could compare append-only memory with validated replacement, decay, and rollback policies across successive repository versions.

4. **Which evolution target offers the best benefit–cost–risk trade-off?**  
   The taxonomy identifies five targets but reports no controlled comparison among them. A factorial study could hold the base model and tasks fixed while separately enabling framework, memory, skill, model, and workflow updates, measuring success, transfer, cost, and regressions.
