# The Personalization Mirage: How LLMs Fabricate User Profiles, and Why Self-Monitoring Misleads

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.04570) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04570)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across MirageBench, all 12 evaluated LLMs made unsupported personalized claims frequently, while self-audits helped rank claims within a model but misleadingly reversed model-level safety rankings.

## Problem and motivation

The paper studies over-inference (OI): personalized LLMs asserting user attributes unsupported by what the user revealed. This matters because persistent-memory systems may store such guesses as facts, allowing errors to influence later interactions.

The authors distinguish OI from world-knowledge hallucination and group-level bias: it concerns unsupported claims about an individual. They argue that existing personalization benchmarks emphasize remembering and applying user information, leaving the evidential faithfulness of generated or stored profiles underexamined (Introduction; Related Work).

## Approach and contributions

- **MirageBench:** 150 personas, each exposing exactly 3 facts, crossed with 6 personalization tasks. Personas are balanced among stereotypical, counter-stereotypical, and neutral profiles (MirageBench: Personas and Personalization Tasks).
- **Faithfulness evaluation:** Claims are labeled Grounded, Reasonable, Stereotype, or Fabricated; the latter two define OI. A fixed external judge is validated against one blind human annotator on 400 stratified claims (Problem Formulation; Evaluation Pipeline).
- **Four instruments:** Probe measures explicit inference, Task elicits personalized generation and self-audit, Judge independently labels claims, and Accum tracks memory across eight turns (Figure 3).
- **Cross-model evidence:** The study evaluates 12 models from 7 families on 143,616 claims and reports model-, task-, persona-, and self-monitoring effects (Metrics and Models; Evaluation Results).
- **Longitudinal pilot:** Accum examines whether unsupported attributes grow or are revised over repeated interactions, though only for two personas and under a retention-oriented prompt (Section 5.4; Appendix A).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| OI affected every evaluated model | Judge-labeled OI was 35%–49% across 12 models and 900 instances per model; cross-model mean 41.6%, claim-weighted mean 41.8% over 143,616 claims. | Table 1; Section 5.1; Appendix B |
| Fabrication was more common than stereotyping | Across models, mean fabrication was 31.1% and mean stereotyping 10.5%. | Section 5.1, discussion of Table 1 |
| Self-monitoring reversed cross-model rankings | Self-audit OI versus Judge OI had Spearman \(\rho=-0.60\), permutation \(p=0.044\), \(n=12\); bootstrap CI was \([-0.90,+0.06]\) and family-clustered CI \([-0.87,+0.14]\). | Table 2; Section 5.2 |
| Self-audit retained within-model ranking value | Across 900 persona-task records per model, AUROC ranged from 0.58 to 0.83; 9 of 12 models exceeded 0.75. Claim extractors differed, so this is record-level rather than matched-claim evidence. | Section 5.2; Table 6; Appendix D |
| Explicit and task-time inference differed sharply | Under Probe, Judge-assessed incorrect rates were 0.7%–4.6%, with a mean 38.6 percentage-point gap relative to Task-generation OI. | Section 5.2 |
| OI depended strongly on task groundability | Pooled across 12 models and 150 personas, apartment descriptions reached 57.8% OI while gift recommendations reached 27.0%; the ordering was consistent across all models. | Table 3; Section 5.3; Table 10 |
| Persona stereotype alignment affected OI | Pooled OI was 44.8% for stereotypical, 37.0% for counter-stereotypical, and 43.9% for neutral personas; the stereotypical–counter-stereotypical gap was positive for all 12 models. | Section 5.1; Table 7; Appendix E |
| Unsupported memory often accumulated in the pilot | Over 8 rounds and 2 personas, 9 of 12 models had linear-fit \(R^2>0.90\), with slopes of 5–15 attributes per round; the five fastest accumulators had 0.4%–5% removal rates. | Table 4; Section 5.4; Table 8 |

The results establish frequent evidence-unsupported personalization within the tested models, prompts, tasks, and judge rubric. They also support a task-dependent pattern and a distinction between within-model and cross-model self-audit utility.

They do not establish that OI is inevitable in LLMs, that the reported absolute rates generalize to richer user histories or other scoring conventions, or that accumulation naturally occurs under neutral memory prompts. The cross-model inversion is exploratory because its confidence intervals include zero.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:**

- A single model judged the full corpus. Validation used only one human annotator, so it measures human–judge rather than inter-annotator agreement; no confidence interval for κ is reported (Appendix A).
- The judge saw ground-truth profiles for a separate contradiction check, and the authors cannot exclude their influence on borderline taxonomy decisions (Appendix A).
- Evidence-only scoring counts task-presupposed claims as unsupported, making OI on presupposition-heavy tasks an upper bound relative to task-aware grounding (Appendix A).
- The self-monitoring inversion uses only 12 correlated model instances, with bootstrap intervals containing zero (Appendix A; Table 2).
- Self-audit and Judge used different claim extractors, preventing one-to-one claim comparison (Appendix A; Appendix D).
- Accum covers two personas and uses a prompt favoring retention, so absolute accumulation is not evidence of spontaneous memory pollution (Appendix A; Appendix G.7).
- No mitigation, downstream recommendation-quality, or user-satisfaction evaluation was conducted (Appendix A).

**Analyst inferences:**

- Because generation used temperature 0.7 but no repeated-run variability is reported, the stability of model rankings across stochastic samples is unclear (Appendix G.9).
- The persona mixture includes 72 constrained LLM-generated and 8 hand-crafted profiles; the supplied text does not report a separate analysis by persona source, leaving possible construction effects unresolved (Section 4.1).
- The “first benchmark” and related novelty claims are author claims; the supplied paper alone cannot independently verify priority.

## Open research questions

1. **Does the cross-model self-monitoring inversion replicate at larger scale?**  
   It remains open because \(n=12\) and both reported confidence intervals contain zero. A next experiment could test more independently trained model families with a preregistered rank-correlation analysis and multiple external judges.

2. **How sensitive are OI rates to the definition of admissible evidence?**  
   Task context is deliberately excluded as evidence, potentially inflating rates for presupposition-heavy tasks. A factorial evaluation could compare evidence-only, task-aware, and explicit-user-confirmation rubrics on the same claims.

3. **Does unsupported memory accumulate without retention instructions?**  
   The pilot prompt explicitly discourages removal and uses only two personas. A larger longitudinal study could randomize retention-oriented versus prune-permitting prompts and introduce controlled contradictions.

4. **Can provenance-aware filtering reduce OI without destroying useful personalization?**  
   The paper recommends external verification and provenance but tests no mitigation or utility tradeoff. A follow-up could compare external judging, calibrated self-audit, and evidence-linked memory writes while measuring both OI and user-rated personalization quality.
