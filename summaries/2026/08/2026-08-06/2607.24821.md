# AVE-Compass: Towards Holistic Evaluation for Audio-Video Editing Abilities

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.24821) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24821)


## One-sentence takeaway

AVE-Compass combines fine-grained multimodal editing diagnostics with an agentic editor, showing that current systems struggle to execute cross-modal changes without damaging non-target content, while AVE-Agent substantially improves edit execution and synchronization.

## Problem and motivation

Real-world video edits often require coordinated visual, sound, and speech changes—for example, altering an action and its sound while preserving background ambience. Existing benchmarks largely test silent visual editing, isolated audio editing, or narrow object-level alignment, leaving implicit cross-modal dependencies, synchronization, and non-target preservation undermeasured (Sections 1–2; Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **AVE-Compass benchmark:** 145 curated source videos, 196 human-verified instructions, 28 operation types across joint, speech, video-only, and audio-only editing, and 2,688 human-refined checklist items (Sections 3.1 and 1).
- **Diagnostic evaluation:** Separates Instruction Following (IF), Fidelity Preserving (FP), Realism, and their per-case product, Editing Intent (EI). Checklist-based MLLM judgments are complemented by seven automated audio, video, and synchronization metrics (Section 3.2; Appendix A).
- **AVE-Agent:** A three-agent framework that converts instructions into dependency-aware subtask DAGs, routes subtasks to specialized editing tools, applies evaluator-guided retries, and globally remixes, regenerates, or replans when needed (Section 4; Figure 3).
- **Empirical diagnosis:** Evaluates five baselines plus AVE-Agent and analyzes response behavior, difficulty robustness, metric agreement, failure modes, and agent components (Section 5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| AVE-Agent leads complete edit execution. | Across 196 instructions, overall EI is **59.8**, versus **42.4** for the strongest listed baseline, Wan2.7; overall IF is **77.3** versus **70.1** for the strongest baseline on that metric, LTX2. Scores use a 0–100 scale. | Table 2 |
| The largest AVE-Agent gains occur on audio behavior. | Relative to Wan2.7, audio EI rises from **24.8 to 50.2**, audio IF from **60.3 to 69.4**, audio FP from **48.1 to 74.8**, and AV Sync from **0.693 to 0.766**. | Tables 2–3 |
| Improvements are not uniform across quality measures. | Compared with Wan2.7, AVE-Agent’s Speech Quality falls from **0.428 to 0.368**, while Video Aesthetic changes only from **0.451 to 0.452**. | Table 3 |
| Non-response inflates preservation scores for some models. | Gemini-Omni’s audio response rate is **22.0%**; its audio FP drops from **96.1** ungated to **80.6** on responding cases. Seedance’s video response rate is **69.1%**, with video FP dropping from **83.0 to 74.9** after gating. | Table 4 |
| AVE-Agent’s gains depend on planning and retry mechanisms. | Removing prompt enhancement lowers EI by **7.76** and IF by **6.91**; removing evaluator-driven retry lowers EI by **8.75** and IF by **8.51**. | Section 5.3; Figure 6 |
| Automatic judging is reported as stable and human-aligned. | Across five scoring runs on a representative subset, automated metrics vary by less than **0.01** and MLLM scores by less than **1%**. Human–MLLM agreement ranges from **87.9% to 93.0%** across reported metric/modality cells. | Section 5.2; Table 5 |
| AVE-Agent has fewer thresholded failures. | It records **175** total errors versus **248–406** for baselines; errors use checklist yes-rate below 0.5 or Realism below 3/5. | Appendix C.5; Table 9 |

These results support the authors’ claim that structured planning and evaluator-driven refinement improve instruction execution, preservation, and synchronization relative to the tested systems. They do not establish universal superiority: results come from one 196-instruction benchmark, some quality metrics do not improve, one baseline misses 16 speech edits due to moderation, and the agent integrates third-party tools rather than isolating a single learned model.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** The benchmark trades scale for coverage because generation APIs and MLLM judging are expensive. A full evaluation of one model is estimated at roughly **$230–235**, before retries, storage, and human verification (Appendix E).
- **Author-stated:** AVE-Agent depends on third-party tools whose behavior may change, weakening long-term reproducibility (Appendix E).
- **Author-stated:** Generative audio-video editing presents deepfake, disinformation, and non-consensual voice-cloning risks, particularly for speech edits (Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference:** The 196 instructions cover the stated taxonomy but may be too few for precise conclusions within all 28 subcategories and difficulty strata; category counts are not fully shown in the retrieved text.
- **Analyst inference:** MLLM–human agreement is measured on “sampled questions,” but the supplied text does not state the sample size, annotator count, or inter-human agreement, limiting interpretation of Table 5.
- **Analyst inference:** AVE-Agent combines planning, retries, specialized tools, and extra computation. The main full-benchmark comparison therefore does not by itself isolate whether gains arise from agent logic, tool access, or compute, although component and 34-case backbone ablations provide partial evidence.
- **Analyst inference:** Some automated scores measure output quality without reference to the requested edit, so high values can coexist with non-response; the paper recognizes this and adds response-gated analysis.

## Open research questions

1. **How well does AVE-Compass generalize beyond its curated sources and API systems?** The benchmark contains 145 sources and 196 instructions. A next experiment could evaluate new source domains, longer videos, and unseen editors while preserving the same taxonomy.

2. **Which AVE-Agent component provides the best benefit per unit of compute?** Prompt enhancement and retries are ablated, but tool choice, replanning, and mixed evaluation are not separately quantified in the supplied results. A factorial ablation with matched tool calls and retry budgets could isolate their contributions.

3. **Can evaluation reduce dependence on closed MLLM judges without losing semantic sensitivity?** Automated and subjective metrics are reported as complementary and only weakly correlated. A next experiment could train or calibrate open evaluators against a larger, multi-annotator human study.

4. **Can stronger edit execution avoid the observed speech-quality trade-off?** AVE-Agent improves synchronization but reduces Speech Quality relative to Wan2.7. Controlled experiments varying speech generation, lip-sync, and remix stages could locate the degradation.

## Confidence and missing information
