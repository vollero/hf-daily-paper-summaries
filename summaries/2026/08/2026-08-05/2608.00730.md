# Push-Wiper: Toward General-Purpose Robotic Cleaning across Varied Stains and Surfaces with Segmented Pushing Trajectories

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.00730) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00730)


## One-sentence takeaway

Push-Wiper learns segmented, topology-driven pushes that aggregate stains before removal, substantially outperforming coverage wiping and one-shot pushing while transferring without retraining to curved surfaces and unseen materials.

## Problem and motivation

Viscous stains are difficult for robots because ordinary wiping can spread them, while forceful scrubbing risks surface abrasion and requires careful control. Their deformation depends on complex, often unknown rheology, making analytical planning difficult; restricted liquid use further complicates tabletop cleaning. The authors identify a gap in learning-based systems that can clean viscous stains while generalizing across stain types and curved surface geometries without redesign or retraining (Sections I–II).

## Approach and contributions

- **Aggregate-then-finish formulation:** Cleaning is treated as progressively reducing stain diameter and fragmentation through segmented pushes, followed by primitives that detach residue and clean the sponge (Sections III-A and III-C; Eq. 2; Figure 2).
- **Topology-based policy:** A Diffusion Policy maps binary stain masks and a fixed capture pose to complete low-frequency pushing strokes in a re-perceive–plan–execute loop. Its action space contains planar position and yaw only (Section III-B).
- **Geometry-decoupled execution:** ASPI projects planar actions onto a known 3D surface, aligns the tool with local surface normals, and interpolates a 6-DoF trajectory; hybrid force–position control maintains normal contact (Section III-B; Algorithm 1; Eqs. 3–5).
- **Cross-condition evaluation:** The system is tested on planar and unseen curved surfaces, two trained stain materials, unseen viscous mixtures, liquids, and solid objects (Sections IV-C–IV-F; Tables I–IV).

These experiments establish strong performance on the tested robot, stains, and geometries, including transfer beyond the planar training setting. They do not establish broad real-world generality, statistical significance, superiority to independently implemented systems, or safe operation around people. The abstract’s “up to 130% higher” and conclusion’s “approximately 130%” claims are not tied to a clearly stated calculation; Table I should be treated as the precise evidence.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated limitation:** Collision constraints for safe human–robot interaction remain future work, as does extending post-processing to more stain types (Section V).
- **Analyst inference—limited evaluation breadth:** Tests use one robot, one sponge geometry, two primary training materials, two curved geometries, and small generalization sets of ten trials per category (Sections IV-A and IV-D–IV-F).
- **Analyst inference—baseline scope:** FC and PO are reimplementations inside the authors’ stack rather than comparisons with independently developed cleaning systems. Baselines receive a matched time budget but are reported at their best intermediate CS, complicating direct operational comparison (Sections IV-B–IV-C).
- **Analyst inference—missing component ablations:** The text isolates segmented pushing through PO and tests post-processing, but does not report separate ablations for binary abstraction, ASPI, force control, data augmentation, or the Diffusion Policy choice.
- **Analyst inference—metric comparability:** Solid-object “CS” is a binary connectivity measure, unlike the stain-area reduction used for liquids and viscous materials, so the values are not directly comparable (Section IV-E).
- **Analyst inference—reproducibility gaps:** Surface reconstruction details, policy architecture dimensions, demonstration protocol, controller gains, surface geometries, and released artifacts are not fully specified in the supplied text.

## Open research questions

1. **Which components actually drive generalization?** The paper does not separately ablate binary masks, ASPI, diffusion modeling, or segmented demonstrations. A factorial ablation across unseen stains and curved surfaces could measure each contribution.
2. **How robust is cleaning to perception and geometry error?** The method assumes usable masks and surface normals, but sensitivity is not reported. A next experiment could inject controlled segmentation, calibration, and normal-estimation errors.
3. **Does the method scale to more varied tools, surfaces, and rheologies?** Evaluation covers limited hardware and geometries. Testing porous, delicate, sharply curved, or discontinuous surfaces with multiple sponge types and stains would probe the claimed general-purpose scope.
4. **Can efficiency and safety improve together?** Trials average 130 seconds and lack human-aware collision constraints. A next experiment could add constrained planning and compare cleaning score, runtime, contact-force excursions, and collision rate.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported method and experimental results because the supplied text contains detailed framework and evaluation sections.

Materially missing information includes statistical-significance tests, confidence intervals, exact baseline trial traces, detailed network architecture, controller gains, surface-reconstruction procedure, curved-object dimensions, failure counts, safety evaluation, and a clear derivation of the claimed 130% improvement. The text mentions a project website but does not establish which code, data, or models are released.
