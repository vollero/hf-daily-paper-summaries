# LegalPincite: Multi-level Legal Information Retrieval Dataset

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.03756) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03756)


## One-sentence takeaway

LegalPincite provides an English CJEU retrieval benchmark with masked queries, complete paragraph corpora, chronological splits, and citation ground truth spanning case-to-case, paragraph-to-case, and paragraph-to-paragraph retrieval.

## Problem and motivation

Legal practitioners often need pinpoint citations to a specific paragraph rather than merely a relevant case. Existing public legal IR collections largely omit paragraph-to-paragraph annotations; the three identified exceptions retain citation clues in queries and restrict candidates to citing or cited paragraphs, potentially inflating retrieval scores and understating realistic search difficulty (Introduction; Table 1).

The authors also identify language ambiguity, missing text, segmentation errors, and unusable expert annotations in the two datasets underlying their work (§2.1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Multi-level test collection:** The authors merge, repair, deduplicate, and update two CJEU datasets to support case-to-case, paragraph-to-case, and paragraph-to-paragraph retrieval (§§2.1–2.3).
- **More realistic retrieval setting:** Candidate corpora contain all available case paragraphs, including paragraphs that neither cite nor are cited (Introduction; Table 1).
- **Leakage mitigation:** Citation-related case names, identifiers, paragraph numbers, parties, and entities are removed from queries using regular expressions and a pretrained legal NER model (§2.2).
- **Temporal evaluation design:** Queries, relevant citations, and candidates are split chronologically so that retrieval cannot use future precedents (Table 2).
- **Reusable release:** CSV queries, documents, binary qrels, case metadata, extraction pipelines, loading examples, and lexical-retrieval examples are released in PyTerrier/TREC-compatible formats (§2.2; §2.4).
- **Empirical characterization:** Four lexical baselines are evaluated, and the effects of citation leakage and limited expert validation are analyzed (§3).

These results establish that the collection supports large-scale multi-level retrieval and that simple lexical methods provide nontrivial baselines. They also demonstrate that leakage treatment can substantially alter scores. They do **not** establish superiority over dense or generative retrieval, because those methods were not evaluated, nor do they establish complete relevance judgments: most qrels come from observed EUR-Lex citations.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Citation-derived ground truth may contain a feedback loop if judges found sources through existing retrieval systems, potentially reflecting those systems’ rankings (§4).
- **Author-stated:** Expert judgments cover the top ten results of one dense retriever, limiting exhaustiveness; the authors therefore recommend precision-oriented short-cutoff metrics rather than recall (§4).
- **Author-stated:** Automatic language filtering and citation masking miss some instances, and no systematic audit is reported (§4).
- **Author-stated:** Dense retrievers were omitted because of document length, dataset scale, and limited computation (§3.1).
- **Author-stated:** Names of people or entities may remain despite masking, although judgments are publicly available (§4).
- **Analyst inference:** Comparisons among retrieval families are narrow because all reported baselines are bag-of-words methods; Table 6 cannot indicate how modern semantic retrieval behaves.
- **Analyst inference:** Table 7 does not validate all train/test qrels against experts—the paper explicitly says those splits contain only EUR-Lex qrels—so agreement on the dev subsets cannot establish dataset-wide judgment completeness.
- **Analyst inference:** Leakage masking’s quality remains uncertain without the systematic audit the authors leave to future work, especially because Table 8 shows masking can move NDCG@10 substantially in either direction.

## Open research questions

1. **How would dense, late-interaction, or hybrid retrieval compare with lexical baselines?** This remains open because §3.1 excludes dense systems. A next experiment could index each retrieval level with representative dense and hybrid models, tune on dev, and report the same HR, NDCG, MAP, and MRR metrics.

2. **How complete are citation-derived relevance judgments?** Observed citations need not include every legally relevant precedent, while expert annotations cover only one retriever’s top ten (§4). A pooled assessment could combine top results from diverse lexical and semantic systems and have legal experts judge a stratified sample.

3. **How accurate is the masking pipeline?** Manual checks found missed citation-related information, but no systematic audit was performed (§4). A next experiment could manually label leakage spans in a representative sample and measure masking precision, recall, and retrieval-score sensitivity.

4. **Does performance transfer beyond English CJEU judgments?** The released collection removes non-English text despite originating from multilingual documents (§§2.1, 4). A multilingual extension could align equivalent judgments across languages and compare monolingual with cross-lingual retrieval under the same chronological protocol.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the dataset design, reported statistics, baselines, and stated limitations because the supplied text includes the complete methods, tables, and discussion.

Material missing information includes per-query confidence intervals or significance tests; detailed masking audit results; dense, neural, hybrid, or generative baselines; expert agreement statistics in this paper; recall-oriented judgment-completeness estimates; and evaluation outside English CJEU case law.
