# RestoreKV: Recovering Full-Cache Behavior Under Aggressive Query-Agnostic KV Cache Eviction

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.01247) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.01247)


## One-sentence takeaway

RestoreKV learns a small context-conditioned KV complement before eviction, substantially recovering long-context performance under tight, query-agnostic cache budgets while preserving the base method’s query-time memory and decoding cost.

## Problem and motivation

Query-agnostic eviction compresses a context before future queries are known, allowing one cache to serve multiple requests. Existing methods mainly optimize which original KV pairs survive, but their performance can collapse under aggressive compression because discarded information cannot be represented by the retained subset alone. The authors address this representational gap by asking whether a learned mechanism can synthesize a compact complement to the retained states without increasing the total cache budget (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Context-conditioned restoration:** After full-context prefill, eight learned restore tokens attend causally to the full KV cache in one LoRA-adapted pass and produce new KV states containing information intended to complement the states that survive eviction (§3.2; Fig. 2).

- **Budget-matched plug-in:** Restore states replace an equal number of retained context states, so the final cache has exactly the base method’s KV budget. RestoreKV leaves the importance scorer and eviction rule unchanged (§3.2, Eq. 6).

- **Offline self-distillation:** Restore embeddings and LoRA adapters are trained to match the frozen full-cache model’s answer-token distributions using symmetric KL divergence. The backbone and evictor remain frozen, and budget ratios are sampled during training (§3.3, Eqs. 7–9).

- **One-time adaptation:** LoRA is active only while constructing the restore cache; subsequent queries and decoding use the original backbone (§3.4).

- **Broad empirical evaluation:** The authors test four backbones, four main long-context benchmarks, and five base eviction methods, with additional LongBench, SCBench, learned-scorer, and ablation studies (§4; Appendices A–B).

These results support the authors’ claim that synthesized restore states can substantially reduce compression damage, particularly at tight budgets, without enlarging the query-time cache. They do not establish universal superiority over synthesized-cache methods: the paper explicitly says AM-fast is stronger at one milder LongHealth budget. Nor do matched cache sizes imply identical end-to-end systems cost, because RestoreKV still requires full prefill and a one-time restore pass.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope dependency:** A restore mechanism is trained once for each model and eviction method (§4). Training–inference evictor mismatch becomes severe at \(r=0.05\): a SnapKV-trained checkpoint paired with KVzip scores 42.0 versus 73.2 for matched training (Appendix A.3).

- **Author-stated training sensitivity:** Exposure to aggressive budgets matters; changing the default training range reduces performance at tight ratios (Appendix A.4).

- **Analyst inference — full-cache construction requirement:** RestoreKV reduces the reusable query-time cache but still requires the entire context cache during prefill and restoration. It therefore does not eliminate peak pre-eviction context processing, although measured incremental overhead is small (§§3.2, 4.5).

- **Analyst inference — comparison breadth:** Direct synthesized-cache comparison is limited to AM-fast on Qwen3-4B and the supplied text reports mixed LongHealth outcomes (§4.2). Broader comparisons against the other synthesized representations discussed in §2.2 are not provided.

- **Analyst inference — hardware scope:** Efficiency measurements use one NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 environment, so latency and memory percentages may not transfer unchanged to other accelerators or serving stacks (§4.5; Appendix C.4).

- **Analyst inference — seed reporting:** Main results use seed 0. A three-seed RULER study reports standard deviation below 0.57, but equivalent multi-seed evidence is not supplied for every benchmark, backbone, or evictor (Appendix C.5).

- **Analyst inference — task coverage:** Evaluation is extensive but centered on text-only decoder LLMs and benchmarked QA, retrieval, summarization, code, and synthetic tasks; interactive workloads and non-text modalities are not evaluated (§4.1; Appendix B).

## Open research questions

1. **Can one restoration checkpoint transfer across eviction methods?** Cross-evictor transfer degrades sharply at tight budgets (Appendix A.3). A next experiment could jointly train over several evictors and compare it with per-evictor checkpoints at identical budgets.

2. **Can restoration avoid holding the full cache?** The present restore pass attends to the full pre-eviction cache (§3.2). A streaming or chunkwise restore generator could be tested for peak-memory reduction while measuring accuracy and construction latency.

3. **How should restore capacity adapt to context and budget?** Eight tokens work best in the reported RULER ablation, but gains plateau beyond that (§4.4). A dynamic allocator could choose token counts per layer, head, context, or budget and be compared under a fixed total cache size.

4. **Does restoration generalize to real multi-query serving patterns?** The formulation targets reusable caches, but the supplied evaluations do not characterize production query distributions or cache amortization. A next study could replay diverse query sequences over each compressed context and measure aggregate quality, throughput, and lifetime cost.

## Confidence and missing information
