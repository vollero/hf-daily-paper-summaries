# LLaDA MoE v2: Scaling Mixture-of-Experts Diffusion Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.03457) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03457)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors derive compute-dependent optimization, model–data allocation, and expert-architecture rules for MoE diffusion language models, then use them to train a 30B-parameter model that is competitive with stronger-token-budget baselines on several language benchmarks.

## Problem and motivation

Scaling laws for diffusion language models (dLLMs) have largely been studied with dense architectures, while existing MoE dLLMs mostly inherit design choices from autoregressive (AR) models. The authors argue that this transfer is unreliable because masked diffusion supplies supervision only at masked positions and routes tokens using corrupted bidirectional contexts rather than causal prefixes. They therefore study how MoE dLLMs should scale their batch size, learning rate, model-versus-data compute allocation, routing sparsity, expert granularity, and shared capacity (§1, §3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Optimization scaling:** Joint batch-size and learning-rate sweeps over 158M–3.6B models and \(10^{18}\)–\(3\times10^{20}\) FLOPs produce dLLM-specific power laws, with extrapolation checked at \(6\times10^{20}\) FLOPs (§3.1, Figures 1–2, Appendix A.3).
- **Compute allocation:** IsoFLOP sweeps from \(10^{17}\) to \(10^{20}\) FLOPs estimate how activated non-embedding FLOPs per token and training tokens should grow (§3.2, Figure 3).
- **MoE architecture rules:** Controlled one-factor-at-a-time sweeps study activation ratio, expert granularity, and shared-expert ratio at five reference compute budgets (§3.3, Figure 4, Appendix A.5).
- **Large-scale validation:** The resulting choices guide LLaDA MoE v2, a 30B-A3B masked-diffusion MoE pretrained from scratch on 23.5T tokens and subsequently fine-tuned on 7M instruction–response examples (§4, Appendices B.1–B.3).

The evidence supports empirical scaling recommendations within the tested masked-diffusion MoE setup and shows that the resulting large model performs strongly against the selected baselines. It does not isolate how much downstream improvement comes from the scaling rules rather than the larger architecture, data composition, training schedule, or other implementation choices. Nor does it establish that the fitted laws extrapolate beyond the reported compute and architecture ranges.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated limitation:** Architectural dimensions are varied separately, so interactions among activation ratio, granularity, and shared capacity are not measured (§6).
- **Limited extrapolation:** The optimization fit is checked only to \(6\times10^{20}\) FLOPs, while allocation and architecture sweeps stop at \(10^{20}\) and \(6\times10^{19}\) reference FLOPs, respectively (§§3.1–3.3).
- **Analyst inference:** The large-scale validation is not a controlled ablation between an otherwise identical model designed with versus without the proposed laws. Figure 5 compares models of different total and activated sizes.
- **Analyst inference:** Cross-model benchmark comparability is imperfect because results are preferentially taken from original publications and only missing results use the authors’ unified setup (Appendix B.4).
- **Analyst inference:** Data reproducibility is limited: the source gives broad descriptions of the 23.5T-token corpus and 7M-example SFT set but not their exact composition or release status (Appendices B.2–B.3).
- Compute accounting omits embeddings, the LM head, normalization, nonlinearities, and attention softmax, so the reported \(C=MD\) is an approximation rather than full measured system cost (Appendix A.1).
- No RL, safety evaluation, decoding-speed comparison, contamination analysis, uncertainty estimates, or repeated-run variability is reported in the supplied text.

## Open research questions

1. **Do the three MoE architecture variables interact?** This remains open because the paper changes one dimension at a time. A factorial sweep over activation ratio, granularity, and shared capacity at several compute scales could estimate interaction effects.

2. **How far do the fitted laws extrapolate?** The sweeps cover substantially less compute than the final training run. Training multiple larger configurations around the predicted optima would test whether the exponents remain stable.

3. **Are the laws specific to uniform masking and the chosen router?** The experiments hold the diffusion objective and top-\(k\) routing design largely fixed. Repeating the sweeps with alternative noise schedules or routing mechanisms could measure transferability.

4. **What is the causal value of scaling-guided design at fixed resources?** A matched experiment could train models with identical parameters, tokens, data, and schedules, varying only whether architecture and hyperparameters follow the proposed rules.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text gives extensive methods, fitted equations, settings, and qualitative comparisons, but the numerical contents of several rendered tables—especially Tables 3–6 and 10–13—are missing. Consequently, exact per-benchmark scores, full architecture-sweep grids and losses, fit uncertainty or goodness-of-fit, run-to-run variance, complete corpus composition, and model/code/checkpoint release details cannot be verified from the retrieved text.
