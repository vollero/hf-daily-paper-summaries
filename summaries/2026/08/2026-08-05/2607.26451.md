# ExplainBench: Evaluating Code Explanations from Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.26451) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26451)


## One-sentence takeaway

ExplainBench evaluates coding-agent explanations through automatically validated questions about patch intent and effect, revealing that explanation quality differs from repair success and that execution-based auditing improves explanation scores.

## Problem and motivation

Coding agents increasingly produce large patches that are costly to inspect manually, making their natural-language summaries important for review and trust. The authors identify a gap: existing benchmarks measure whether agents resolve issues, but not whether their explanations accurately describe the intended behavior or the patch’s actual effect. This prevents reliable comparison and systematic improvement of explanations (Sections 1 and 3).

## Approach and contributions

- **ExplainBench:** A benchmark that gives an agent’s explanation to an LLM and measures whether it enables correct answers to validated multiple-choice questions. Its explanation score is the proportion answered correctly (Sections 4.1 and 4.5).
- **Four evaluation dimensions:** Questions cross intent versus actual effect with end-to-end versus function-local behavior. Intent is grounded in developer patches; effect is grounded in agent patches (Sections 4.3–4.4).
- **Execution-grounded construction:** Property-based tests support end-to-end questions, while traced pre/post-patch executions and validated behavioral-delta expressions support local questions (Section 5).
- **Empirical diagnosis:** Five coding agents are evaluated, showing that patch efficacy and explanation quality yield different rankings and that explanations frequently overstate patch correctness (Sections 7.2 and 8.1–8.2).
- **ExplanationAuditAgent:** An independent agent generates tests, compares behavior before and after a patch, inspects call graphs, and revises or confirms explanation claims using execution evidence (Section 6).

The evidence supports the authors’ claims that explanation quality is not interchangeable with patch success, that overconfidence is a prominent failure mode, and that execution-based auditing can improve the benchmark score. It does not establish improved developer trust, readability, satisfaction, or real-world review performance. Because several table bodies are missing from the supplied text, exact agent scores, effect sizes by agent, exclusion counts by category, and most failure-rate breakdowns cannot be verified here.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** The benchmark covers 297 reproducible, traceable issues from open-source projects in SWE-bench Verified, limiting external validity to similar repositories and testing environments (Sections 7.3.2 and 10).
- **Author-stated:** Tracing excludes cases with excessive runtime, traces, CPU use, or serialization-induced fragile states. The authors report no statistically significant composition, difficulty, or patch-size differences after filtering, but the underlying statistics are absent (Section 7.3.2).
- **Author-stated:** Explanation score assesses behavioral alignment, not readability, usefulness, or developer satisfaction (Sections 1 and 10).
- **Author-stated:** LLM scoring is stochastic; five runs and low SEM mitigate but do not remove this dependency (Sections 7.3.3 and 10).
- **Analyst inference:** Treating the developer patch as the oracle for intent may miss alternative valid fixes or specifications; the paper explicitly states the oracle assumption but does not report a sensitivity analysis (Section 4.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Benchmark construction and evaluation depend on specific OpenAI models, while only the ranking is reported as replicated with GPT-5-nano; broader evaluator-model robustness is not established (Sections 7.3.3 and 8.1).
- **Analyst inference:** ExplanationAuditAgent is evaluated using the same benchmark’s test-centered criteria, so its reported gains do not alone demonstrate better human comprehension or calibrated trust (Sections 8.3 and 10).

## Open research questions

1. **Do higher ExplainBench scores help human reviewers detect faulty patches?** The paper does not evaluate developers. A controlled study could compare review accuracy, time, and confidence using original versus audited explanations.

2. **How robust are scores across evaluator models and prompting choices?** Only one alternate evaluator ranking is reported. A next experiment could compare multiple model families, decoding settings, and human labels across the full benchmark.

3. **Can local explanations be improved as much as end-to-end explanations?** Auditing primarily executes end-to-end tests, and the largest stated gains occur there. An experiment could add targeted function-level probes and compare local intent/effect gains against the existing auditor.

4. **Does the developer-patch oracle penalize valid alternative implementations?** No oracle sensitivity analysis is reported. Independent maintainers could annotate behavioral intent and assess whether benchmark answers remain valid across semantically equivalent fixes.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text describes the framework, construction, evaluation setup, and qualitative conclusions in detail, but omits the numerical contents of Tables 1–6. Material missing information includes exact per-agent explanation and efficacy scores, per-category exclusion counts, failure-breakdown percentages, per-agent audit gains, confidence intervals beyond reported SEM, supplementary robustness results, detailed prompts, and complete replication-package contents.
