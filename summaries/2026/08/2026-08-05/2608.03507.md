# ChronoLens: Measuring Language Change Across Time, Languages, and Linguistic Levels

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.03507) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03507)


## One-sentence takeaway

ChronoLens uses feature-aligned crosscoders over frozen multilingual-model representations to compare four linguistic levels across five parliamentary traditions, yielding substantially higher agreement with direct linguistic statistics than dense or pooled-sparse baselines while showing that similar amounts of change can follow different directions.

## Problem and motivation

Computational studies usually measure morphological, syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic change with incompatible representations, datasets, and units, preventing direct comparison of whether these levels evolve together. Existing sparse representations also lack canonical feature alignment across independently trained dictionaries. The authors seek a common space spanning languages, historical periods, and linguistic levels, motivated by the need to separate shared historical patterns from language-, institution-, or corpus-specific change (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Feature-aligned framework:** ChronoLens encodes matched sentences with frozen multilingual language models, then trains crosscoders with a shared sparse feature index and condition-specific encoders/decoders for languages or periods (§4.1; Figure 1).
- **Post-hoc linguistic attribution:** Features are learned without linguistic labels, then assigned a dominant level—morphology, syntax, semantics, or pragmatics—using probes and held-out feature ablations across 23 sentence-level tasks (§4.1).
- **Two-dimensional change measurement:** Normalized Euclidean displacement measures magnitude relative to an initial decade, while cosine similarity between displacement vectors measures directional alignment (§4.2, Equations 3–6).
- **Multilingual diachronic corpus:** The authors construct a unified corpus of 44.98 million documents and approximately 17.2 billion tokens from 22 sources, covering English, German, Italian, Polish, and Turkish during 1803–2026 (§3; Tables 1 and 4).
- **Controlled evaluation:** Crosscoder representations are compared with frozen dense embeddings and a pooled sparse autoencoder on reconstruction, trajectory stability, linguistic agreement, and linguistic specificity (§4.2; Table 2).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Crosscoder features align more closely with direct linguistic changes. | Mean Spearman agreement is \(\rho=0.72\), versus 0.29 for dense embeddings and 0.28 for the pooled SAE; results are averaged over four backbones. | §5.1, Table 2 |
| Crosscoder features are more level-selective. | Linguistic specificity is 0.89, versus 0.74 for embeddings and 0.73 for the pooled SAE. | §5.1, Table 2 |
| The linguistic gains are not explained by much smoother trajectories. | Crosscoder FVU is 0.04 versus 0.07 for the pooled SAE; all three representations have bootstrap trajectory cosines of 0.90–0.92. FVU is undefined for embeddings. | §5.1, Table 2 |
| Full-record change differs by language and temporal profile. | German ends at magnitude 0.89, English at 0.62, Polish at 0.45 after reaching about 0.53 near 1980, and Italian and Turkish both end at 0.53 despite different trajectories and coverage. | §5.2, Figure 2 |
| Rankings depend on the historical interval. | Over the common 1950–2020 window, level-averaged magnitudes are Turkish 0.61, German 0.43, English 0.35, Italian 0.35, and Polish 0.34. | §5.2, Figure 3 |

| Direct indicators show partial coordination but substantial level-specific divergence. | Of 90 language–measure trends over 1950–2020, 37 remain significant after Benjamini–Hochberg correction; mean cross-language cosine is 0.92 for pragmatics but 0.00 for syntax. | Appendix D; Tables 10–11 |
| Several observable changes reverse across languages. | Passive voice decreases significantly in English, German, and Turkish but increases in Polish; long dependencies decrease significantly in English, German, and Italian but increase in Polish. | Appendix D; Table 10 |

The results support the authors’ claim that the aligned representation better tracks their operational linguistic indicators than the two selected baselines, and that magnitude and direction encode distinct aspects of diachronic change. They do not establish causal political, cultural, or institutional explanations, population-wide language change, or generalization beyond the five parliamentary traditions. The genealogical observations are descriptive because only five languages are studied (§5.3; Appendix D.1).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** The four linguistic levels overlap; hard dominant-level assignment prevents double counting but can hide genuinely cross-level features (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** The evidence is restricted to parliamentary discourse in five languages with unequal historical coverage and should not be generalized to entire national populations (Limitations; Broader Impact).
- **Author-stated:** Residual OCR errors, especially before 1949, may mimic change even after filtering (§3; Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** Tokenization, pretraining data, and uneven language coverage of the frozen backbones may affect trajectories; Turkish shows comparatively large between-model spreads (§5.2; Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** Semantic and pragmatic targets use deterministic weak supervision, and probe diagnostics do not establish label correctness across languages or periods; a stratified manual audit remains necessary (Appendix C.5).
- **Author-stated:** Pragmatic features constitute only 0.4–0.6% of assigned features and do not meet the filtered-trajectory feature-count threshold in the reported sensitivity analysis (Appendix C.4).
- **Analyst inference:** Resampling can reuse sentences, with only about 293 distinct sentences per decade condition despite 20,000 sampled tuples; this may limit effective sample diversity even though distinct-instance counts are reported (Appendices C.1 and C.3).
- **Analyst inference:** The main controlled comparison covers only dense embeddings and one pooled-SAE construction, so superiority over other alignment or diachronic representation methods is not established (§4.2).
- **Analyst inference:** The supplied text reports aggregate Table 2 values but omits the table’s model-wise spreads, uncertainty intervals, and significance tests, limiting assessment of baseline-comparison uncertainty.

## Open research questions

1. **Does ChronoLens generalize beyond institutional political discourse?** The paper studies mainly parliamentary speech. A next experiment could train and evaluate the same pipeline on matched newspapers, fiction, conversation, and parliamentary text while testing genre-by-time interactions.

2. **How reliable are the weakly supervised labels across historical periods and languages?** Appendix C.5 explicitly says probe performance does not validate label correctness. A stratified multilingual human audit could estimate precision and recall by language, decade, and linguistic task, then propagate annotation uncertainty into trajectory estimates.

3. **Can overlapping linguistic structure be modeled without hard feature assignment?** The authors acknowledge that dominant-level assignment obscures cross-level features. A next experiment could compare hard assignment with soft multi-level weights and evaluate stability, agreement with direct indicators, and cross-level coupling.

4. **Which social or institutional events explain directional changes?** Feature interventions establish predictive relevance, not historical causation. An event-study design using dated institutional reforms or political transitions, with unaffected measures or corpora as controls, could test specific causal hypotheses.
