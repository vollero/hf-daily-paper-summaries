# SkillJack: Persistent Skill Backdoors in Self-Evolving Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.03509) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03509)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors show that poisoned agent experiences can be compiled into persistent, benign-looking skills that remain routable—and often effective—even after their source records are deleted.

## Problem and motivation

Self-evolving agents transform interaction histories into reusable skills, making untrusted experiences capable of affecting behavior beyond a single retrieval event. Prior poisoning research largely focuses on malicious records influencing an agent when retrieved as context; the authors identify a different lifecycle risk: an agent may itself convert poisoned experience into a separately stored behavioral artifact that survives source cleanup (Sections 1–3.1).

This matters because extraction can obscure malicious intent, promote transient input into reusable capabilities, and break the assumed link between deleting a poisoned record and removing its effects.

## Approach and contributions

- **SkillJack attack:** Inject a poisoned trajectory into the experience corpus without modifying the skill library, extractor, router, or extraction code; the normal pipeline converts it into a persistent skill (Sections 2.2 and 3.1).

- **Transformation-resilient payloads:** Frame policy-violating actions as plausible functionality—such as backup or archival behavior—so extraction preserves the action while producing benign-looking metadata. Paired trajectories use directly malicious wording as a baseline (Section 3.2).

- **Three-property security model:** Formalize sanitization whitewashing, cross-layer promotion, and persistence isolation across the load–transform–persist–route lifecycle (Section 3.1).

- **Cross-system evaluation:** Test SkillX and Anything2Skill on the same 150 trajectories spanning four risk categories, measuring extraction, detectability, routing, persistence, collateral triggering, metadata sensitivity, and preliminary defenses (Sections 4.1–4.10).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Skill extraction sharply reduced detectability in SkillX. | On 65 framed trajectories and their 264 derived skills, pattern detection fell from 44.6% to 11.0%, while LLM-judge detection fell from 98.5% to 11.4%. | Sections 4.2–4.3; Table 2; Figure 2 |
| Whitewashing also appeared in Anything2Skill. | With the same 65 framed trajectories, raw-versus-derived LLM detection was 100.0% versus 44.4% in DOC mode and 100.0% versus 28.6% in SDK mode. DOC and SDK extracted outputs from 41.5% and 10.8% of framed trajectories, respectively. | Sections 4.3–4.4; Table 2; Figure 2 |
| Derived skills remained after source deletion. | Across the same 25 attack-oriented tasks, memory-only triggering was 68.0%, skill-based triggering was 40.0%, and post-deletion triggering was 32.0%; thus 80.0% of skill-based triggers persisted. | Section 4.5; Figure 3 |
| SkillX skills triggered on target and benign tasks. | Native routing over 356 skills—305 poisoned and 51 clean—produced a 56.2% proxy trigger rate on 32 attack-oriented tasks and 20.0% on 15 clean tasks. | Section 4.6; Figure 4 |
| A2S-derived skills were broadly retrievable and triggering. | A lexical retriever over 30 DOC-mode skills produced an 89.2% proxy trigger rate across 65 attack-oriented queries and a 95.0% clean-task retrieval rate. Category trigger rates ranged from 80.0% to 100.0%. | Section 4.7; Table 3 |
| Benign metadata influenced judge decisions. | For sampled SkillX framed skills, detection was 36.7% on content alone but 10.0% when name and documentation accompanied it. For 30 A2S skills, full-skill and content-only detection were 26.7% and 46.7%. | Section 4.8; Figure 5 |
| Defense results were mixed and configuration-sensitive. | On 15 SkillX attack tasks, extraction review reduced triggering to 6.7%, runtime monitoring to 0.0%, but their composition yielded 13.3%. On A2S, original signatures left 85.0% triggering; post-hoc expansion to 54 signatures reduced it to 20.0%, and the combined defense reached 5.0%. | Section 4.9; Table 3 |
| A small generation check found dangerous code patterns. | Pattern matches occurred for 75.0% of four poisoned tasks and 0.0% of two clean tasks; code was generated but not executed. | Section 4.10 |

The evidence establishes that, in the tested pipelines and single-model configuration, skill extraction can reduce proxy detectability, preserve harmful actions, and decouple those actions from their source records. It does not establish real-world compromise rates, universality across models or skill architectures, or robust defense effectiveness. The two downstream ASRs are not directly comparable because SkillX and A2S use different routers, skill banks, and task sets (Section 4.7).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** All experiments use DeepSeek-v4-flash, so cross-model generality is untested (Sections 4.1 and 5.4).
- **Author-stated:** Triggering is primarily a routing-level pattern-plus-LLM proxy, not executed compromise against live services (Sections 4.1, 4.10, and 5.3).
- **Author-stated:** The defense study uses small samples and rule-based monitors; the A2S signature expansion was tuned after observing failures (Section 4.9).
- **Author-stated:** The supplementary execution-oriented check covers only four poisoned and two clean tasks and inspects generated code rather than executing it (Section 4.10).
- **Author-stated:** A2S SDK extracted only 7 of 65 framed trajectories, making its downstream routing result degenerate; primary A2S downstream results therefore use DOC mode (Section 4.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Using the same model for extraction, routing, reasoning, and judging may couple attack behavior to evaluator behavior; the supplied experiments do not isolate this dependence.
- **Analyst inference:** Clean-task evidence is limited—15 tasks for SkillX and 20 in the A2S defense setting—so collateral-trigger estimates have substantial sampling uncertainty.
- **Analyst inference:** Different routing configurations and library compositions prevent interpreting the 56.2% and 89.2% results as a system leaderboard.

## Open research questions

1. **Does SkillJack transfer across model families?** The paper uses one model for every LLM-mediated stage. A next experiment could cross model families for extraction, routing, and judging independently.

2. **How often does routing-level triggering become harmful execution?** Most success measurements are proxies. A controlled sandbox could execute generated actions with instrumented mock services and compare routing, code generation, attempted action, and realized effect.

3. **Can provenance guarantee descendant revocation?** The paper motivates provenance-aware cleanup but does not implement it. A next experiment could maintain a trajectory-to-skill dependency graph and test whether source quarantine reliably revokes all derived and recursively transformed artifacts.

4. **Can defenses generalize without post-hoc vocabulary tuning?** The A2S monitor required signatures added after failures were observed. A preregistered evaluation could compare semantic behavior monitors, static review, and provenance-based controls on held-out payload framings and systems.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported experimental claims and scope because the supplied text includes methods, settings, counts, and major results.

Material missing information includes the full numeric contents of Tables 2–5 and per-category SkillX results; detector prompts and judge calibration; uncertainty intervals or repeated-run variability; detailed trajectory-generation and sampling procedures; complete routing parameters; and independent replication across models, real experience corpora, or executable environments.
