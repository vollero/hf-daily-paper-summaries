# CURV: Enhancing Chart Understanding Through Curriculum Visual Grounded Reasoning

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.02833) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02833)


## One-sentence takeaway

CURV trains multimodal language models to interleave stepwise reasoning with dynamically grounded chart regions, substantially improving synthetic chart QA while transferring more modestly to real-world chart and broader multimodal benchmarks.

## Problem and motivation

Chart question answering (CQA) requires accurate perception of chart elements, logical decomposition, and integration of visual evidence across reasoning steps. The authors argue that existing MLLMs often generate text-centric reasoning disconnected from the relevant image regions and depend on external chain-of-thought prompts or visual cues rather than possessing intrinsic grounded-reasoning abilities (§1–2).

In a 60-example CharXiv analysis, GPT-4o reached 43.33% accuracy with direct answering, 53.33% with reasoning assistance, 61.67% with human-provided visual information, and 86.67% when given both (§2.1, Table 1). The authors interpret this as evidence that both decomposition and perception constrain CQA, though the small sample and oracle visual input limit the strength of that diagnosis.

## Approach and contributions

1. **CURV training framework:** CQA is reformulated as a sequence of paired reasoning steps and visual regions, \((R_t,V_t)\), followed by an answer. Stage I learns reasoning-to-region alignment; Stage II jointly supervises reasoning, grounding, and answer generation while feeding each grounded visual state into the next reasoning step (§3.1–3.2).

2. **Curriculum design:** Training progresses from single-operation, single-chart questions to nested operations and then multi-chart localization or relational reasoning. Complexity is described separately by the number of reasoning steps and the depth of nested functions (§3.3; §C.2–C.3).

3. **Dynamic grounding alternatives:** Applied highlighting, bounding boxes, and cropped views provide different visual feedback mechanisms. The framework also supports explicit coordinate generation, an implicit grounding module, SFT, RL, and combined SFT–RL variants (§3.3; §D; §G.4–G.5).

4. **CCQA dataset and evaluation:** The authors construct a synthetic, template-based curriculum dataset spanning seven chart types, 30 domain categories, 12 operators, three curriculum levels, and five fine-grained tiers. Each reasoning step has a corresponding grounding annotation and binary mask (§4; Tables 4–6).

The evidence establishes consistent improvements across several backbones, chart benchmarks, curriculum levels, and two out-of-domain benchmarks. Ablations also support the value of two-stage curriculum training and explicit grounding.

It does not establish that generated grounding chains are causally faithful explanations of every answer, nor that benefits generalize beyond the tested models, English charts, chart families, and benchmarks. Several evaluations use GPT-4.1-mini as judge, while CCQA training and testing share the same synthetic construction framework. The abstract’s “up to 20.50%” and §5.3’s “20.92%” two-stage improvement also conflict slightly, so concrete table values are more reliable than those summaries.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:**

- CCQA covers seven common chart types but excludes formats such as Sankey diagrams, treemaps, and geographic maps (§I).
- Charts and questions are English-only, leaving multilingual generalization untested (§I).
- Cropped grounding has a resolution–computation trade-off; experiments reduce it to \(128\times128\), versus \(448\times448\) for applied and boxed grounding (§G.2, Table 8).
- Explicit multi-step grounding can accumulate localization and reasoning errors during inference, making it more effective as training supervision than as an inference mechanism (§G.3; §H.1).
- Adding Level 3 training can improve complex reasoning while degrading simpler foundational performance (§G.8).

**Analyst inferences:**

- **Synthetic-to-real validity:** CCQA uses only 210 base chart-structure metadata combinations—30 domain datasets across seven types—with many questions instantiated per image (§4.2, §C.4). Although external benchmarks reduce this concern, template or rendering regularities may contribute to the larger CCQA gains.
- **Evaluation dependence:** External benchmark results use GPT-4.1-mini as the answer judge (Table 3), so reported accuracy may inherit judge error or bias. Rule-based metrics are reported for CCQA but not Table 3.
- **Limited diagnostic scale:** The motivating oracle-assistance experiment contains only 60 CharXiv examples and does not report uncertainty (§2.1).
- **Resource burden:** SFT requires two 80 GB H100 GPUs, RL at least eight, and inference one, under the reported configuration (Table 8).
- **Incomplete reproducibility from the text alone:** The total number of CCQA instances, exact train/test counts, Stage II loss weights, and several RL reward weights are not specified in the supplied text. The paper states that code is available, but artifact contents and licensing are not established here.
- **Metric ambiguity:** The narrative alternates between percentage and absolute-point language, while the abstract and §5.3 disagree on the maximum two-stage gain.

## Open research questions

1. **Does dynamic grounding remain beneficial on substantially different chart distributions?**  
   This remains open because training covers seven synthetic chart families and evaluation excludes several specialized formats (§I). A next experiment could train on current CCQA and test, without adaptation, on manually annotated Sankey, treemap, map, and irregular dashboard questions.

2. **How faithful are predicted grounded regions to the model’s actual decision process?**  
   IoU measures localization agreement, but it does not prove that the highlighted region causally supports the answer (§F.3). A next experiment could mask predicted regions, counterfactually replace chart values, and measure whether answers change in the expected direction.

3. **Can inference avoid cascading grounding errors while retaining explicit visual interaction?**  
   RVA inference underperforms direct answering because early coordinate errors propagate (§G.3; §H.1). A next experiment could compare confidence-gated grounding, beam search over regions, or recovery training against standard RVA on the same 500-sample evaluation.

4. **What curriculum schedule best balances basic and complex reasoning?**  
   Adding Level 3 improves complex performance but can reduce Level 1–2 gains (§G.8). A next experiment could vary replay proportions, difficulty sampling, and anti-forgetting objectives while measuring all three levels and external benchmarks after each stage.
