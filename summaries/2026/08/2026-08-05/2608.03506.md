# When Many Answers Are Valid, Voting Fails: Symbolic Verification for Best-of-K Causal Reasoning in LLMs

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.03506) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03506)


## One-sentence takeaway

CALVER replaces answer-frequency voting with deterministic, reference-free checks of causal validity, substantially improving best-of-\(K\) selection when correct answers are numerous and their probability mass is fragmented.

## Problem and motivation

Self-consistency assumes correct reasoning traces concentrate on one answer, but causal queries may admit many valid adjustment sets, separating sets, or other witnesses. Votes then split among valid outputs while a recurring invalid answer becomes the largest individual mode. The authors argue that generic reward models, confidence scores, and LLM judges do not directly test the causal predicate and therefore miss this structure (Introduction; Related Work, “Inference-time selection”).

The gap is a reference-free selection method that recognizes any member of a query’s full validity class, rather than matching a single listed answer or measuring agreement between candidates.

## Approach and contributions

- **CALVER:** A training-free best-of-\(K\) selector that requires each trace to expose six typed slots—graph, query, strategy, derivation record, computed result, and answer—and scores them using deterministic causal checks. It returns the earliest maximum-scoring candidate without consulting a target answer (Method, “Setting and trace contract” and “Graph-dependent verification”; Table 1).

- **Two verification modes:** With a supplied graph, all candidates are checked against that graph; for graph-from-text tasks, each candidate is checked against its own reconstructed graph and evaluated later against the hidden source graph (Method, “Two modes”).

- **Executable causal predicates:** Checks cover d-/m-separation, backdoor adjustment, intervention reachability, mediator witnesses, and numerical ATE recomputation. A truth-table checker demonstrates the broader candidate-validity principle outside causal reasoning (Method; External transfer).

- **Theory:** The authors prove soundness of a strict ATE certificate under stated assumptions, and show that plurality’s probability of choosing a valid answer can decay exponentially when one invalid answer is individually more probable than every valid answer (Theorems 1–2).

- **Architecture comparison:** The paper contrasts candidate-wise verification with extracting one graph and solving exactly, finding that direct solving is preferable when graph extraction is reliable and candidate-wise verification is competitive when textual interpretations diverge (Figure 3).

These results establish that executable validity checks select better candidates than the tested agreement-based and generic scorers on the reported frozen pools. The transfer studies also support—but do not prove universally—that the principle extends across causal structures, representations, model families, and one logic domain.

The experiments do not establish that CALVER improves candidate generation itself, works without a formal validity predicate, or dominates exact solving when a reliable formal structure can be extracted. The ATE audit reports zero observed false strict certificates, while its general soundness depends on Theorem 1’s graph, positivity, and margin assumptions.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** CALVER only applies when validity is executable. It is less useful when answers are nearly unique, while extract-then-solve is preferable when text-to-graph transcription is reliable (Limitations & Conclusion).

- **Author-stated graph dependence:** In constructed-graph mode, candidates are verified against their own recovered graphs. An omitted query-relevant confounding edge can reverse the decision even if global edge recovery is otherwise strong (Theory, “Why imperfect graph recovery can still suffice”).

- **Author-stated ATE assumptions:** Strict-certificate soundness assumes the supplied DAG and observational distribution are correct, positivity holds, treatment and outcome are binary, and the recomputed effect clears the declared error margin (Theorem 1; Method).

- **Analyst inference—evaluation concentration:** Most causal evaluations use graph predicates and benchmarks where validity is mechanically computable. The K&K experiment broadens the evidence, but one additional formal domain does not establish generality to open-ended reasoning.

- **Analyst inference—checker/evaluator overlap:** On supplied-graph CLEAR, verification and grading deliberately apply the same predicate. The authors mitigate this with graph-from-text, DoVerifier, K&K, and exact-ground-truth ATE studies, but the primary benchmark remains especially favorable to exact symbolic checking (Experiments, “Protocol and evaluation questions”).

- **Analyst inference—missing reproducibility details:** Exact model revisions, adapters, prompts, seeds, reward definitions, and run manifests are deferred to a future public code/data package and are absent from the supplied text. The supplementary proofs, per-policy Table 2 values, detailed controls in Table 4, and some calibration results are also not included here.

## Open research questions

1. **How robust is candidate-wise verification to systematically wrong reconstructed graphs?** The paper shows gains even below 40% exact graph recovery, but does not fully map performance against query-relevant edge errors. A next experiment could inject controlled omissions and reversals of confounding, mediator, and irrelevant edges and measure verifier precision by error type.

2. **Can the method handle approximate or partially decidable validity?** CALVER assumes an executable predicate, excluding many open-ended tasks. A next experiment could compare hard verification with calibrated partial certificates on a task where only some reasoning steps can be checked.

3. **What selection rule is best for imperfect verifiers?** CALVER chooses the earliest maximum-score trace, yet it still introduces 54 errors in the 576-pool decomposition. A next experiment could compare this rule with calibrated score thresholds, ensembles of independent checkers, or abstention while preserving frozen pools.

4. **When should a system switch between extract-then-solve and candidate-wise verification?** Figure 3 reveals a crossover but provides no learned routing policy. A next experiment could train a router using extraction agreement, parse success, and query-local graph uncertainty, then evaluate it on held-out graph families.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the paper’s stated method, assumptions, and reported headline results because the supplied text contains detailed method, theory, and experimental sections.

Materially missing are the supplementary proofs and decompositions; the numerical contents of Tables 1, 2, and 4 beyond their surrounding descriptions; per-policy and per-network results; exact prompts, seeds, model revisions, adapters, and reward definitions; the declared ATE tolerance value; detailed calibration results; and confirmation that the promised code/data package is actually available.
