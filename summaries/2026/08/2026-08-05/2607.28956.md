# MerchantBench: Benchmarking LLM Agents for Long-Term Coherence in E-Commerce Operations

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.28956) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28956)


## One-sentence takeaway

MerchantBench finds that current LLM agents struggle to sustain and adapt coherent e-commerce strategies over a simulated year, with the best configuration reaching only 27.3% of novice human participants’ mean final net assets.

## Problem and motivation

Most agent benchmarks use bounded tasks with immediate success signals, whereas real deployments may require policies to remain purposeful across long horizons, persistent state changes, and delayed feedback. The authors call this capability **Long-Term Coherence**.

Seller-side e-commerce exposes this gap because sourcing, pricing, cash management, and risk response are interdependent: orders consume cash immediately, but supplier problems, refunds, reviews, and settlement outcomes may surface much later. Existing vending and retail environments do not jointly provide order-level delayed outcomes and a large catalog with changing annual demand (Introduction).

## Approach and contributions

- **Persistent benchmark:** MerchantBench formulates store operation as a partially observable decision process covering 365 control days, 8,760 hourly steps, and agent decisions every 12 hours (Task Formulation).

- **Data-grounded simulator:** Its catalog contains 98,843 products from 36,576 suppliers across ten categories, with 365-day demand histories and date-aligned market reports derived from 1688 data (Real-World Data Grounding; Appendix B).

- **Order-level dynamics:** Individual orders progress through procurement, shipping, delivery, and settlement, with three supplier-event types and six abnormal downstream outcomes becoming visible at different delays (Upstream Supplier Simulation; Order Lifecycle).

- **Standardized agent interface:** Agents receive partial observations and operate through 26 merchant tools covering sourcing, listing and pricing, cash flow, and supplier/order monitoring (Agent Interface; Table 2).

- **Comparative evaluation:** The study runs eight LLMs with ReAct and Hermes, using three repetitions per pairing—48 LLM runs in total—and compares them with a rule-based system and three novice human participants (Experimental Setup; Appendix J).

The results establish performance differences within this simulator and identify recurring trace patterns associated with weak outcomes. They do **not** establish that activity, framework features, or seasonal reallocation causally improve performance: those claims rely largely on averages, associations, and selected trajectories rather than controlled interventions.

## Limitations and validity risks

The supplied conclusion does not contain a dedicated author-stated limitations discussion. Material risks visible in the study design are therefore mostly analyst inferences:

- **Analyst inference—small replication count:** Each LLM/framework result uses only three stochastic runs, and several outcomes have high variability; this limits confidence in rankings (Experimental Setup; Figure 4).

- **Analyst inference—limited human baseline:** The comparison uses three participants, each without prior e-commerce operating experience, over five calendar days. It does not establish performance relative to experienced merchants or a broader human population (Appendix J).

- **Analyst inference—single simulated market:** Evaluation is restricted to one dropshipping environment, ten categories, and data from one Chinese wholesale platform; transfer to other markets or operational models is untested (Real-World Data Grounding; Order Lifecycle).

- **Analyst inference—simulator validity:** Demand, exposure, ratings, supplier events, and order outcomes follow specified synthetic mechanisms calibrated from platform signals. The supplied text does not report external validation showing that full simulated business trajectories reproduce real merchant behavior.

- **Analyst inference—framework confounding:** Hermes adds code execution, memory, planning, skill management, and other capabilities beyond the shared 26 tools. The reported Hermes–ReAct difference does not isolate which capability causes an improvement (Experimental Setup; Tables 5–8).

- **Analyst inference—trace-selection risk:** Several strategic-failure claims use representative single-run episodes. The text does not give prevalence estimates or systematic annotation reliability for these qualitative categories (Order-Level Risk Propagation; Strategic Coherence).

- **Reproducibility uncertainty:** Detailed parameters and observable fields are supplied, but the retrieved text does not establish whether simulator code, processed data, seeds, prompts for every run, or evaluation traces are publicly released.

## Open research questions

1. **Which agent component produces Hermes’s average advantage?**  
   This remains open because Hermes changes code, memory, planning, summarization, and skill support simultaneously. A factorial ablation could enable each component separately for the same models and seeds.

2. **Can explicit temporal memory prevent activity decay and premature abandonment?**  
   The paper identifies memory errors and shrinking intervention but does not test a targeted remedy. A next experiment could compare structured event ledgers, scheduled follow-ups, and objective-state checkpoints against ordinary context summarization.

3. **How robust are model rankings across markets and simulator assumptions?**  
   Results come from one platform-derived catalog and one configured operating model. Replication could vary regions, categories, fulfillment models, rating dynamics, demand elasticity, and event-delay distributions.

4. **Do the proposed coherence diagnostics predict real operational competence?**  
   SWR, demand alignment, and sourcing gain correlate with simulated outcomes, but external validity is untested. A prospective study could compare these measures with performance in replayed historical episodes or controlled live-store trials.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the benchmark design and reported aggregate results because the supplied text includes the main paper, tables, and extensive appendices; **medium** for broader interpretation because causal and external-validity evidence is limited.

Material missing information includes public-release status and access instructions; exact random seeds; inferential statistics or confidence intervals for most comparisons; quantitative correlation coefficients for Figures 8 and 10; systematic prevalence and annotation procedures for trace-level failure modes; compute, token, latency, and monetary costs; contamination safeguards; experienced-merchant results; and external validation of the simulator against real store trajectories.
