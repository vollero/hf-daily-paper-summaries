# Human-Centric Intelligence in the Era of Foundation Models: A Survey

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.18184) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18184)


## One-sentence takeaway

The survey proposes a six-level taxonomy that connects human appearance and geometry, motion and interaction, and world simulation and embodied agency, while organizing their data, architectures, optimization strategies, and evaluation resources in the foundation-model era.

## Problem and motivation

Research on human-centric intelligence is fragmented across tasks, modalities, and communities, obscuring conceptual and methodological connections. The authors argue that prior surveys typically focus on individual areas—such as pose estimation, action recognition, motion generation, or human–object interaction—and rarely treat foundation-model developments as their central concern. Their goal is therefore to provide a full-spectrum framework spanning human perception through executable embodied behavior (Abstract; Sec. 1, “Comparison with Other Human-Centric Surveys” and “Scope and Contributions”).

## Approach and contributions

- **Human-context taxonomy:** Organizes the literature into six interconnected levels—visual appearance, spatial geometry, kinematic dynamics, interaction modeling, world simulation, and embodied agency—grouped under humans as observable subjects, dynamic actors, and situated agents (Sec. 2; Fig. 2).
- **Methodological synthesis:** Defines five signal families, four computational architecture paradigms, seven training mechanisms, and five inference-optimization mechanisms (Secs. 3.1–3.3; Figs. 4–5).
- **Cross-area review:** Surveys representative methods across perception, generation, reconstruction, motion, interaction, simulation, planning, and control (Secs. 4–6; Tables 1–4).
- **Evaluation and resource organization:** Collects representative datasets, benchmarks, and commonly used metrics, and reports an externally maintained human-centric AI resource collection (Abstract; Sec. 1; Sec. 7).

The evidence establishes a broad organizational synthesis and documents the reported configurations and resources of prior work. It does not establish that the proposed taxonomy improves model performance, that larger models or datasets causally produce better results, or that methods listed in different tables are directly comparable. No survey-level meta-analysis, controlled experiment, or aggregate quantitative comparison is visible in the supplied text.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Source limitation:** The supplied paper is explicitly truncated by 109,265 characters. Parts of Sec. 7, the open-challenges section, conclusion, and portions of the references are unavailable, so this digest cannot provide complete coverage.
- **Author-stated scope boundary:** The survey excludes human–computer interaction, human-oriented language modeling, and policy studies of human-centered AI (Sec. 1).
- **Author-stated taxonomy boundary:** The six contexts overlap; multi-context methods are assigned by their principal modeling target and evaluation objective, introducing judgment into categorization (Sec. 2).
- **Recurring author-identified risks:** The visible text highlights privacy and demographic concerns, expensive geometric acquisition, sensor calibration and transfer problems, embodiment mismatch, incomplete physical grounding, and the gap between perceptual plausibility and actionability (Secs. 3.1, 4–7).
- **Analyst inference:** Tables combine methods with different modalities, datasets, scales, objectives, and evaluation settings; their entries support mapping the field but not performance ranking.
- **Analyst inference:** The visible excerpts provide no documented systematic-review protocol, search strategy, inclusion criteria, quality assessment, or coverage statistics. These may exist in omitted material, so their absence cannot be asserted for the complete paper.
- **Analyst inference:** The externally maintained resource collection is announced, but its contents, license, versioning, and reproducibility procedures are not available in the supplied text.

## Open research questions

1. **How can semantic foundation-model priors be tied to metrically accurate and physically valid human geometry?** The survey repeatedly notes that perceptual plausibility does not ensure geometric accuracy. A next experiment could compare semantic-only, geometry-only, and jointly constrained models on matched reconstruction and physical-validity measures.

2. **Do unified models preserve specialist performance as task coverage expands?** The survey identifies a tension between generality and task-specific precision but supplies no controlled cross-task study. A next experiment could add task families incrementally to a shared backbone and measure transfer, interference, and calibration on fixed benchmarks.

3. **Which world-model properties predict planning utility?** The authors distinguish convincing future generation from actionable consequence prediction. A next experiment could evaluate the same models on perceptual fidelity, intervention consistency, uncertainty calibration, and downstream closed-loop task success.

4. **Which representations transfer reliably across human and robot embodiments?** The survey describes latent actions, retargeting, and embodiment alignment, while noting uncertain generality. A next experiment could pretrain several action abstractions on identical human videos and test transfer across multiple robot morphologies and control spaces.

## Confidence and missing information
