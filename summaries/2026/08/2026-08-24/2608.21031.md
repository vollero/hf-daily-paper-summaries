# PhysCaP: Grounding Code-as-Policy Agent with Physics-Informed Exploration

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.21031) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21031)


## One-sentence takeaway

PhysCaP adds proprioception-based mass and stiffness measurement plus separate planning and prioritization agents to code-as-policy robotics, reducing exploratory interactions on small tabletop tasks while generally maintaining or improving success.

## Problem and motivation

Robotic policies often act from passive visual observations, which cannot reveal latent properties such as mass, stiffness, or hidden contents. The authors argue that existing vision-language-action policies lack explicit information-seeking behavior, reinforcement learning can be sample-inefficient and difficult to interpret, and prior code-as-policy agents lack mechanisms for deciding what physical information to acquire and when to stop exploring (Sections 1–2).

The target gap is therefore task-level active perception: integrating physical measurement, exploration planning, stopping decisions, and manipulation within one executable agent.

## Approach and contributions

- **Training-free physical measurement:** `get_mass` estimates mass from differential joint torques and the vertical Jacobian after a fixed 15 cm lift; `get_stiffness` estimates deformation at normalized motor effort 0.50 and maps it to five calibrated levels (Sections 3.1.1–3.1.2).
- **Dual-agent exploration:** a Planner identifies missing information and stops exploration once evidence is sufficient, while a Prioritizer uses visual heuristics to rank candidates and avoid implausible or redundant interactions (Sections 3.2–3.3).
- **Executable integration:** a CaP-Agent0-based coding agent invokes measurement, visual localization, and robot-control APIs in a closed loop (Sections 3.1.3 and Appendix F).
- **Real and simulated evaluation:** the system is tested on three real-world tabletop tasks and a simulated LIBERO empty-container task, including comparisons with passive CaP, interactive variants, and three VLA models (Sections 4.1–4.7).

These results support the authors’ claim that explicit physical exploration can reduce unnecessary interactions in the evaluated scenarios. They do not establish broad manipulation generalization: the real evaluation contains only three designed tasks, ten trials per task, one robot setup, and highly structured candidate sets. Because OI and time exclude failed episodes, efficiency figures cannot be interpreted independently of success rates. No uncertainty or significance analysis is supplied for SR or OI.

## Limitations and validity risks

The authors identify three principal limitations (Section 5):

- Commercial VLM APIs introduce variable latency and reasoning behavior.
- Single-camera 2D localization and depth back-projection propagate localization errors into 3D manipulation.
- PiPER communication latency can make executed trajectories diverge from generated code.

Analyst inferences supported by the text:

- **Narrow evaluation:** real-world evidence covers three tabletop tasks, ten trials each, four or fewer candidates, and basic pick-and-place actions (Sections 4.1–4.2).
- **Heuristic dependence:** prioritization relies on intentionally informative cues—cup size, open cans with straws, and avocado color—which may not remain reliable under distribution shift (Section 4.2; Appendix A.1).
- **Success-conditioned efficiency:** OI and execution time are averaged only over successful episodes, potentially favoring methods whose failures terminate early or are excluded (Sections 4.1 and 4.7).
- **Incomplete estimator reporting:** Figure 4 is described qualitatively, but the supplied text gives no mass error, stiffness classification accuracy, confidence interval, or comparison against alternative estimators.
- **Limited ablation:** the paper compares merged versus separate agents and replaces hardware mass with an oracle, but does not report isolated Prioritizer removal under otherwise identical ordering or controlled alternatives to its visual heuristics.
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Appendix Table 3 contains a malformed GPT-5.6 execution-time value (“231.83.0±39.63 s”), so that comparison cannot be interpreted precisely.

## Open research questions

1. **Do the prioritization heuristics generalize when appearance and physical state conflict?** The evaluated scenes deliberately correlate visual cues with plausibility. A next experiment could randomize straws, packaging state, fruit color, and container size independently of mass, stiffness, or contents.

2. **How accurate and stable are the PhysX estimators across poses, objects, and robots?** The paper reports test ranges but no aggregate estimator errors. A next experiment could measure absolute and relative mass error and stiffness confusion matrices across grasp poses, grippers, materials, and robot platforms.

3. **Does dual-agent decomposition remain beneficial after controlling model calls and prompt budgets?** The merged variant performs worse, but the source does not isolate context length, latency, or sampling variability. A next experiment could compare matched-token, matched-call, repeated-seed variants of merged and separate agents.

4. **Can exploration optimize expected information gain rather than hand-specified visual plausibility?** The Prioritizer uses heuristic scores rather than a calibrated uncertainty objective. A next experiment could compare it with learned or Bayesian information-gain selection under controlled interaction costs.
