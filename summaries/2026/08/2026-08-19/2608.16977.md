# The Problem Is the Problem: Towards Scalable Mathematical Discovery

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.16977) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16977)


## One-sentence takeaway
The authors propose FAR, a literature-to-review cascade that retrieves, attempts, and triages open mathematical problems, yielding 77 potentially publishable artifacts from 4,717 attempted combinatorics conjectures, though only 15 received human mathematical review.

## Problem and motivation
Most AI-for-mathematics systems begin after a human has selected a theorem or conjecture, leaving problem selection and final expert review as bottlenecks. Because frontier-model reasoning and mathematical review are scarce, the authors argue that discovery should be treated as an effort-allocation problem: humans specify a research direction, while AI searches the literature for suitable problems and concentrates review on promising outputs (Sections 1–2; Figure 1).

The identified gap is a scalable, source-grounded workflow spanning literature search, open-status checking, problem solving, and recommendation—not merely solving a preselected problem.

## Approach and contributions
- **Direction-to-problem discovery:** FAR’s **Find** stage labels papers by research direction, extracts unresolved statements with source context, and checks whether they appear well posed and still open (Sections 3.1 and 4.1).
- **Large-scale resolution attempts:** Its **Attempt** stage gives every retained conjecture one model-agent run, classifying the outcome as known, new, defective, or unresolved (Section 3.2).
- **Review-oriented triage:** Its **Recommend** stage uses three independent judges followed by novelty/significance grading; only artifacts judged correct and graded as standalone-publishable proceed to expert review (Sections 3.3 and 4.1).
- **Effort-allocation formulation:** The authors formalize selecting at most \(B\) conjectures as constrained optimization under three objectives: artifact count, total importance, and maximum importance. They derive exact ranking strategies when success probabilities are known for the first two objectives and a \(1-1/e\) greedy approximation for the third (Sections 4.3.2, pp. 9–10).
- **Combinatorics pilot and mathematical results:** The pipeline processes 51,110 papers into 4,717 attempted conjectures and 77 recommended artifacts. The authors manually review 15, including proofs, counterexamples, and answers to open-ended questions (Sections 4.1–5; Appendix C).

The evidence establishes that FAR can create a steep literature-to-review funnel and that score-based allocation retrospectively outperformed uniform sampling on this pilot’s outcomes. It does not establish that all 77 recommended artifacts are correct or novel, that the measured improvements generalize beyond this corpus and model stack, or that FAR outperforms other end-to-end discovery systems: only 15 artifacts were human-reviewed, and no competing pipeline evaluation is reported in the supplied text.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope dependence:** Allocation results depend on the scoring model, literature sources, and models used throughout the cascade (Section 4.3.2, p. 11).
- **Limited human validation:** Only 15 of 77 recommended artifacts were reviewed, selected by author interest. The correctness and novelty of the remaining 62 are not established (Sections 4.2 and 5).
- **Status-search failures:** One reviewed artifact duplicated a result published four months earlier, and another reused a known finite-geometry construction in a new connection. Automated judging therefore does not certify novelty (Sections 5.1–5.2).
- **Single domain and run:** The empirical evaluation is one combinatorics pilot with one attempt per conjecture; broader mathematical domains, repeated attempts, and adaptive bandit allocation remain untested (Sections 2.3, 4.1).
- **Analyst inference—selection bias:** Because the 15 reviewed artifacts were chosen by interest, their 15/15 correctness rate cannot estimate precision across all 77 recommendations.
- **Analyst inference—retrospective allocation risk:** Cross-validation prevents each item’s own outcome from entering its fitted score model, but all comparisons still reuse outcomes from the same corpus and pipeline. An independent prospective run would provide stronger evidence.
- **Analyst inference—baseline coverage:** The supplied text compares allocation policies mainly with uniform random selection, not alternative retrieval, solving, judging, or human-led prioritization workflows.
- **Source truncation:** The supplied paper text omits 121,595 characters from the middle of Appendix C. Consequently, coverage of the reviewed mathematical results and their detailed validation is incomplete.

## Open research questions
1. **How precise is automated recommendation across all 77 artifacts?** Only 15 interest-selected items were reviewed. A blinded, randomly sampled expert audit of all recommendations—or a statistically powered subset—could estimate correctness and novelty precision.
2. **Do FAR and its allocation strategies generalize beyond combinatorics?** The only reported pilot uses the direction “combinatorics.” A matched run in another mathematical field could compare funnel retention, judge precision, and artifacts per unit of compute.
3. **Can adaptive allocation improve on one attempt per conjecture?** The paper interprets problems as bandit arms but evaluates only single pulls. A prospective experiment could allocate an initial exploration round and then adaptively assign additional attempts using observed outcomes.
4. **How robust are scores and rankings across models and corpora?** The authors acknowledge model/source dependence. Repeating the pilot with different scoring models, literature collections, and temporal cutoffs could measure rank stability and out-of-distribution performance.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly documents the pipeline, funnel counts, score analysis, allocation results, and selected examples. However, the source is truncated inside Appendix C, so many reviewed proofs and their supporting details are unavailable. Also missing are complete cost and compute accounting, wall-clock performance, full expert-review protocols, results for the 62 unreviewed recommendations, independent reproduction evidence, comparisons with other end-to-end discovery systems, and prospective validation of the learned allocation policies.
