# DiSCO: Defending text-to-image generation through distribution-guided contrastive prompt optimization

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.17067) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17067)


## One-sentence takeaway

DiSCO uses black-box image feedback and contrastive suffix search to reduce unsafe text-to-image generations substantially, though its default search requires hundreds of candidate generations and can occasionally distort prompt semantics.

## Problem and motivation

The paper addresses “benign adversarial” prompts: text that passes language-level safety checks but still causes unsafe image generation because of the target model’s learned output distribution. The authors argue that prompt rewriting alone cannot reliably address this mismatch, while defenses that edit weights, attention, or inference internals are unsuitable for proprietary models (Sections 1 and 2.1).

The identified gap is therefore a portable defense that uses only observable generations, works with both closed and open models, and preserves the original request while steering outputs away from unsafe regions.

## Approach and contributions

- **Problem formulation:** A prompt is defined as benign adversarial when it passes text-level assessment but produces unsafe images with probability at least 0.5 (Equation 1, Section 2.1).

- **Model-specific reference distributions:** DiSCO queries the target generator on non-adversarial I2P prompts and constructs safe and unsafe image pools. By default, an image enters a pool only when NudeNet and Q16 agree on its label (Section 2.2; Figure 2).

- **Contrastive prompt optimization:** Candidate generations are embedded with a frozen CLIP image encoder and scored by their mean similarity to sampled safe references minus their mean similarity to unsafe references (Equation 5).

- **Suffix search:** LLaMA-3-8B proposes suffix tokens, while beam search retains the candidates with the highest contrastive scores. Defaults are beam width \(K=4\), suffix length \(T=16\), and eight references from each pool per scoring step (Sections 2.2 and 4).

- **Adaptive deployment:** DiSCO is normally invoked only after an initial generation is classified as unsafe; safe initial generations pass through unchanged (Section 2.2).

- **Empirical contribution:** The paper evaluates the method across four generators, four existing defenses, four principal attacks, multiple safety evaluators, quality metrics, and several ablations (Sections 4 and A.2–A.10).

The evidence supports broad empirical improvements across the tested generators, attacks, defenses, and evaluators. The held-out ShieldGemma2 and T2I-RiskyPrompt studies reduce concerns about direct detector-boundary exploitation and I2P-only overfitting.

It does not establish robustness against a fully adaptive attacker designed with knowledge of DiSCO, universal effectiveness on arbitrary proprietary systems, or human-judged preservation of intent. Metrics based on automated classifiers and CLIP/ImageReward also do not prove that every resulting image is safe or semantically faithful.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated limitation:** Suffixes can inject visually dominant concepts—such as common animals or animated characters—that override the original content or style. The authors propose adding an explicit semantic-preservation constraint (Section A.11; Figure 5).

- **Author-stated limitation:** The method incurs substantial inference overhead. Its default configuration generates 256 candidate images and takes roughly 146–270 seconds per optimized prompt on an A100, although selective activation, caching, and coarse candidate rendering can reduce the burden (Sections 2.2 and A.10).

- **Author-stated limitation:** The APT evaluation uses the authors’ reimplementation because no official implementation was available, and APT is not adaptive to DiSCO. The paper explicitly treats this as a proof-of-concept stress test (Section A.9).

- **Author-stated scope:** Experiments use a binary safe/unsafe partition. Multi-level, audience-specific safety preferences and weight calibration are proposed but not evaluated (Section A.7).

- **Analyst inference:** Reference pools depend on classifier judgments. Held-out ShieldGemma2 results mitigate circularity concerns, but they do not eliminate shared evaluator blind spots or establish agreement with human moderation decisions (Sections A.2 and A.6).

- **Analyst inference:** Several ablations are narrow: pool and search studies focus on SD 1.4, and the reduced-denoising-step test uses only Ring-A-Bell against SD 1.4 (Sections 4.3 and A.10). Their conclusions may not transfer uniformly to other backbones and attacks.

- **Analyst inference:** The paper says DiSCO is applicable to any text-to-image system, but evaluations cover four named generator families/configurations; unrestricted portability remains an author claim rather than an exhaustive result (Sections 4 and 5).

## Open research questions

1. **How robust is DiSCO to a fully adaptive attacker?**  
   The APT stress test does not optimize against DiSCO itself (Section A.9). A next experiment could jointly optimize an attack suffix against the complete pool construction, scoring, and beam-search pipeline.

2. **Can semantic drift be constrained without sacrificing safety?**  
   Dominant suffix concepts occasionally override the initial prompt (Section A.11). A suitable experiment would add a text-embedding or human-preference constraint and measure ASR alongside human-rated intent preservation.

3. **Can the query cost be reduced across all architectures and attacks?**  
   Coarse scoring was validated only for Ring-A-Bell on SD 1.4 (Section A.10). Future work could test early stopping, surrogate ranking, or fewer candidates across every model–attack combination while tracking ASR and latency.

4. **How should safety preferences be calibrated for different deployments?**  
   The multi-pool weighted objective is only proposed conceptually (Section A.7). A next study could construct graded reference pools, calibrate weights for distinct policies, and evaluate both moderation accuracy and demographic or cultural consistency.
