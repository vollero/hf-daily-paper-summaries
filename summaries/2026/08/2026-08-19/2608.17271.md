# ASI-Bench: At the Dawn of Artificial Superintelligence

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.17271) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17271)


## One-sentence takeaway

ASI-Bench evaluates end-to-end scientific agents under progressively reduced methodological guidance and finds that average performance across 18 agent–model configurations falls sharply when detailed procedures are removed, suggesting that operationalizing methods is a larger bottleneck than selecting them.

## Problem and motivation

Most existing evaluations test known-answer problems or workflows substantially specified by humans, providing limited evidence about whether AI can independently choose methods, execute long-horizon research, and produce verifiable artifacts. The authors identify a gap in jointly evaluating cross-domain generality, methodological autonomy, end-to-end execution, and sensitivity to guidance within matched versions of the same research project (Sections 1 and 2.1; Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Matched guidance gradient:** Each project has four conditions with fixed objectives, data, outputs, and scoring: B1 supplies detailed procedures, B2 supplies method-level guidance, B3 removes methodological guidance, and B4 adds plausible but irrelevant information to B3 (Sections 2.2 and Appendix D).
- **Project-level benchmark:** The benchmark contains 60 executable research tasks across 11 scientific domains, requiring multi-stage investigation and verifiable artifacts rather than isolated answers (Sections 1 and 2.2).
- **Construction and validation pipeline:** Tasks underwent expert development, AI-assisted auditing, five human-review rounds, sandbox execution, scorer checks, and screening for leakage and shortcuts (Section 2.2).
- **Agent–model evaluation:** Eighteen configurations were compared without external tool access, generally over three independent runs, using task-macro-averaged scores and computational-cost measurements (Table 2; Section 3.2).

These results establish performance and cost differences for the evaluated configurations, tasks, harnesses, and guidance formulations. They do not establish that the benchmark score directly measures superintelligence, that the inferred bottleneck is causal, or that the findings generalize to scientific problems outside the 60-task collection. The source also does not report statistical significance tests for the aggregate comparisons.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope limitation:** The authors acknowledge that no fixed benchmark or single team can represent the breadth of meaningful, verifiable scientific problems; the current 60 tasks are described as a starting point (Section 4; Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference — construct validity:** The paper interprets low-guidance scores as evidence about autonomy and higher-order intelligence, but supplies no external validation showing how these scores correspond to real-world scientific discovery.
- **Analyst inference — B2 ambiguity:** B2 is summarized as specifying “only the method,” yet the Appendix D example provides a PDE class, physical structure, and several reasonable solver families rather than one method name. Variation in how B2 is instantiated could complicate the claim that B2 cleanly isolates method operationalization.
- **Analyst inference — limited uncertainty analysis:** Most configurations have three runs and one has only a single run; no confidence intervals or significance tests are reported (Table 2).
- **Analyst inference — restricted operating setting:** Main results exclude external tool access, so they may not transfer to research agents using retrieval, specialized scientific tools, or external services (Table 2).
- **Analyst inference — incomplete reproducibility detail in supplied text:** The text describes public benchmark infrastructure and task packages but does not provide the full task inventory, scorer formulas, model parameters, hardware, or complete cost methodology.

## Open research questions

1. **Does the B1–B3 gap truly isolate procedural operationalization?** It remains open because B2 guidance may vary in specificity across tasks. A next experiment could annotate guidance content and compare multiple standardized B2 variants ranging from a method name to a structured but nonprocedural description.

2. **How stable are system rankings across domains and repeated runs?** Only aggregate results and mostly three-run standard deviations are supplied. A next experiment could use more repetitions and report domain-stratified confidence intervals and rank stability.

3. **Do benchmark gains predict successful real-world research?** The paper does not validate scores against independent expert assessments of novelty, correctness, or practical scientific value. A next experiment could compare ASI-Bench performance with blinded expert evaluation on held-out research projects.

4. **How does external tool access affect autonomy and cost?** The main evaluation explicitly excludes it. A controlled follow-up could rerun matched configurations with retrieval and domain tools while holding tasks, budgets, and scoring fixed.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the benchmark design and reported aggregate results because the supplied text includes the relevant sections, tables, figures, and appendix example.

Material missing information includes the complete task list and domain distribution, per-task and per-domain results, exact scorer definitions and score scale, model/harness implementation details, hardware, token and monetary-cost calculation procedures, contamination checks beyond the described review process, statistical significance testing, and external validation of the benchmark’s autonomy construct.
