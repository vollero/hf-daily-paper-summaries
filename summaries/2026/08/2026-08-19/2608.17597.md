# HarnessRisk: A Lifecycle-Oriented Benchmark for Agent Harness Safety

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.17597) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17597)


## One-sentence takeaway

HarnessRisk shows that agent safety depends strongly on the deployed model–harness combination: high task completion and even explicit risk detection can coexist with substantial attack success across the harness lifecycle.

## Problem and motivation

Agent harnesses mediate tools, permissions, extensions, persistent state, and external actions, so failures can occur beyond ordinary runtime prompt injection. The authors argue that prior benchmarks emphasize runtime operation and action control while inconsistently covering configuration, capability extension, persistence, and incident recovery (Introduction; Table 1, pp. 1–2). This makes it difficult to compare safety failures across harness responsibilities or determine whether safety belongs to the model alone or to the full deployed configuration.

## Approach and contributions

- **Lifecycle framework:** Organizes harness safety into six phases—Harness Configuration, Capability Extension, Runtime Operation, State Persistence, Action Control, and Incident Recovery (Section 2.2, p. 4).
- **HarnessRisk benchmark:** Provides 128 approximately balanced, sandboxed cases. Each combines a benign objective with an adversarial instruction embedded in an untrusted workflow artifact and unfolds over three owner turns (Sections 3.1–3.2, pp. 4–5; Table 4, p. 16).
- **Multidimensional evaluation:** Separately scores binary Utility, Attack Success Rate (ASR), Persistence, and Detection using complete trajectory evidence and a GPT-5.4 evaluator (Section 3.3, pp. 5–6).
- **Configuration-level study:** Evaluates six models in three harnesses, totaling 14 model–harness configurations with three sampling seeds per case (Section 4.1, p. 7).
- **Released evaluation infrastructure:** The authors report releasing cases, adapters, mock services, evaluator prompts, analysis scripts, configuration metadata, and archived trajectories (Appendix H, p. 20).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Safety varies widely despite generally high utility. | Across 14 configurations, ASR spans **12.6%–80.87%** and Utility spans **75.0%–97.6%**; each configuration nominally covers 128 cases × 3 seeds. | Table 2 and Section 4.1, p. 7 |
| Useful execution is often unsafe. | Useful-but-unsafe trajectories constitute **59%** on OpenClaw, **38%** on Nanobot, and **43%** on Hermes; useful-and-safe shares are **36%**, **51%**, and **53%**, respectively. | Figure 4 and Section 4.3, p. 8 |
| The same model’s measured safety changes substantially with its harness. | GLM-5.2 has **54.7% ASR** on OpenClaw versus **12.6%** on Nanobot, a reported **4.3×** difference; DeepSeek-V4-Pro ranges from **37.3%** on Nanobot to **65.4%** on Hermes. | Table 2 and Section 4.3, p. 8 |
| Detection correlates with lower ASR but does not ensure containment. | Across 12 shared configurations, Detection–ASR correlations are Pearson **r = −0.708, p = 0.010** and Spearman **ρ = −0.769, p = 0.003**. After adding model and harness indicators, the Detection coefficient is not significant (**p = 0.101**). | Figure 5, p. 9; Appendix E, p. 19 |
| Very high detection can coexist with substantial attack success. | MiniMax M3/OpenClaw records **97.9% Detection and 31.2% ASR**; GLM-5.2/OpenClaw records **92.2% Detection and 54.7% ASR**. | Table 2, p. 7; Section 4.3, p. 9 |
| Harness Configuration is the most vulnerable phase. | It has the highest mean ASR on every harness when phase ASR is macro-averaged across the four models common to all three harnesses; intervals use 10,000 bootstrap samples. | Figure 6, p. 9; Appendix D.3, p. 18 |
| Evaluator agreement is substantial but imperfect. | Utility: **92.5%, κ=0.83**, and ASR: **89.7%, κ=0.77**, against deterministic labels on 360 trajectories. Persistence: **84.3%, κ=0.65**, and Detection: **85.7%, κ=0.69**, against adjudicated human labels on 300 trajectories. | Table 3 and Section 4.2, pp. 7–8 |

The evidence establishes broad variation across the tested deployed configurations, separation between utility and safety, and recurring vulnerability at configuration time. It does not establish that a harness alone causes the differences: prompts, tool surfaces, state handling, and models were not controlled independently. The correlations are descriptive, and neither risk detection nor low ASR should be interpreted as proof of effective containment.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:**

- Failed or unparsable runs are excluded; because provider failures may not be random, estimates are conditional on valid runs (Appendix D.1 and G, pp. 18–19).
- Harnesses differ simultaneously in system prompts, tools, and state management, preventing harness-only causal attribution (Appendix G, p. 19).
- Persistence and Detection depend on exported state and visible language, which differ across harnesses (Appendix G, pp. 19–20).
- Low ASR can reflect safe refusal, failure to reach a tool, or general task failure; Persistence and ASR capture distinct failure types (Appendix G, p. 20).
- Bootstrap intervals do not model repeated-case, seed, or model-selection dependence; phase tests are descriptive and correlations use only 12 configurations (Appendix G, p. 20).
- Hosted model endpoints and serving policies may drift, limiting exact reruns (Appendix G, p. 20).
- The sandbox uses process-level state and side-effect isolation without a kernel namespace, chroot, or firewall (Appendix B.3, p. 17).

**Analyst inference:**

- The benchmark evaluates attacks embedded in predefined synthetic workflows, so transfer to uncontrolled, long-running deployments is not established.
- Qualitative mechanisms are based on representative trajectories without controlled component ablations; they suggest explanations but do not identify causal harness features (Appendix F, p. 19).
- Reliance on one main model-based evaluator remains a measurement risk despite validation agreement, particularly for the more semantic Persistence and Detection labels (Section 4.2, pp. 7–8).

## Open research questions

1. **Which harness component causes the cross-harness safety differences?** The deployed systems vary along several dimensions at once. A factorial experiment could hold the model and cases fixed while independently swapping prompts, authorization context, tool schemas, and state representations.

2. **Can detection be converted reliably into containment?** High Detection sometimes coexists with high ASR. A follow-up could add enforced confirmation gates, provenance checks, or rollback policies and measure changes in ASR and Utility separately.

3. **How well do results transfer to longer and less synthetic workflows?** Cases use three owner turns, mock services, and isolated state. A next experiment could extend workflows over many sessions with benign and adversarial artifacts interleaved while retaining controlled side effects.

4. **How robust are rankings to evaluator and validity-filter choices?** The judge is imperfect and failed runs are excluded. A sensitivity study could compare multiple independent judges, full human annotation, and alternative treatments of invalid runs.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the benchmark design, reported aggregate results, and stated limitations because the supplied text includes the full methods, appendices, tables, and metric definitions.

Material missing or unresolved information includes per-case results, exact counts of excluded runs by configuration, full phase-level numerical values behind Figure 6, model/evaluator prompts in the paper text, annotation guidelines beyond the summarized rubric, and controlled ablations of individual harness components.
