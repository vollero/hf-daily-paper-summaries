# SkillForge: Self-Distilling Agents for Project-Specific Issue Resolution

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.18933) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18933)


## One-sentence takeaway

SkillForge improves repository-level issue resolution by proactively generating test-grounded synthetic bugs, distilling model-specific diagnostic and repair skills from agent trajectories, and injecting those skills when relevant code entities are encountered.

## Problem and motivation

LLM-based software-engineering agents often approach every issue without knowledge of repository-specific APIs, module relationships, conventions, or recurring pitfalls. Existing adaptations are reactive: history-driven methods require sufficiently rich prior issues and trajectories, while online methods incur exploration costs for each new issue. SkillForge addresses this cold-start gap by acquiring knowledge from the repository’s code and tests before real issues arrive (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Functionality-level issue synthesis:** SkillForge traces passing tests, selects critical executed segments, and asks an LLM—without access to their original implementations—to rewrite coordinated code regions. Rewrites that cause test failures become executable SWE-bench-style synthetic issues (Section II-A, Fig. 2).

- **Self-distillation from repair attempts:** A SWE agent resolves the synthetic issues, and SkillForge aligns accessed and edited code with AST-derived repository entities before extracting reusable knowledge from successful and failed trajectories (Section II-B, Fig. 3).

- **Dual-level, entity-grounded skills:** Global diagnostic skills describe entity roles, debugging playbooks, and related APIs; local intervention skills encode entity-specific repair practices and pitfalls (Section II-B).

- **Context-sensitive delivery:** BM25-selected diagnostic skills initialize a repair attempt, while local skills are injected just in time when shell commands access their associated files (Section II-C).

- **Empirical contribution:** The authors evaluate the framework with two model backbones on SWE-bench Verified and SWE-bench Pro, including component ablations, cross-model transfer, hyperparameter studies, repository-level analysis, cost reporting, and a case study (Sections III–IV).

The results establish consistent benchmark improvements for this Mini-SWE-Agent implementation, the two tested LLMs, and the reported protocols. Ablations support the usefulness of both knowledge levels, while the controlled variants suggest that functionality-level synthesis contributes beyond static summarization or isolated-function rewriting.

They do not establish generality to other agent scaffolds, models, repositories without strong tests, or software tasks outside these benchmarks. Statistical significance is marked for selected comparisons, but the supplied text does not report confidence intervals, effect variance, or correction for multiple comparisons.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated—dependence on tests:** Poorly tested or non-runnable code produces fewer synthesis signals, potentially weakening coverage of important regions (Section V-B).

- **Author-stated—synthetic issue language:** LLM-generated problem statements may differ from developer-written reports despite manual checks for fidelity and leakage (Section V-B).

- **Author-supported—model specificity:** Cross-backbone results show that skills learned with one LLM can provide limited or harmful guidance to another, requiring model-specific preprocessing (Table IV).

- **Analyst inference—restricted system coverage:** Only Mini-SWE-Agent with DeepSeek-V3.2 and GPT-5-mini is evaluated, so transfer to other scaffolds and models remains uncertain (Section III-E).

- **Analyst inference—manual validation is underspecified:** The paper reports manual inspection of skill and problem-statement quality but gives no sample size, annotator count, rubric, or agreement measurement (Section V-A).

- **Analyst inference—partial ablation coverage:** The study removes each complete skill level, but the supplied text does not isolate successful versus failed trajectories, AST alignment, macro versus JIT injection, golden-patch inclusion, or individual skill fields.

- **Analyst inference—cost interpretation depends on amortization:** Average cost includes offline work amortized over evaluated instances, but the text does not provide a break-even analysis for repositories receiving different numbers of future issues (Sections III-D and IV-A).

- **Analyst inference—possible test-suite validity constraints:** Success is measured through benchmark tests; the supplied study does not report independent assessment of patch semantic correctness beyond those tests.

## Open research questions

1. **Can model-independent repository knowledge be separated from model-specific repair habits?** Cross-model transfer loses 5.6–7.0 Pass@1 points relative to same-model skills (Table IV). A next experiment could factor skills into repository facts and model-specific interventions, then test each component across additional backbones.

2. **How does test coverage control downstream benefit?** The authors identify limited coverage as an external-validity threat but do not quantify the relationship. A next experiment could stratify repositories or entities by coverage and measure synthesis yield and repair gains within each stratum.

3. **Which trajectory sources make local skills effective?** Successful and failed attempts are combined, but their individual contributions are not reported. An ablation could compare success-only, failure-only, and contrastively combined intervention skills under identical synthetic tasks.

4. **When does offline preprocessing become economically worthwhile?** Reported costs amortize repository-level preparation over benchmark issues. A next experiment could report fixed preprocessing cost, marginal online cost, and break-even issue counts across repository sizes.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the described method and reported benchmark results because the supplied text includes the full methodology, experimental setup, tables, ablations, and stated threats.

Material missing information includes confidence intervals or run-level variance; exact statistical test procedures; detailed offline/online cost decomposition; manual-inspection protocol; per-repository task counts behind Fig. 5; exact numerical results for every synthesis-width setting in Fig. 4(b); synthesis success/rejection rates; and evaluation on agents or LLMs beyond the two reported backbones and Mini-SWE-Agent scaffold.
