# LEGO-RL: Harness-Native Reinforcement Learning for Coding Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.17393) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17393)


## One-sentence takeaway

LEGO-RL connects unmodified coding-agent harnesses to policy-gradient training through token-faithful rollout capture, guarded sandbox execution, and trajectory-level observability, improving Qwen3.5-35B-A3B by 5.8–9.4 percentage points on SWE-bench Verified across three harnesses.

## Problem and motivation

Coding-agent reinforcement learning operates over long, tool-using trajectories whose prompts, context management, repository state, and execution are controlled by an agent harness. The authors identify three resulting gaps:

- Harness-side compaction or history rewriting can make reconstructed training trajectories differ from the tokens actually sampled.
- Sandbox failures, verifier errors, and reward-hacking shortcuts can corrupt sparse executable rewards.
- Asynchronous, distributed pipelines make execution and trajectory failures difficult to diagnose.

Existing frameworks either require agents to adopt framework-defined rollout loops or do not jointly provide harness-native fidelity, scalable execution, reward protection, and observability (§1; Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Harness-native optimization:** An in-process proxy intercepts model API calls without changing the harness control flow. It records token IDs, log-probabilities, response masks, and generation metadata; aligns histories at message level; and excludes content that cannot be aligned reliably (§3.2.2).
- **Sparse-MoE routing fidelity:** Rollout-time expert-routing decisions are recorded and replayed during training so trainer probabilities follow the behavior policy’s routing (§3.1.1, §3.2.2).
- **Reliable execution and rewards:** Fresh sandboxes, stage-specific failure handling, termination-aware trajectory filtering, network controls, hidden grading state, and deterministic dependencies reduce infrastructure contamination and observed reward exploits (§3.2.1–§3.2.3; Table 6).
- **Scalable asynchronous training:** Fully asynchronous scheduling, bounded policy staleness, partial-rollout recovery, image caching, and multiple training/sandbox backends support long-running trajectories (§3.2.3).
- **Operational observability:** A plugin and Live UI provide preflight validation, monitoring, failure breakdowns, consistency diagnostics, and trajectory inspection (§3.3; Figures 2, 7–8).
- **Released artifacts:** The authors state that they open-source the framework, trained models, datasets, task index, and harness adapters (Abstract; §1; §5).

The results establish that, for the tested model, task pool, and three harnesses, the complete system can deliver substantial harness-specific gains while maintaining close rollout–training probability agreement. The routing negative control and task-selection ablation support two individual design choices.

They do not establish run-to-run statistical reliability, generalization to other model architectures, or that every system component is necessary for the end-task gains. Direct cross-harness score comparisons are also inappropriate because each harness has a different step-0 baseline and trajectory distribution (§4.2).

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations**

- Every main experiment uses Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, and each harness is trained separately; other architectures and mixed-harness training are untested (§5).
- Each main configuration has only one production run, so variance in performance gains and efficiency is unknown (§5).
- Terminal binary verification cannot provide intermediate behavioral credit (§5).
- The implemented defenses cover observed reward exploits but cannot guarantee protection against unseen strategies (§5).
- Sandbox and image-delivery speedups are deployment-specific (§5).
- Diagnostic explanations are observational rather than automated causal verification (§5).

**Analyst inferences grounded in the text**

- The paper does not provide a full component-wise ablation tying proxying, filtering, observability, and sandbox defenses separately to SWE-bench gains. Thus, the contribution of each component to final solve rate remains unclear.
- Task screening used one model–scaffold pairing, Qwen3.6-27B with OpenHands SDK, even though the selected pool was later used for all three harnesses (§4.3). Its selection bias across other policies is not quantified.
- The synchronous–asynchronous comparison is partially confounded by different optimizer throughput; the corrected result is an estimate, not a fully matched measurement (§4.6).
- Task-level improvement and regression counts are based on eight asynchronous samples per task and are explicitly not direct evidence of learning or forgetting (§4.5).
- The source claims release of code, models, and data, but the supplied paper text does not report an independent reproducibility attempt.

## Open research questions

1. **Does LEGO-RL generalize across model architectures and scales?**  
   Only Qwen3.5-35B-A3B is used in the main experiments. A next experiment could repeat matched runs with dense and sparse models at several scales while reporting multiple seeds.

2. **Can one policy learn across multiple harnesses without losing harness-specific performance?**  
   Each harness is trained separately, and Table 2 shows strong harness dependence. A factorial study could compare single-harness training, mixed-harness training, and cross-harness transfer on identical held-out tasks.

3. **Which system components cause the end-task gains?**  
   The paper validates routing replay and task screening, but not every component’s contribution to SWE-bench performance. A controlled ablation could remove trajectory filtering, routing replay, generation-time capture, and difficulty screening individually while measuring both solve rate and signal corruption.

4. **Can richer credit improve efficiency without weakening reward integrity?**  
   The binary terminal verifier cannot credit intermediate recovery or verification behavior. A next experiment could compare binary reward against verifier-grounded process rewards, including adversarial audits for new reward-hacking paths.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the paper’s stated methods, experimental settings, and reported measurements because the supplied text includes the complete main paper and appendices.

Material missing or unresolved:

- Multi-seed estimates, confidence intervals, and statistical tests for the main solve-rate gains.
- Production training compute, hardware inventory, total cost, and end-to-end resource consumption.
- Component-wise end-task ablations for most framework features.
- Results on model architectures other than Qwen3.5-35B-A3B.
- Mixed-harness and cross-harness generalization results.
- Independent verification that every claimed released artifact is complete and reproducible.
