# CardioState-JEPA: Delay-Aware Cross-Modal Learning of a Shared Cardiac Representation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.12944) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12944)


## One-sentence takeaway

CardioState-JEPA uses masked latent prediction and learned physiological-delay alignment to train one frozen encoder across ECG, PPG, and PCG, outperforming modality-specific self-supervised baselines on aggregate downstream evaluations.

## Problem and motivation

ECG, PPG, and PCG observe electrical, hemodynamic, and acoustic aspects of the same cardiac cycle, but existing cardiac representation models generally treat them separately. This leaves potentially useful cross-sensor physiological supervision untapped.

A unified model faces two central problems: corresponding events occur at different times because of electromechanical and pulse-transit delays, and their waveform appearances differ substantially. The authors argue that timestamp matching can therefore align different cardiac phases, while raw cross-modal reconstruction can overemphasize sensor-specific morphology. Synchronized multimodal recordings are also much scarcer than unimodal recordings (Introduction; Sections 3.2 and 4.1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Shared architecture:** Lightweight modality-specific tokenizers normalize differing channels, sampling rates, and morphology into a common token space, followed by one shared ViT-B Transformer encoder and projector (Sections 3.1 and 3.3; Figure 2).

- **Two-stage JEPA curriculum:** Stage I learns each modality through contiguous masked latent-state prediction on abundant unimodal data. Stage II warm-starts from Stage I and adds cross-modal prediction using synchronized recordings while retaining unimodal samples (Sections 3.4–3.6; Algorithm 1).

- **Delay-aware cross-modal alignment:** A learned head predicts bounded, per-token physiological offsets. A differentiable Gaussian kernel gathers target representations at shifted times, with uncertain alignments downweighted by kernel entropy (Section 3.5, Equations 3–6).

- **Physiological guidance:** Detected R-peak-to-S1 and pulse-arrival offsets supervise the delay head where reliable anchors exist; VICReg-style state alignment and intra-beat phase prediction further shape the shared representation (Sections 3.5–3.6, Equations 7–8).

- **Unified evaluation:** The same frozen encoder is assessed by linear probing across 25 ECG, PPG, and PCG tasks, including label-efficiency tests for ECG (Sections 4.1–4.2).

The results support the authors’ claim that multimodal, delay-aware pretraining can improve frozen single-modality transfer, particularly for PPG and PCG. The controlled objective and component ablations strengthen attribution beyond a baseline-only comparison.

They do not establish clinical utility, prospective performance, generalization beyond the listed datasets and modalities, or superiority under fine-tuning. The modality-interleaving result is evidence of reduced sensor separability, but by itself would not prove physiological alignment; the downstream, ablation, and landmark-timing results provide complementary evidence. The aggregate PPG regression MAE also combines different targets and units, limiting its standalone interpretability.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:**

- Synchronized paired and trimodal corpora are much smaller than unimodal corpora, leaving the delay aligner trained on comparatively few clean beats (Section A.10).
- PCG has the smallest pretraining corpus, increasing its dependence on cross-modal transfer (Section A.10; Table 5).
- Adding the third modality provides its clearest benefit for PPG and PCG; ECG performance is within noise of the strongest bimodal variant (Section A.10; Table 4).
- Delay supervision assumes detectable reference events. Very noisy recordings fall back to unsupervised alignment (Sections 3.5 and A.10).
- Evaluation is limited to frozen linear probes; full fine-tuning and larger encoders were not tested (Section A.10).

**Analyst inferences grounded in the text:**

- Cross-modal gains may depend on the populations and acquisition conditions represented in the relatively limited paired datasets; no cross-site or prospective validation is reported.
- The delay visualization covers ECG→PPG and ECG→PCG on selected paired datasets, but the supplied text gives no aggregate delay-error distribution, sample count, or trimodal timing evaluation (Figure 8).
- Several comparisons use reference models trained with labels or clinical text and are explicitly described by the authors as not directly comparable (Section 4.2).
- Although the paper provides extensive task results and training settings, the supplied text does not establish code, checkpoint, or processed-data availability.
- Attention and t-SNE visualizations are descriptive rather than causal evidence that the learned features encode specific physiological mechanisms (Figures 3 and 7).

## Open research questions

1. **How much synchronized data is necessary for reliable alignment?**  
   The paired corpora are described as scarce, but scaling behavior is not reported. A next experiment could train with progressively smaller paired-data fractions and measure downstream transfer plus held-out delay error.

2. **Does learned delay alignment remain reliable under severe noise or failed beat detection?**  
   Anchor supervision is masked when too few clean beats are found. A controlled corruption study could vary ECG, PPG, and PCG noise levels and compare learned alignment with fixed-delay and unsupervised alternatives.

3. **Does the shared representation improve full fine-tuning or clinical generalization?**  
   Only frozen linear probing is evaluated. A next experiment could compare linear probing, partial unfreezing, and full fine-tuning on patient-disjoint external cohorts.

4. **Which additional cardiac modality or pairing provides complementary supervision?**  
   The study is restricted to ECG, PPG, and PCG, and the third modality does not clearly improve ECG. A modality-addition study could test whether new synchronized measurements yield gains beyond matched increases in data and training budget.
