# StartupBench: Benchmarking General-Purpose Agents on Market-Validated End-to-End Workflows

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.17800) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17800)


## One-sentence takeaway
StartupBench shows that leading general-purpose agents can satisfy many individual requirements in market-derived professional workflows, yet the best tested system completes only 31.27% of runs under the benchmark’s strict success threshold.

## Problem and motivation
Existing agent benchmarks often use researcher-selected or simulated tasks, so their relevance to work users demonstrably want to delegate remains uncertain. Their holistic evaluation methods may also miss functional, structural, formatting, and domain-specific defects in complex deliverables.

The authors address this gap by sourcing end-to-end workflows from adopted AI-startup products and their users, then evaluating complete professional artifacts with criterion-level rubrics (Introduction, pp. 1–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Demand-driven benchmark construction:** The authors survey market-validated AI startups, interview deep users, and have domain experts convert observed workflows into reproducible tasks (Figure 2; Sections 3.1.1–3.1.4).
- **StartupBench dataset:** It contains 97 tasks across six professional domains and supports deliverables including DOCX, XLSX, PPTX, PDF, Markdown, images, and text (Table 2).
- **Fine-grained artifact evaluation:** Each task averages 25.3 weighted rubrics spanning six quality dimensions. A tool-using judge independently checks each rubric against submitted artifacts and evidence views (Sections 3.2–3.3; Appendix C).
- **Empirical evaluation and diagnosis:** Nine models are tested for three runs each under a common harness, followed by domain, rubric, failure-mode, harness, and specialized-agent comparisons (Sections 4.1–4.5).

The evidence establishes a substantial gap between partial rubric satisfaction and strict task completion within this curated benchmark and execution protocol. It also supports the reliability of rubric-wise automated evaluation against the sampled expert judgments.

It does not establish performance on the natural distribution of professional work, because easy pilot tasks were intentionally removed. Nor does it isolate whether specialized-agent gains come from domain models, prompts, tools, orchestration, or workflow design.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope choice:** Pilot models were used to remove consistently easy tasks, explicitly prioritizing difficulty and discrimination over estimating performance on the natural task distribution (Section 3.1.4, footnote 1).
- **Author-stated sourcing proxy:** Funding above USD 1M is treated only as a coarse market-expectation signal; adoption evidence is additionally required (Section 3.1.1).
- **Analyst inference — selection bias:** Startup-derived, adopted workflows may underrepresent valuable work not served by funded AI-native companies. The text supplies no population-level coverage analysis.
- **Analyst inference — judge dependence:** All automatic results rely on GPT-5.5 as judge. Human agreement is high, but only outputs from two representative models are described as manually evaluated, and judge-model sensitivity is not reported (Sections 4.1, 4.3).
- **Analyst inference — specialized comparison confounding:** Production startup agents differ through model selection, prompting, tools, orchestration, and workflow design, so Table 7 cannot attribute their advantage to any single factor.
- **Analyst inference — limited harness ablation:** The framework comparison covers only three of the nine models and three general-purpose harnesses (Table 6).
- **Analyst inference — reproducibility uncertainty:** The supplied text describes a project page and detailed construction process, but does not establish benchmark licensing, public availability, exact harness configuration, judge prompts, or access to proprietary startup agents.

## Open research questions

1. **How well does performance transfer to an unfiltered distribution of real user requests?** Easy tasks were excluded during calibration. A follow-up could sample workflows before difficulty filtering and report both demand-weighted and challenge-set results.

2. **Which specialized-agent component produces the improvement?** Table 7 changes several components simultaneously. A factorial experiment could independently vary model, prompt, tools, orchestration, and domain-specific workflow logic.

3. **Can explicit artifact verification close the completion gap?** The paper identifies missing cached spreadsheet values and unreconciled fields but does not test a verification intervention. A controlled study could add reopen-and-check, deterministic validation, or rubric-conditioned repair loops.

4. **How robust are scores to judge choice and rubric formulation?** Only one underlying judge model is reported. A follow-up could compare multiple judge models, blinded experts, deterministic checkers, and paraphrased or independently authored rubrics.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported benchmark design and numerical results because the supplied text includes the complete main paper and appendices.

Material missing or unclear: public release and licensing status; full per-task data and rubrics; exact prompts and tool configuration; confidence-interval values; human-evaluation sample counts beyond “two representative models”; expert disagreement analysis; judge-model sensitivity; startup identities and task provenance; task-level specialized-agent matching details; compute, cost, and runtime; and statistical significance tests for model differences.
