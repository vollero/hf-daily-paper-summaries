# Security Assessment of DeepSeek Harness with A.I.G: Evaluating Resistance to Indirect Prompt Injection

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.16393) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16393)


## One-sentence takeaway

In 14,560 controlled DeepSeek Harness executions, indirect prompt-injection success varied sharply with attack wording, carrier representation, source channel, and judging method, showing that security must be evaluated across the full content-to-action runtime path rather than from model output alone.

## Problem and motivation

Tool-using agents ingest externally controlled webpages, files, emails, chat records, and reusable skills. Malicious instructions in this content can redirect the agent toward disclosure, command execution, submissions, or financial actions (Section 1).

The authors argue that model-level prompt testing misses operational failures arising in the composed runtime. They address the gap with a real-runtime, source-to-sink assessment that observes whether tainted content reaches the model, changes behavior, and causes a sensitive tool call with attacker-specified arguments (Sections 1–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Real-runtime evaluation:** A.I.G drives the actual DeepSeek Harness TypeScript agent loop, tool registry, model adapter, and session-event path through a runtime adapter (Sections 1 and 4.1; Figure 3).
- **Controlled attack matrix:** The study executes 14,560 runs spanning 16 channels, text/file carrier modes, 35 objectives, one unmodified baseline, and 12 transformed attacks (Section 5.1; Figure 5; Table 2).
- **Safe source-to-sink tracing:** Six source fixtures inject tainted artifacts, while eight simulated sinks record attempted actions without external effects (Sections 2.1 and 4.1; Table 2).
- **Complementary outcome judgments:** A deterministic RuleJudge (JR) and semantic LLMJudge (JL) separately classify full success, partial compliance, and failure/not reached (Section 3.3; Figure 2).
- **Code-grounded control analysis:** The authors locate model-visible tool-result paths, additional-context handling, and pre-execution guard hooks where deployments can preserve provenance or block sensitive calls (Sections 4.2 and 7.2; Figure 4).

These results establish that indirect-injection outcomes in this controlled DSH configuration depend on carrier processing, attack framing, channel, objective type, and verdict semantics. Trace collection also distinguishes content exposure from completed attacker objectives.

They do not establish universal vulnerability rates for DSH, the backend model, or agent frameworks generally. Sink calls represent attempted actions against local fixtures, not successful real-world effects, and the experiment does not evaluate defenses (Appendix B; Section 8).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Percentages are descriptive for one controlled configuration and are not universal vulnerability estimates (Appendix B).
- **Author-stated safety abstraction:** All sinks are simulated, so results measure attempted actions rather than completed external consequences (Sections 2.1 and Appendix B).
- **No defense comparison:** The study identifies possible control points but does not test prompt-boundary or other mitigations (Section 8).
- **Judge validity:** JL produced nine judge errors and assigns semantic labels more broadly than JR; the authors recommend retaining both exact evidence and semantic review rather than collapsing them into one score (Table 3; Section 6.6).
- **Analyst inference — narrow system coverage:** Only one DSH source snapshot, one stated backend, and one baseline configuration are reported, limiting conclusions about other versions, models, and policies (Section 4.2; Table 2).
- **Analyst inference — missing uncertainty analysis:** The supplied text gives counts and percentages but no confidence intervals, repeated trials, or variance across stochastic runs.
- **Analyst inference — limited causal isolation:** The matrix compares attacks and carriers, but no reported ablation separately isolates parser behavior, prompt construction, model behavior, and tool authorization as causes of individual failures.

## Open research questions

1. **Which controls reduce action success without breaking benign tool use?** The paper recommends provenance, argument checks, allowlists, and approval but evaluates none. A next experiment could apply each control separately and measure attack success alongside benign task completion.

2. **How stable are the findings across models and runtime versions?** Only one reported DSH snapshot and backend configuration are assessed. A factorial replication across model adapters, DSH commits, and policy configurations could measure variance and interaction effects.

3. **Why does hidden Unicode change from 0.0% to 25.5% JR success across modes?** The paper attributes the difference to file representation and extraction but does not isolate the responsible stage. Parser-, normalization-, serialization-, and prompt-level ablations could localize the effect.

4. **How well do JR and JL correspond to expert judgments?** Their disagreement is analyzed, but no human-labeled validation set is reported. Experts could annotate a stratified trace sample to estimate each judge’s precision, recall, and error modes.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported design and descriptive findings because the supplied text includes the complete method, tables, denominators, and scope qualifications.

Material missing information includes JL’s model and prompt, generation settings, stochasticity controls or random seeds, confidence intervals, human validation of judge labels, per-objective breakdowns, benign-task utility measurements, runtime resource costs, and empirical results for proposed defenses.
