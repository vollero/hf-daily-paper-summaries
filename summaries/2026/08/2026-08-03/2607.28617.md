# AISPA: User-Centric System Prompt Auditing for Large Language Model Applications

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-03.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.28617) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28617)


## One-sentence takeaway

AISPA audits system-prompt spans across eight user-protection dimensions, finding protective directives in 87 of 88 sampled commercial products but complete dimensional coverage in only 21, while 34 contain at least one problematic directive.

## Problem and motivation

System prompts govern deployed LLM behavior but are usually hidden from users and independent auditors. The authors argue that existing governance frameworks offer little concrete prompt-level guidance, while prompt-security research generally treats the system prompt as trusted and focuses on malicious user inputs. AISPA addresses the resulting gap: evaluating whether developer-authored instructions themselves protect users or encourage deception, manipulation, privacy violations, unsafe actions, or other harms (Sections 1–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **User-centric taxonomy:** Eight dimensions grounded in cited UDHR articles: identity transparency, truthfulness, privacy, tool/action safety, user agency, unsafe-request handling, harm prevention, and fairness (Section 3; Figure 2; Table 1).
- **Span-level auditing:** Auditable, generally non-core instructions receive a dimension and a protective `+1` or problematic `−1` label; pure product-functionality instructions are excluded (Section 4.1; Figure 3).
- **Human–LLM workflow:** An LLM generates candidates, trained annotators screen and supplement them, and three experts adjudicate; problematic labels require unanimous expert agreement (Section 4.2; Figure 4).
- **Commercial-prompt audit:** The framework is applied to prompts from 88 products collected through six public repositories, with repository-maintainer and cross-source checks (Section 5.1; Appendices A–B).
- **Borderline-risk analysis:** The authors separately characterize gray areas involving human mimicry, parasocial cues, user-overridable protections, and permissive or politically framed content policies (Section 6).

These results establish descriptive patterns within the collected and annotated prompt corpus. They do not establish that the prompts match current production versions, that instructions produce corresponding runtime behavior, that AISPA coverage causes safer outcomes, or that the 88 products represent the commercial ecosystem. The paper also inconsistently describes the audit as covering **3,249 instructions** in the Abstract and Conclusion but reports a final dataset of **2,420 entries from 1,818 spans** in Section 5.1; the supplied text does not reconcile these quantities.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** The prompts came from leaks or community disclosures rather than official channels, so their exact authenticity, version, and current deployment status cannot be guaranteed (Section 10).
- **Author-stated:** The corpus may overrepresent easily extracted prompts and technically engaged communities while underrepresenting products with stronger protections against extraction (Section 10).
- **Author-stated:** Cross-repository checking is incomplete: 38 of 88 prompts have no independent same-product match and rely on maintainer verification (Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference:** The study evaluates written instructions, not observed model behavior; prompt labels therefore cannot demonstrate actual user safety or compliance.
- **Analyst inference:** Agreement evidence is narrow: the reported 0.933 pairwise IAA comes from only 20 calibration spans, while annotators subsequently handled separate prompt subsets. Reliability across the full corpus is not reported (Section 5.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Requiring unanimity only for problematic labels reduces false accusations but may systematically undercount harmful directives (Section 4.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Temporal and provider comparisons are descriptive and may be confounded by changing product composition, prompt length, extraction scope, or tool schemas; no matched statistical analysis or uncertainty estimates are reported.
- **Analyst inference:** The unresolved 3,249-versus-2,420 counting discrepancy weakens clarity about corpus construction and reproducibility.

## Open research questions

1. **Do AISPA prompt scores predict runtime behavior?** The paper audits text rather than outputs. A next experiment could test standardized safety scenarios against products whose prompts have different dimension scores and measure behavioral agreement.

2. **How robust is AISPA across auditors and cultures?** The taxonomy is normatively grounded, but full-corpus agreement and cross-cultural validity are not reported. A preregistered multi-site annotation study could compare labels, disagreements, and gray-area judgments.

3. **How representative are leaked prompts?** Selection bias and uncertain deployment status remain unresolved. A next study could audit an officially supplied, versioned sample and compare its distributions with the leaked corpus.

4. **Which safeguards add protection rather than merely prompt length?** The observed trends are correlational. Controlled prompt ablations could remove or add individual AISPA-aligned spans and measure safety, task performance, refusals, and user control.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text gives substantial methodological and numerical detail, but important information is unavailable or unclear: reconciliation of the 3,249, 2,420, and 1,818 counts; full annotation instructions from Appendix C; per-product labels and dates; statistical uncertainty or significance tests; full-corpus reliability measures; LLM pre-annotation recall and precision; adjudication disagreement rates; demographic or disciplinary composition of annotators and experts; dataset/code release details; and any behavioral validation linking prompt content to model outputs.
