# Safeguards Based on Copyable Context Cannot Provide Reliable Safety for LLMs

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-03.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.27951) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27951)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors prove that, for dual-use tasks, safeguards relying only on evidence attackers can reproduce cannot preserve positive legitimate utility while reducing worst-case attacker assistance below a capability-dependent floor; crossing that floor requires noncopyable evidence predictive of actual use.

## Problem and motivation

LLM safeguards must decide what to release before observing how the output is ultimately used. In dual-use settings, the same output may aid both an authorized professional and an attacker, while claimed intent and interaction history can be imitated.

The authors argue that existing impossibility results do not quantify the exact attacker-assistance floor caused specifically by hidden downstream use and reproducible evidence. This matters because improving classifiers or adding clarification turns cannot resolve a distinction absent from the observable evidence (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Capability–evidence decomposition:** The paper separates the legitimate and malicious utility of each possible release from the evidence available to the safeguard (§3.1–3.2).

- **Exact capability frontier:** It defines \(\Gamma(q)\), the minimum attacker assistance compatible with legitimate-use utility \(q\), and characterizes it as the lower boundary of the convex hull of release-utility pairs (§3.2, Eq. 4).

- **Copyable-evidence impossibility result:** It proves that arbitrary randomized and interactive safeguards reduce exactly to this static frontier when attackers can reproduce the legitimate transcript distribution (§3.3–3.4, Theorem 1).

- **Trusted-signal analysis:** It formalizes credentials as noncopyable signals predictive of downstream use, derives the resulting frontier \(\Gamma_S(q)\), and states exact and approximate conditions for reducing or eliminating attacker assistance (§3.5, Theorems 2–3 and Corollary 3).

- **Composition extensions:** The framework covers task families, imperfect per-turn copying, correlated retries, and additive assistance across sessions (§3.6).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Positive legitimate utility entails positive attacker assistance under the dual-use condition. | For finite release menus, \(\Gamma(q)\geq \rho q>0\) for every feasible \(q>0\), where \(\rho\) is the minimum malicious-to-legitimate utility ratio over useful releases. | §3.2, Eqs. 5–6 |
| Interactive safeguards offer no worst-case improvement when evidence is copyable. | If the legitimate transcript law belongs to the closed convex class of attacker-induced laws, then \(R_\kappa(q)=\Gamma(q)\) for every feasible \(q\). | §3.4, Theorem 1, Eq. 10 |
| Useful capability, reliable safety below the floor, and credential-free open access cannot coexist. | For \(q>0\) and \(\beta<\Gamma(q)\), no mechanism using only copyable evidence satisfies all three objectives. | §3.4, Corollary 1 |
| Imperfect copying weakens the floor continuously. | With copying distance \(\delta_\kappa\), \([\Gamma(q)-\delta_\kappa]_+\leq R_\kappa(q)\leq\Gamma(q)\). | §3.4, Eq. 12 |
| Under conditional copying, access history adds no worst-case information beyond a trusted signal. | The optimal value using history and signal equals the signal-conditioned frontier: \(R_\kappa^{H,S}(q)=\Gamma_S(q)\). | §3.5, Theorem 2, Eq. 18 |
| Zero assistance requires legitimate utility on signal values unavailable to attackers. | \(\Gamma_S(q)=0\) exactly when \(q\leq q_{\max}P_B^S(\mathcal S_0)\), with \(\mathcal S_0\) outside malicious support. | §3.5, Corollary 3, Eqs. 19–20 |
| Credential separation and copying error must jointly cover the original floor. | Any \(q\)-feasible rule guaranteeing assistance at most \(\beta\) must satisfy \(\beta+d+\eta_\kappa\geq\Gamma(q)\); the corresponding balanced-accuracy threshold is necessary, not sufficient. | §3.5, Theorem 3, Eq. 22 |
| The additive floor composes across correlated sessions. | Under complete-process copyability, \(R_N^{\mathrm{sum}}(\mathbf q)=\sum_i\Gamma_i(q_i)\), without an independence assumption. | §3.6, Proposition 2 |
| Prior adaptive-attack evidence supports practical relevance but does not validate the theorem’s deployment parameters. | A cited study reports success above 90% against most of 12 recent defenses; the authors still require policy-specific estimates of \(\delta_\kappa\). | §4.2 |

The formal evidence establishes conditional lower bounds once the release utilities, attacker class, and copyability assumptions are fixed. It does not empirically estimate \(\Gamma(q)\), \(\rho\), \(d\), \(\delta_\kappa\), or \(\eta_\kappa\) for a concrete deployment. Section 4 mainly interprets results reported by prior studies and deployed programs; it is not a new controlled evaluation of the proposed framework.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Results depend on a fixed utility calibration, finite operational resolution, and a specified attacker class (§5). These choices determine both the floor and whether copying assumptions hold.

- **Author-stated:** Deployment claims require policy-specific estimates of copying error (§5), while credential evaluation must account for transfer, compromise, new-account creation, and misuse by authorized holders (§4.4).

- **Author-stated:** “Open access” means credential-free access to a committed inference-time mechanism, not access to model weights; user-controlled open-weight deployments fall outside the model (§5).

- **Analyst inference:** The central quantities are not measured on an original benchmark, so the theory does not reveal how large the safety floor is in practical systems. Section 4 explicitly says action-level dual-use and \(\rho\) require release-menu evaluation.

- **Analyst inference:** Utility functions encode direct assistance at release rather than realized harm probability or severity (§3.1). Conclusions about real-world harm therefore require an additional causal model.

- **Analyst inference:** The strongest equality results use unrestricted finite Markov kernels; restricted implementations retain the lower bound but may incur unquantified implementation loss (§3.4, Corollary 2).

## Open research questions

1. **How large is \(\Gamma(q)\) for real model deployments?** The paper provides no empirical release-menu calibration. A next experiment could enumerate outputs for matched dual-use tasks, obtain separate legitimate and malicious utility ratings, and estimate the convex frontier.

2. **How copyable are specific interactive safeguards?** The theory requires policy- and attacker-specific \(\delta_\kappa\), which §4.2 does not measure. An adaptive red-team study could estimate transcript-distribution distance for intent clarification, multi-turn checks, and randomized verification.

3. **Do trusted credentials predict actual use after compromise and authorized-user misuse?** The paper identifies \(d\) and malicious support as decisive but supplies no deployment estimates. A prospective evaluation could hold one access policy fixed across credential-transfer and account-compromise scenarios.

4. **How sensitive are conclusions to utility calibration and aggregation?** The main composition result uses additive assistance, while binary success has a different rule (§3.6). Experiments could compare estimated floors under additive utility, probability of one successful misuse, and realized-harm-weighted objectives.
