# TSDS-Toolbox: A Toolbox for Measuring Time-Series Dataset Similarity

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.08119) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08119)


## One-sentence takeaway

TSDS-Toolbox unifies time-series dataset-similarity measurement and downstream evaluation, with experiments showing that the most informative similarity metric varies by task and model.

## Problem and motivation

Time-series dataset similarity can guide source-data selection for transfer learning, dataset inspection, benchmark curation, and analysis of foundation-model generalization. The authors argue that existing implementations are fragmented: general time-series toolboxes do not systematically benchmark dataset-level similarity, while similarity libraries tend to compare individual series. This leads to inconsistent settings and repeated integration work (Introduction, p. 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Unified benchmarking:** A common interface compares dataset-level and reducer-based series-level metrics under consistent preprocessing and evaluation settings (Sections 3–4.3; Figure 1).
- **Downstream evaluation:** Standardized pipelines test whether similarity predicts classification and forecasting losses in out-of-distribution and transfer-learning settings (Section 4.4).
- **Extensible architecture:** Four configuration-driven layers cover data ingestion, similarity computation, downstream evaluation, and analysis; users can add loaders, metrics, reducers, tasks, model adapters, and outputs (Sections 4.1–4.5).
- **Integrated analysis:** The toolbox produces distance matrices, heatmaps, network graphs, and Pearson correlations between similarity scores and task errors (Section 4.5; Figure 2).

These results establish that the toolbox can execute a common comparison across multiple similarity families and that metric–loss alignment is sensitive to the downstream setting. They do not establish that correlation causes better source selection, that the rankings generalize beyond the 25 datasets and tested models, or that small numerical differences are statistically meaningful; no uncertainty estimates or significance tests are reported.

## Limitations and validity risks

The paper does not provide a dedicated limitations section.

- **Analyst inference — narrow evaluation pool:** All reported results use 25 GluonTS datasets transformed into 100 length-100 windows, so robustness to other repositories, dataset sizes, sequence lengths, and preprocessing choices remains untested (Section 5).
- **Analyst inference — limited downstream coverage:** Evaluation covers one-class classification and forecasting with Lag-Llama and Time-MoE; other tasks named in the motivation, such as anomaly detection or generation, are not evaluated (Sections 1, 4.4, and 5).
- **Analyst inference — no uncertainty analysis:** Tables 2–3 report single Pearson correlations without confidence intervals, repeated-run variability, or significance tests, making close comparisons such as 0.420 versus 0.406 difficult to interpret.
- **Analyst inference — missing component ablations:** The supplied text does not isolate sensitivity to window sampling, normalization, flat-sequence removal, metric hyperparameters, or correlation choice.
- **Analyst inference — reproducibility metadata risk:** The PDF text contains placeholder conference and DOI fields and labels the citation as 2018 despite an August 2026 arXiv header (pp. 1–2); the supplied text does not explain these inconsistencies.

## Open research questions

1. **How stable are metric rankings under different preprocessing choices?** Only one window length, sample count, normalization scheme, and flat-sequence policy are reported. A factorial experiment varying these choices could measure rank stability and correlation variance.
2. **Does correlation translate into successful source-dataset selection?** The paper correlates distances with losses but does not report top-\(k\) retrieval or selection regret. A next experiment could rank sources by each metric and compare selected-source performance with an oracle and random selection.
3. **How well does the framework generalize to additional tasks and models?** The evaluation is limited to classification and forecasting with two forecasting adapters. Adding anomaly detection, generation, and more model families would test whether task dependence persists.
4. **Are the observed differences statistically reliable?** No confidence intervals or significance tests are supplied. Bootstrap resampling over datasets and windows could quantify uncertainty in correlations and metric rankings.
