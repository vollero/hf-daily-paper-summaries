# JigShape: Evaluating Visual-Geometric Reasoning in VLMs through Jigsaw Puzzles

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.27670) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27670)


## One-sentence takeaway

JigShape shows that VLMs can learn small tab-and-blank jigsaw puzzles but suffer a sharp scaling failure as grids grow, with fine-tuned models also relying much more on boundary geometry than visual continuity.

## Problem and motivation

The paper evaluates whether VLMs can reconstruct images by jointly using visual content and geometric constraints. The authors argue that existing jigsaw benchmarks are weak diagnostics because rectangular pieces can be interchangeable in repeated-texture regions and are generally evaluated only on coarse \(2\times2\)–\(5\times5\) grids. JigShape seeks to provide less ambiguous targets and test whether constraint satisfaction scales to substantially denser arrangements (Introduction; Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Geometry-constrained task:** Pieces have complementary tabs and blanks, plus flat outer edges, and models predict each labeled piece’s grid coordinates. Pieces are shuffled but not rotated (Sections 3.1–3.2, 3.5).
- **Large, difficulty-scaled benchmark:** JigShape contains 95,468 instances derived from 23,742 source images, spanning \(4\times4\), \(8\times8\), \(12\times12\), and \(16\times16\) grids (Sections 3.3–3.4; Tables 2–3).
- **Controlled geometry ablation:** A paired 500-instance \(4\times4\) set replaces shaped boundaries with rectangular cuts while retaining source images and permutations (Section 3.4; Appendix A).
- **Multi-metric evaluation:** The study evaluates five frontier VLMs in six zero-shot configurations and two supervised models using piece, exact-match, adjacency, and shape-compatibility accuracy (Sections 4.1 and 5).
- **Constraint verification and released tooling:** A backtracking solver using exact edge signatures solves all 500 test instances at every grid size; the authors state that the generation pipeline, evaluation code, and configurations are released (Appendix E.2; Section 7).

|---|---|---|

| Other zero-shot systems remained near chance on \(4\times4\). | Reported PA range was 6.62%–11.97%, with 0% EM; SC was 32.9%–37.6% versus the 30.8% random baseline. | Tables 4–5; Section 5.1 |
| Explicit reasoning gave Grok-4.2 only a modest small-grid gain. | Reasoning mode reached 11.97% PA versus 6.90% without reasoning on \(4\times4\), a 5.07-point difference; the improvement disappeared on larger grids. | Section 5.1; Appendix C |
| GPT-5.5 displayed a strong scaling cliff. | PA fell from 69.65% at \(4\times4\) to 4.37% at \(8\times8\) and 0.67% at \(12\times12\); random PA is \(1/N^2\). | Table 4; Sections 5.1 and 5.3 |

| Exact edge constraints suffice for the benchmark’s test puzzles. | A backtracking solver using edge signatures achieved 100% accuracy on all 500 test instances at each of the four grid sizes. | Appendix E.2 |
| Extensive oracle clues did not remove precise-placement errors. | With 60 of 64 positions revealed on \(8\times8\), GPT-5.5 achieved 25% PA on the four remaining pieces; the authors report that pieces were placed in the correct \(2\times2\) region but swapped. | Table 10; Appendix E.3 |

The results establish strong empirical difficulty for the evaluated models under the paper’s prompts, output parser, and synthetic puzzle construction. They do not establish that all VLM architectures fundamentally lack scalable constraint reasoning: only a limited model set and training recipe were tested. Moreover, the \(8\times8\) SFT results are degraded but still well above random PA, so “collapse to near-random on \(8\times8\) and larger” overstates the reported SFT evidence at \(8\times8\).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope choices:** Pieces are never rotated, grids are square, and the no-shape comparison is restricted to \(4\times4\) because the authors regard denser rectangular puzzles as ambiguous (Sections 3.4–3.5).
- **Author-stated evaluation gap:** The held-out test set was reserved for a future competition; reported model results use the 250-instance-per-grid evaluation split (Section 3.4; Section 5).
- **Analyst inference — geometry may dominate by construction:** The classical solver obtains 100% using edge signatures alone (Appendix E.2). This complicates the framing that visual content is needed to obtain unique solutions and means high benchmark performance need not demonstrate joint visual-geometric integration.
- **Analyst inference — limited architectural evidence:** Only two model families receive supervised fine-tuning, using one main mixed-grid recipe. Failed inverse-accuracy weighting does not by itself prove that architecture, rather than optimization, resolution, representation, or training design, causes the scaling cliff (Appendix C; Appendix E.1).
- **Analyst inference — parsing confound:** Invalid JSON receives zero on every metric, but the supplied text does not report invalid-output rates by model, leaving unclear how much measured failure is spatial versus formatting failure (Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference — incomplete numerical reporting in the retrieved text:** Captions for several tables are present without their full cells, limiting verification of all model-by-grid comparisons, weighted-SFT results, and hint experiments.
- **Analyst inference — dataset concentration:** Unsplash supplies 21,342 of 23,742 source images, so visual diversity is largely determined by one photographic source (Section 3.4).

## Open research questions

1. **Can models be trained to require both appearance and geometry?** The shape-only solver and severe no-shape drops show that geometry can dominate. A next experiment could construct puzzles where several pieces share edge signatures and only visual continuity resolves the placement, then report separate geometry-only and vision-only controls.

2. **What causes the scaling cliff?** Reweighting harder examples did not help, but that does not isolate resolution, tokenization, search, working memory, or optimization. A factorial experiment could vary piece count, rendered resolution, constraint density, and output length independently.

3. **Would explicit decomposition or external search improve dense-grid performance?** The tested VLMs predict full mappings directly, while the classical solver uses constraint propagation. A plausible experiment would compare direct generation against iterative local matching, backtracking, or a VLM-guided constraint solver under the same inputs.

4. **How robust are the findings beyond fixed-orientation photographic puzzles?** Rotation, non-photographic imagery, irregular layouts, and real scanned pieces are untested. A follow-up benchmark could vary these factors while retaining verified solution uniqueness.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text contains detailed task construction, metric definitions, and many headline results, but the numerical bodies of Tables 1, 2, 4–10 are absent. Material missing information includes complete per-model results, uncertainty estimates or repeated-run variability, invalid-JSON rates, detailed weighted-SFT and visual-hint numbers, compute requirements, public artifact locations, and evidence from the reserved held-out test set.
