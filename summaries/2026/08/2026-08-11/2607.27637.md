# MMOOC: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Out-of-Context Evaluation in Multimodal Large Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-11.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.27637) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27637)


## One-sentence takeaway

MMOOC evaluates whether multimodal models can both refuse genuinely unsupported image questions and continue answering when distracting context leaves the core question answerable, finding substantial and format-dependent weaknesses across 18 models.

## Problem and motivation

MLLMs may hallucinate when an image does not support a question, but excessive refusal also harms usability when sufficient evidence remains. Existing benchmarks predominantly test unanswerable questions and offer limited coverage of question formats, contextual shifts, and visual scenarios; they largely omit “shifted in-context” cases containing misleading or unsupported details that do not invalidate the core query (Introduction, pp. 1–3; Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Joint evaluation formulation:** MMOOC separates five genuinely out-of-context categories requiring refusal from three shifted in-context categories requiring robust answering (Section 3.1; Figure 2).
- **Large, varied benchmark:** More than 41K image–question pairs span yes/no, multiple-choice, and open-ended VQA; eight shift types; and six visual scenarios (Abstract; Introduction, p. 2; Table 1).
- **Construction and verification pipeline:** Questions come from multi-MLLM generation, manual design, and Auto-Shuffle transformations of MME, MMStar, and OK-VQA. Three models must agree on answerability before retained samples undergo human review (Sections 3.2–3.3).
- **Behavior-sensitive metrics:** Answerable cases use Accuracy and Answer Rationality; OOC cases use Refusal Rate and Refusal Rationality, with rationality judged independently by three LLMs and checked against human annotations (Section 3.5).
- **Diagnostic interventions:** The authors compare SFT, DPO, refusal prompting, chain-of-thought prompting, misleading prompts, Gaussian noise, and question-only inputs (Section 4.3; Figures 6–8; Table 5; Table A1).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| OOC performance is low and uneven. | Qwen3-VL-2B’s combined Refusal Rate/Refusal Rationality mean is **5.75** on yes/no USPC and **8.25** on VQA USPC. | Table 3; Section 4.2, p. 7 |
| Performance depends strongly on format and shift type. | Across models, results vary among yes/no, MCQ, and VQA and among MA, VFP, USPC, ULS, and MKB; the authors identify USPC and ULS as especially difficult overall. | Tables 3 and A2–A4; Section 4.2 |
| Shifted-IC performance is higher but still fragile. | Partial Answerability in VQA ranges from a combined score of **11.00** for LLaVA-1.5-7B to **72.25** for Qwen3.5-122B-A10B among evaluated models. | Tables 4 and A7 |
| Model scale is not a monotonic predictor of robustness. | Qwen3.5-122B-A10B does not consistently outperform smaller Qwen variants across the reported OOC cells. | Table 3; Figure 5; Section 4.2 |
| SFT improves OOC metrics but can reduce general capability. | For Qwen3-VL-8B, SFT raises \(R_{\mathrm{ref}}\) from **32.00** to **46.00** and \(R_{\mathrm{rat}}\) from **43.00** to **49.50**, while MMStar falls from **65.13** to **61.39**. | Table 5; Section 4.3 |
| Automated judging broadly agrees with humans. | On 2,000 sampled responses, agreement is **96.78%** for Refusal Rate and **89.34%** for rationality metrics. | Section 3.5, p. 6 |
| MMOOC responses are reported as less repetitive than comparison benchmarks. | MMOOC has average length **26.69**, Self-BLEU **2.00**, and semantic similarity **0.09**, versus the three listed benchmarks. | Table 2; Section 3.4 |
| Question-only behavior reveals possible shortcuts. | Refusal scores without images range from **4.00** for Gemini-3.1-Pro to **92.00** for Gemma-4-26B; the authors treat this only as a diagnostic. | Table A1; Appendix, p. 21 |

These results establish that the tested models frequently fail to calibrate answering versus refusal on MMOOC and that behavior changes with format, shift category, prompting, and post-training. They do not establish universal rankings beyond the evaluated samples and protocol, nor that the benchmark cleanly isolates multimodal reasoning: the question-only results explicitly suggest linguistic cues or conservative refusal strategies.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated limitation:** MMOOC covers image–text interactions only; video, audio, and embodied environments remain untested (Conclusion, p. 10).
- **Analyst inference — possible generator artifacts:** Much of the data is generated by MLLMs or produced through Auto-Shuffle. Although consensus filtering and human review improve quality, question-only performance indicates that some samples may expose linguistic shortcuts (Sections 3.2–3.3; Table A1).
- **Analyst inference — judge dependence:** Rationality scores rely on three proprietary or externally specified judge models. Human validation covers 2,000 responses and reports agreement, but the text does not provide category-level agreement, annotator counts, or uncertainty estimates (Section 3.5).
- **Analyst inference — incomplete construction detail:** The retrieved text does not report exact counts by category, format, source, or visual scenario, despite claiming balance in Figure 3.
- **Analyst inference — intervention scope:** SFT and DPO are tested on only three models in Table 5, while prompt comparison is shown for Qwen3-VL-8B; broad claims about intervention effectiveness therefore have limited model coverage.
- **Analyst inference — unclear statistical reliability:** No confidence intervals or significance tests are reported for the main model comparisons in the supplied text.

## Open research questions

1. **Can linguistic shortcuts be removed without making questions unnatural?** Question-only models sometimes refuse successfully, so a next experiment could adversarially rewrite questions while preserving their answerability labels and then compare image-conditioned versus question-only performance.

2. **Can alignment improve refusal without degrading general multimodal ability?** SFT improves OOC scores but lowers MMStar for all three reported models in Table 5. A next experiment could train with jointly balanced IC, OOC, and general-capability objectives and trace the resulting Pareto frontier.

3. **Do the findings transfer beyond static images?** The authors explicitly exclude video, audio, and embodied settings. A next experiment could instantiate the same eight shift categories in temporally grounded video QA and compare error patterns with MMOOC.

4. **How stable are rationality scores across judges and populations?** Aggregate human–judge agreement is reported, but category-level reliability is unavailable. A next experiment could use blinded expert annotators and report per-category agreement, calibration, and confidence intervals.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the benchmark design, reported metrics, and tabulated results because the supplied text includes the complete retrieved paper and appendices.
