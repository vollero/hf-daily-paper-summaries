# Sci-VBench: Evaluating Knowledge- and Reasoning-Intensive Video Generation in Science Domains

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-11.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.09873) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09873)


## One-sentence takeaway

Sci-VBench shows that current text-to-video models can produce visually plausible clips while still failing to represent prompt-specified scientific mechanisms and causal dynamics reliably.

## Problem and motivation

The paper addresses evaluation of scientific text-to-video generation, where a visually convincing video may nevertheless violate domain knowledge, causal relations, or required state transitions. The authors argue that existing benchmarks emphasize perceptual quality, prompt alignment, compositionality, or generic physical commonsense and lack multidisciplinary, expert-defined tests of scientifically correct temporal mechanisms (Sections 1 and 2.2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Multidisciplinary benchmark:** Sci-VBench contains 1,253 expert-authored and independently reviewed tasks across 60 subjects in Natural Science, Healthcare, Humanities & Social Sciences, and Engineering (Sections 3 and 3.5).
- **Mechanism-requiring task design:** Minimal prompts specify observable setups or interventions but omit expected outcomes, requiring generators to infer and visualize the underlying mechanism over time (Section 3.3).
- **Reusable evaluation specifications:** Every task includes a phase-based reference guide and observable 1–5 rubric anchors for Prompt Grounding, Scientific and Causal Correctness, and Spatiotemporal Consistency (Section 3.4).
- **Scalable evaluation protocol:** Expert ratings provide reference labels; VBench-derived Vision Tools assess low-level perceptual fidelity, while a rubric-conditioned video MLLM judges the other dimensions over three independent runs (Sections 4.1–4.2).
- **Broad model study:** The authors evaluate eight proprietary and eight open-source text-to-video systems and analyze evaluator reliability, model-family gaps, domain variation, errors, and prompt rewriting (Sections 4.3 and 5).

These results support the authors’ claim that the benchmark distinguishes mechanistic correctness that the chosen perceptual proxy does not resolve well. They also support the portability of the rubrics across experts, non-experts, and MLLM judges. They do not establish that the automatic judges are interchangeable with experts, that low-level perceptual quality is objectively saturated, or that the observed proprietary–open-source gap generalizes beyond the tested models and settings.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope restriction:** Tasks exclude scientific concepts whose correctness cannot be verified from video evidence alone (Section 3.3).
- **Author-stated cost restriction:** Proprietary systems were evaluated only on the 150-example testmini split, whereas open-source systems were also evaluated on all 1,253 examples (Section 3.5).
- **Unequal generation conditions:** Models used default configurations with different resolutions, frame rates, and durations—5 to 15 seconds—which may affect temporal and perceptual comparisons (Appendix A.3).
- **Incomplete generations:** Provider filters rejected six Gemini-Omni-Flash prompts and two Seedance-2.0 prompts; their averages use only successful generations (Table 4 caption).
- **Automatic-evaluator limits:** The authors report weaker MLLM agreement on fine-grained perceptual and temporal artifacts than on PG and SCC (Section 4.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Because Qwen3.5-397B-A17B is both selected as the strongest tested judge and used for automatic benchmarking, some reported model differences may depend on that evaluator’s sensitivities.
- **Analyst inference:** The prompt-rewriting study covers only two open-source generators and one rewriting model, so it cannot isolate whether improvements generalize across generator or rewriter families (Section 5.4).
- **Analyst inference:** “Expert” annotators include 11 undergraduates, 45 graduate students, and five authors; matching expertise is asserted, but the supplied text does not provide enough biography detail to assess expertise consistently across subjects (Section 3.2; Appendix A.4).

## Open research questions

1. **How robust are rankings to the choice of automatic judge?** The paper compares judge–expert correlations but reports benchmark rankings primarily with one MLLM judge. A next experiment could compute rank stability across several rubric-conditioned judges and an expert-only subset.

2. **How much do unequal clip duration and resolution influence the four scores?** Models were evaluated under different supported defaults. A controlled experiment could standardize duration, resolution, frame rate, and post-processing for models that permit common settings.

3. **Can training on explicit mechanisms close the scientific-correctness gap?** Prompt rewriting improves SCC but does not close the gap. A next experiment could compare mechanism-annotated fine-tuning, rubric-guided preference optimization, and prompt rewriting on a held-out subject split.

4. **Does evaluator portability hold for unfamiliar or disputed scientific scenarios?** The study validates specifications within its curated, visually verifiable tasks. A next experiment could test independent experts, non-experts, and judges on new subjects and scenarios with multiple defensible interpretations.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text gives detailed methods and selected numerical findings, but it omits the actual cells of Tables 1–5 and 7–9, complete subject lists, full per-model scores, exact correlations for each MLLM and prior evaluator, sampling details for non-expert cohorts, uncertainty estimates or significance tests, generation randomness and seed controls, compute costs, and licensing or access terms for the released benchmark.
