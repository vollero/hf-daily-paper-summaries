# RynnValue: Scaling Robotic Value Foundation Models with Temporal Distance

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-11.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.09853) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09853)


## One-sentence takeaway

RynnValue learns language-conditioned time-to-completion from timestamped robot trajectories and uses it as a dense reward, outperforming preference-supervised ranking models and improving real-world online and offline robot-learning success.

## Problem and motivation

General-purpose robot learning needs rewards that transfer across tasks, embodiments, and data sources. The authors argue that hand-designed or sparse rewards scale poorly, while preference labels and normalized trajectory progress depend on task-internal anchors that are expensive or inconsistent across trajectories of different durations and structures (Section 1).

Their proposed alternative is goal-conditioned temporal distance: the remaining time from an observation to a language-specified completion point. It approximates minimum-time cost-to-go, can be labeled from timestamps once completion cutoffs are identified, and can be converted into dense rewards (Sections 1 and 3.3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Scalable supervision:** Convert heterogeneous robot, simulation, and egocentric trajectories into instruction-conditioned segments, relabel semantic completion cutoffs, and derive remaining-time targets directly from timestamps. The resulting mixture contains over 7,000 hours and 3,091,969 segments (Section 3.1; Table 1).
- **Language-conditioned value model:** RynnValue predicts absolute time-to-completion and signed relative temporal displacement using separate distributional heads over grouped visual-value queries (Section 2).
- **Shortcut suppression:** Irregular temporal sampling, temporal-order shuffling, and value-isolation attention prevent predictions from relying on fixed intervals, sequence position, or other value-query representations (Sections 2 and 3.2; Figure 2).
- **Auxiliary semantic grounding:** Natural-language video description, instruction matching, and success prediction jointly train the backbone; 10% instruction-mismatch augmentation teaches instruction–trajectory discrimination (Sections 2 and 3.2.2).
- **Reward interface:** Negated predicted temporal distance becomes a potential and is combined with sparse completion rewards through potential-based shaping for offline IQL and online DSRL (Section 3.3; Equation 11; Appendix B).

The results support the authors’ claim that timestamp-derived temporal distance can provide useful OOD rankings and reward shaping without trajectory-preference labels. The ablations also support the importance of the complete training recipe, particularly order shuffling.

They do not isolate temporal distance from every associated change against a fully matched progress-supervision system, establish statistical significance, or demonstrate broad deployment beyond four manipulation tasks and one real-world platform. The policy comparisons also use different shaping coefficients for RynnValue and Robometer.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Temporal distance assumes an approximately minimum-time objective and therefore omits costs such as safety, energy, and precision (Section 5).
- **Author-stated:** The model uses a short sampled observation window; longer-horizon and streaming inference remain future work (Section 5).
- **Author-stated:** Evaluation remains centered on manipulation, with dexterous hands and mobile manipulation identified as extensions (Section 5).
- **Author-stated:** Third-person RGB can conceal grasp stability and geometric alignment, limiting reward quality on precision-sensitive Box-in-Drawer Placement (Section 4.5.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Completion labels may inherit error from dataset-specific trimming and cutoff approximation; the paper says endpoints are defaults and trimming is applied “when necessary,” but does not report cutoff-label accuracy (Section 3.1.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Reward-model fairness is imperfect because shaping uses \(\kappa=0.1\) for RynnValue and \(1.0\) for Robometer; no coefficient-sensitivity study is reported (Section 4.5.1; Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference:** The real-world conclusions have uncertain statistical precision: 20 evaluation trials per task are reported, but confidence intervals, seeds, and significance tests are not.
- **Analyst inference:** Online initialization depends on task type, including Robometer-trained offline checkpoints for two tasks. Initialization is fixed across reward variants within each task, but this complicates interpretation of absolute online performance across tasks (Section 4.5.1; Appendix B.4).

## Open research questions

1. **Can temporal value incorporate safety, energy, or precision costs without losing cross-dataset scalability?** The current target approximates minimum completion time. A next experiment could compare time-only supervision with multi-cost targets on tasks containing explicit speed–safety or speed–accuracy tradeoffs.

2. **How robust is the method to erroneous completion cutoffs?** Cutoffs are partly approximated through endpoint and trimming rules, but label-noise sensitivity is unreported. A controlled experiment could perturb cutoffs by known temporal offsets and measure ranking, calibration, and RL success.

3. **Does it remain effective for long-horizon streaming control?** Training uses eight observations, while reward inference in the policy experiments uniformly subsamples histories to four frames. A next experiment could compare fixed-window, recurrent, and streaming variants on longer mobile-manipulation tasks.

4. **How much of the downstream gain depends on reward-scale tuning?** The two reward models use different shaping coefficients. A coefficient sweep under identical policies, datasets, and seeds could compare best-case performance and sensitivity to reward scaling.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported architecture, training recipe, benchmark results, and real-world settings because the supplied text includes detailed sections, tables, equations, and appendices.
