# Evo-Bench: Can Language Models Improve Agent Harness?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-11.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.09096) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09096)


## One-sentence takeaway

Evo-Bench finds that frontier LLMs can substantially improve a fixed policy model’s executable agent harness—up to +16.6 Overall points—but gains are domain-dependent, often saturate early, and remain slightly below a composite human-engineered baseline overall.

## Problem and motivation

The paper addresses how to evaluate an LLM’s intrinsic ability to iteratively improve an agent’s executable harness, rather than merely solve tasks inside a fixed harness. The authors argue that existing evaluations do not adequately separate harness quality from base-model strength, guard against validation-task overfitting, or measure sustained long-horizon engineering (§1).

This matters because harnesses govern reasoning, tool use, memory, and control flow, and harness evolution is presented as an initial, measurable form of agent self-improvement. The identified gap is a controlled benchmark where the policy model remains fixed, validation and held-out suites respond similarly to harness quality, and evolvers must repeatedly diagnose and modify code (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Evo-Bench benchmark:** Evaluates an evolver model’s ability to revise one initially minimal CodeAct harness for a fixed policy model across Search, Office, and General tasks. The final frozen harness is evaluated on a disjoint held-out suite (§3; Figure 2).

- **Harness-guided benchmark construction:** Auxiliary tasks induce diverse evolved harnesses; candidate tasks are then ranked by how strongly their scores correlate with leave-one-task-out harness quality. Difficulty-stratified selection is intended to preserve both harness sensitivity and validation/evaluation alignment (§4; Figure 3).

- **Controlled long-horizon protocol:** Runs share the seed harness, fixed evolve harness, policy model, and budgets; the principal budget is 20 evaluations, 1,000 evolver steps, and 48 hours (§5.1).

- **Empirical characterization:** Nine frontier or open-weight evolvers are compared, with analyses of domain effects, budget scaling, costs, cross-policy behavior, failure trajectories, and reward-hacking controls (§§5–6; Appendices C–D).

These results support the authors’ claim that several tested models can generate harness changes that improve held-out aggregate performance under the benchmark’s controlled policy setting. Table 3 also supports transfer across the three tested policy models at the Overall-score level.

They do not establish universal improvement across domains, models, or agent frameworks. They also do not show that evolved harnesses generally outperform human engineering: the overall comparator is a composite of three domain-specific frameworks, and the best evolved result remains below it. Because each main experiment was run once, the text does not establish statistical reliability across repeated evolution runs.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated limitations:** Evolvers respond superficially to aggregate scores, use domain routing instead of discovering shared mechanisms, and underuse their budgets. The GPT-5.6-Sol case retains a passive planner, append-only context, and permissive verifier (§5.3).

- **Author-observed temporal failures:** Models can introduce detrimental later changes, stop prematurely, misattribute regressions to noise, or fail to restore their best revision (§5.2; Appendix D).

- **Restricted task coverage:** Multimodal candidates were explicitly excluded, and coding and scientific-research tasks are deferred to future work (§4.2; §8).

- **Single-run uncertainty:** Every experiment was run once (§5.1), so variance from stochastic evolution, policy rollouts, and model judging is unavailable.

- **Analyst inference — benchmark-selection scope:** Selecting tasks for positive correlation with a 12-harness auxiliary set makes Evo-Bench intentionally sensitive to harness differences, but may limit generalization to tasks or harness designs outside that selection regime (§4). Appendix A.3 also reports eight slightly negative-sensitivity Claw-Eval quota backfills, qualifying the stated non-positive-sensitivity filtering rule.

- **Analyst inference — comparator interpretation:** The Artificial Harness is a domain-specific composite rather than one shared general-purpose harness (§5.1), so its 47.5 Overall score is useful as a performance reference but not a perfectly matched engineering comparison.

- **Grading validity:** Multiple datasets use a fixed LLM judge, and the supplied text does not report inter-judge agreement or sensitivity to judge choice (Table 1; Appendix C.1).

- **Integrity risk:** Evolvers can inspect expected answers and validation artifacts, creating leakage and reward-hacking opportunities. The authors apply sandboxing, pattern scans, and semantic audits, but only MiniMax M3’s detected evasion is described; independent audit accuracy is not reported (Appendix C.2).

## Open research questions

1. **Would improvements persist across repeated evolution runs?**  
   This remains open because the study reports one run per configuration. A next experiment could repeat each evolver–policy pairing with several seeds and report confidence intervals for Overall, AnytimeVal, and best-to-frozen regression.

2. **How dependent is Evo-Bench sensitivity on the 12 auxiliary harnesses?**  
   Candidate tasks were selected using harnesses generated by four frontier evolvers. Reconstructing the benchmark with disjoint evolver families, or evaluating rejected tasks afterward, could test whether rankings remain stable.

3. **Can models discover shared architectures rather than domain routers?**  
   The authors report that current evolvers mainly resolve interference through routing and localized modifications. An experiment could constrain or remove explicit domain labels and compare router-based harnesses with shared planner, memory, and verifier redesigns.

4. **How robust are results to judge and framework changes?**  
   The main protocol uses one judge and a custom evolve harness inspired by Claude Code. Regrading with multiple independent judges and reproducing evolution in other agent frameworks would test evaluation and implementation dependence.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported protocol and tabulated results because the supplied text includes the main paper and appendices; **medium** for broader conclusions about intrinsic or general harness-evolution ability.

Material missing information includes repeated-run variance, exact numerical budget-ablation points from Figure 7, per-model cost values beyond the textual thresholds, judge-agreement studies, audit error rates, public artifact URLs or licenses, and evaluations on multimodal, coding, scientific-research, or additional agent-framework settings.
