# Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-11.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.09867) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09867)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors show that portable encrypted reasoning blocks could be replayed into weaker models from the same API provider to recover hidden reasoning, exposing proprietary traces, private data, harmful content, and an invisible prompt-injection channel—although provider mitigations later made the reported attacks unreproducible.

## Problem and motivation

Proprietary LLM APIs encrypt client-side reasoning traces to protect intellectual property and sensitive information while preserving stateless, multi-turn operation. The authors argue that these envelopes authenticate content without adequately binding it to a user, session, conversational position, or model, making traces portable across security boundaries (Sections 1, 2.2–2.3; Appendix A).

The identified gap is that earlier work established replayability across contexts, whereas this paper investigates cross-user and cross-model compatibility and its consequences: reasoning extraction, distillation, secret recovery, harmful-information disclosure, and hidden prompt injection (Sections 1–4).

## Approach and contributions

- **Cross-model extraction attack:** Capture an encrypted trace from a protected source model, replay it into a compatible weaker model, and prompt that model to transcribe the reasoning. The decoder models were Haiku 4.5, GPT-5.6 Luna, and Gemini Robotics 1.6 for the respective provider families (Section 2.4).
- **Compatibility characterization:** Test in-session, cross-session, cross-user, and cross-model replay across Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google model families (Section 2.3; Table 1; Figure 2).
- **Attack demonstrations:** Apply the vulnerability to proprietary-reasoning extraction, harmful-content recovery, large-scale privacy leakage, and opaque prompt injection (Sections 3–4).
- **Privacy audit:** Decode blocks from publicly shared agent trajectories and classify recovered privacy artifacts using an LLM-as-a-judge pipeline (Sections 2.5 and 4.1; Figure 6).
- **Mitigations:** Propose user/session/context-bound encryption, key rotation, cross-model isolation, replay detection, server-side storage, and decoder refusal training (Section 5.5; Appendix A, Table 2).

These results support the authors’ claim that replayable reasoning envelopes created a practical security and privacy exposure in the tested API versions. They do not prove exact recovery of every original reasoning token: plaintext ground truth was unavailable, token-count agreement is only a proxy, and decoder generation was stochastic. The Appendix B observations establish behavioral similarity under prefilling, not memorization or distillation.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Evaluation covers only selected Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google API versions available in early July 2026; proprietary implementations could change without notice (Section 5.1).
- **Author-stated:** Exact extraction fidelity cannot be verified because the original plaintext reasoning is unavailable and decoder outputs are stochastic (Sections 2.4 and 5.1).
- **Author-stated:** The public-trace collection is a targeted, non-exhaustive scan rather than a prevalence audit (Sections 4.1 and 5.1).
- **Author-stated:** Providers mitigated the issue after disclosure, so the principal Figure 1 attacks were no longer reproducible by August 2026 (page 14).
- **Author-stated:** Appendix B is small, benchmark-skewed, based on fuzzy reconstruction, and cannot causally establish distillation (Appendix B disclaimer, page 22).
- **Analyst inference:** Privacy counts depend on an LLM-as-a-judge procedure whose detailed validation is omitted from the supplied portion; Appendix D is listed but its substantive contents are absent.
- **Analyst inference:** Figure 1 reports qualitative closeness between token counts, but the supplied text lacks aggregate fidelity statistics, error distributions, and plaintext-based validation.
- **Analyst inference:** The corpus mixes genuine and synthetic benchmark identities, so headline totals should not be interpreted as counts of affected real people.

## Open research questions

1. **How exact is reconstructed reasoning?** Token-count agreement does not establish token-level identity. A next experiment could evaluate recovery against provider-controlled plaintext traces using edit distance and semantic consistency.
2. **Which cryptographic binding offers the best security–usability tradeoff?** The proposed hash-chain and identity binding may interfere with compaction, branching, and model switching. A prototype could benchmark replay resistance, storage cost, latency, and compatibility across these workflows.
3. **Can decoder-side defenses close the residual same-context attack surface?** Appendix A labels transcription refusal training as future work. A next experiment could train such defenses and evaluate adaptive jailbreak success and false refusals on legitimate continuation tasks.
4. **What is the real prevalence of sensitive data in private deployments?** The public scan is non-exhaustive and likely selection-biased. A privacy-preserving audit across consenting production or enterprise deployments could measure leakage without retaining recovered secrets.
