# Cultivar: A Contrastive and Locale-Oriented Translation Benchmark for Investigating Contamination and Localisation Robustness

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-11.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.09766) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09766)


## One-sentence takeaway

Cultivar pairs structurally comparable FLORES sentences with locale-specific variants, revealing broadly stable model rankings but weaker localisation robustness in many MT-specialised and smaller models, possible FLORES overfitting, and better performance on US-grounded Hindi and Chinese content.

## Problem and motivation

Multilingual translation benchmarks commonly originate in English and translate identical content into many languages. The authors identify three consequences: public test content can contaminate training data; source-to-English evaluation can mismatch realistic, natively produced source content; and treating each language as one cultural unit obscures regional and diaspora variation (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

Existing culturally grounded datasets improve authenticity but can lose controlled comparability. The paper therefore seeks an evaluation design that varies locale-specific content while preserving sentence structure, allowing localisation sensitivity to be separated from general translation ability (Section 1, p. 2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Source-contrastive benchmark:** Cultivar localises 200 named-entity-rich FLORES dev sentences for each of 27 locales while retaining broadly parallel structure between original and localised instances (Sections 2.1–2.4, pp. 2–4).

- **LLM–human construction pipeline:** spaCy-assisted entity identification and manual selection are followed by GPT-5.5 paraphrasing and review by annotators proficient in both languages who have lived in the target location (Sections 2.1–2.3, pp. 2–3).

- **Contrastive robustness evaluation:** The authors define paired ΔBLEU and ΔchrF as localised-minus-original scores and introduce lexical false-positive and false-negative measures for removed and newly introduced locale-specific words (Section 3.2, p. 5).

- **Broad model study:** Thirty-two open-weight MT models, translation-tuned LLMs, and general LLMs spanning 0.8–122B parameters are evaluated on FLORES and Cultivar (Sections 3.1 and 4, pp. 5–10).

- **Released asset:** The dataset is reported as available under CC BY-SA 4.0 (p. 1).

The evidence establishes that paired localisation can alter scores and expose model- and locale-dependent behavior while largely retaining aggregate ranking consistency. It supports the authors’ claim that some models are less robust to localisation.

It does **not** establish that the large score drops are caused by training-set contamination: lexical FP analysis finds no direct verbatim-memorisation signal, and the paper presents overfitting or contamination only as a possible explanation. Likewise, the two-language comparison is evidence of US-favouring behavior in those particular settings, not proof of a universal bias across languages or applications.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Cultivar covers only source-to-English translation; its findings do not directly establish robustness for English-to-other-language or non-English-to-non-English directions (Section 2, p. 2).

- **Author-stated data-artifact risk:** Locale difficulty may reflect both model behavior and artifacts introduced by LLM localisation or individual annotators (Section 4.4, p. 8).

- **Author-stated metric caveat:** Lexical false negatives are an upper bound on failures because valid translations may differ from the benchmark wording (Section 3.2, p. 5).

- **Author-stated scale caveat:** The controlled cross-location analysis covers only Hindi and Chinese, while manual error analysis is limited to Chinese-to-English outputs from selected models (Sections 4.5 and 5.1, pp. 8–9).

- **Analyst inference:** Each locale’s 200 examples were reviewed by one annotator, so annotator preferences are confounded with locale; no inter-annotator agreement or independent cultural validation is reported (Section 2.4, p. 4).

- **Analyst inference:** Selection specifically favors sentences with at least two named entities and excludes some numeral-, medicine-, and science-heavy examples, limiting representativeness beyond entity-centric localisation (Section 2.1, p. 2).

- **Analyst inference:** Because sentence structure is deliberately retained, Cultivar is well suited to controlled comparison but may not capture broader differences in locally authored discourse, syntax, or topic distributions (Section 2, p. 2).

- **Analyst inference:** No causal contamination audit using training data, canaries, or controlled exposure is reported; score discrepancies and error patterns cannot distinguish contamination from domain/style sensitivity.

## Open research questions

1. **Does the apparent US advantage persist across more languages and diaspora settings?** It remains open because the same-language location comparison uses only Hindi and Chinese. A next experiment could construct balanced native-region, US, and other-diaspora localisations for a larger language sample with matched entity types.

2. **Can contamination be separated from ordinary localisation fragility?** The lexical probe finds no direct verbatim memorisation despite large score drops. A controlled experiment could train otherwise identical models with and without FLORES exposure and compare their Δ metrics and error profiles.

3. **How reliable are locale-level conclusions across annotators?** One person contributes each locale, leaving locale and annotator effects intertwined. Multiple independent annotators per locale, followed by agreement and cross-review analysis, would quantify this variance.

4. **Does source-contrastive evaluation generalise beyond entity replacement and into-English translation?** Cultivar intentionally focuses on named-entity-rich, structurally preserved examples. Future tests could vary idioms, pragmatics, discourse structure, and target directions while measuring whether paired comparability remains valid.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the benchmark design, reported experimental results, and stated caveats because the supplied text includes the complete methods, tables, and analysis.

Material missing information includes per-example data, model decoding prompts and generation settings, statistical significance or uncertainty for most score differences, inter-annotator reliability, independent cultural-quality assessment, detailed compute/runtime results, and direct evidence about the evaluated models’ training-data exposure to FLORES.
