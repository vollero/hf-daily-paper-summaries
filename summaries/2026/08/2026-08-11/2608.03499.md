# WeClawArena: An Auditable Sandbox and Benchmark for Cross-User Agents Collaboration and Security in Human-Centered Agent Networks

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-11.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.03499) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03499)


## One-sentence takeaway

WeClawArena evaluates whether owner-bound agents can jointly complete tool-based tasks across private workspaces while resisting collaboration, security, privacy, and governance attacks, revealing that task success and final harm are related but distinct outcomes.

## Problem and motivation

Persistent personal agents increasingly act over users’ files, databases, tools, policies, and messages while collaborating with other users’ agents. This creates a distributed setting in which information, capabilities, and decision rights are split across owners, so agents must coordinate without leaking protected data, trusting poisoned evidence, or bypassing consent and authority boundaries.

The authors argue that prior benchmarks cover tool use, multi-agent coordination, social interaction, or agent security separately, but lack an end-to-end environment combining verifiable joint tool use, owner-scoped workspaces, adversarial pressure, and auditable runtime evidence (Section 1; Table 2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Problem formulation:** Models users as principals whose delegated agents operate under owner-specific visibility and authority constraints. Files, databases, tools, policies, consent, and approval records are part of both the task state and its boundaries (Sections 2.1–2.3).

- **Benchmark:** Provides 124 base tasks across bargaining, bidding, travel, SWE-Workspace, clinical, and trading. Every base task has one benign control and four matched attack variants, yielding 620 scenarios (Section 3; Table 4).

- **Four harm surfaces:** Tests collaboration disruption, compromised resource or evidence integrity, unauthorized information disclosure, and invalid authority or consent paths (Section 2.3).

- **Auditable sandbox:** Runs separate owner workspaces in Docker and mediates messages, tools, resource operations, and policy decisions through a gateway that preserves typed evidence and final states (Appendix A–B; Figure 5).

- **Separated evaluation:** Measures deterministic, domain-specific task success independently from post-hoc, LLM-judged attack success requiring both final harm and an evidence link to the attack (Section 3.2; Appendix I.1).

- **Validation and release:** Compares two LLM judges and a 200-row expert pilot, while releasing scenario manifests, runtime code, verifiers, judge prompts, scorecards, and selected sanitized evidence; full exploit payloads are restricted (Appendix H; Appendix L, Table 12).

The evidence supports the authors’ central claim that task utility cannot substitute for explicit harm auditing: some runs completed the visible task while producing privacy, integrity, or authority violations. It also demonstrates substantial differences across models, domains, and attack surfaces within this sandbox.

It does not establish real-world attack rates, causal robustness outside the authored variants, or a definitive safety ranking. ASR depends on post-hoc judges, scoreability filtering, simulated workspaces, and unequal coverage for some model cells.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** ASR relies on a headline LLM judge. Swapping to Opus 4.7 increased model-surface macro ASR by 3.3 percentage points overall, although aggregate model ranking was unchanged (Appendix H; Table 8).

- **Author-stated:** Human validation covers only 200 rows and two author-annotators with attack-domain expertise. The authors acknowledge that agreement may change with a larger, more diverse panel (Appendix H).

- **Author-stated:** Attack-success claims require all scored actions and final artifacts to pass through the gateway. Any bypass falls outside the benchmark’s audit boundary (Appendices A–B).

- **Author-stated:** Some model evaluations have partial attacked-mode or judged coverage, notably Kimi K2.5 and Qwen3 235B, weakening direct comparisons involving those rows (Tables 3, 10, and 11).

- **Author-stated:** Full attack payloads are redacted, summarized, or access-controlled for safety. This limits fully open replication of exact attacks despite the release of predicates and metadata (Appendix L).

- **Analyst inference:** The six domains are simulated, structured workflows. The supplied evidence does not show transfer to uncontrolled personal-agent deployments, arbitrary communication channels, or other ownership regimes.

- **Analyst inference:** No ablation isolates the contribution of gateway mediation, matched variants, particular evidence fields, or judge context. Their individual effects on score reliability therefore remain unquantified.

- **Analyst inference:** Excluding malformed or unscorable executions is operationally reasonable, but model-dependent readiness failures could bias reported denominators; the supplied text does not report exclusion rates by model and domain.

## Open research questions

1. **How well does benchmark resistance transfer to deployed personal-agent networks?**  
   The paper evaluates Dockerized, gateway-mediated scenarios rather than uncontrolled deployments. A next experiment could replay matched tasks in several real agent frameworks and compare TSR, ASR, and trace completeness against sandbox results.

2. **Can deterministic or human-calibrated evaluators reduce reliance on LLM attack judges?**  
   Judge agreement is substantial but imperfect, and the expert pilot is small. A next study could label a larger stratified sample with independent non-author experts and train or validate surface-specific deterministic checks.

3. **Which audit evidence is necessary for reliable harm attribution?**  
   Evidence packets contain messages, tools, observations, scores, and governance context, but the paper does not ablate them. A controlled masking experiment could remove each evidence class and measure changes in human and inter-judge agreement.

4. **What defenses improve safety without sacrificing cross-user utility?**  
   The benchmark characterizes vulnerabilities but does not evaluate a systematic defense suite. A plausible experiment would compare provenance checks, information-flow controls, scoped capabilities, and approval validation on matched TSR and ASR.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high.** The supplied text includes the main paper, appendices, task definitions, raw counts, metric formulas, judge validation, and release plan.

Material missing or unresolved:

- Exclusion counts and readiness-failure rates by model, domain, and variant.
- Full attack payloads and unrestricted evidence packets.
- Exact model-generation settings beyond the common runtime and turn caps.
- Costs, latency, and compute requirements.
- Independent reproduction results.
- A broad, non-author human evaluation of ASR.
- Evidence of transfer from the sandbox to live personal-agent deployments.
