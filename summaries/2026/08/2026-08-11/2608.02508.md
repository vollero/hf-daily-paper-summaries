# RoMeRL: Balancing Feedback Coverage and the Memory-Reward Trap in Self-Evolving Agent Memory via Reduced-Order Utility States

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-11.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.02508) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02508)


## One-sentence takeaway

RoMeRL replaces an ever-growing trajectory-level memory utility space with four persistent per-task coordinates, concentrating outcome feedback while improving agent success and reducing reward contamination, memory size, and inference cost on the reported benchmarks.

## Problem and motivation

Outcome-driven agent memories commonly assign a utility to every stored trajectory. As histories grow, limited feedback is spread across more utilities, producing cold-start values and sparse updates. Increasing exploration improves coverage but also retrieves more irrelevant or outdated memories; because all co-retrieved memories share the trajectory reward, unhelpful memories can receive positive updates—the authors’ “memory-reward trap” (MRT) (§1; Figure 1).

The identified gap is that exploration can reduce uncertainty around observational returns but cannot recover each memory’s causal contribution. Existing trajectory-indexed systems also leave the support exposed to misattributed rewards growing with memory-bank size (§4.1; Theorems 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Reduced-order utility state:** RoMeRL maps each task’s variable-length history into four fixed semantic coordinates formed by outcome polarity—positive or negative—and memory dynamics—consolidated or adaptive (§5, Equations 7–10).

- **Role-specific memory management:** PCC retains the most efficient success; PAC retains the first success following a failure; NCC promotes a failed trajectory with strong downstream utility; and NAC tracks the most recent failure. Representatives are retained, promoted, or replaced rather than permanently added (§5.1, Equations 13–19).

- **Runtime learning without backbone updates:** Up to four active memories are ranked using embedding similarity plus learned utility, and retrieved coordinates receive exponential task-outcome updates (§5.1, Equations 14–15 and 20–21).

- **Formal analysis:** The paper distinguishes observational return from marginal utility, derives the resulting credit gap, gives a sufficient feedback budget for full-pool estimation, analyzes feedback concentration under state reduction, and bounds erroneous-coordinate occupancy under a generic transition model (§4.1; §5, Theorems 1–3 and Proposition 1).

- **Empirical evaluation and released implementation:** The method is evaluated on ALFWorld and the OS/DB subsets of LifelongAgentBench; the abstract supplies a public code repository.

The evidence supports the authors’ claim that RoMeRL improves the reported aggregate performance and efficiency while reducing measured feedback sparsity and synthetic noise persistence in the evaluated settings. It does not establish causal credit assignment, universal superiority outside these benchmarks, or that every gain results specifically from reducing MRT exposure. Exact per-task results, uncertainty estimates, and cross-model transfer numbers are absent from the supplied table text.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** RoMeRL still uses outcome-level rewards and therefore does not solve causal credit assignment (§7).

- **Author-stated:** Estimating the transition parameters \(\gamma\) and \(\lambda\) requires coordinate-level causal labels from paired counterfactual rollouts or equivalent attribution (§7).

- **Author-stated:** Open-ended and longer-horizon evaluation remains future work (§7).

- **Theory boundary:** The feedback and variance results assume stationary targets, bounded rewards, independent samples, and approximately balanced allocation. The appendix notes that adaptive stopping may require separate analysis and that a fixed-step EMA retains a nonzero variance floor (Assumption 1; Theorems 1–3; Appendix A.1–A.3).

- **Analyst inference:** The stress test is informative but narrow: it uses one OS setting and synthetic null-action corruption introduced only in the first round. This does not establish robustness to other contamination mechanisms (Appendix B.5).

- **Analyst inference:** The ablation removes NCC and PAC individually but the supplied text reports no PCC/NAC ablations, alternative state dimensions, or exact ablation scores, limiting attribution of gains to the full 2×2 design (§6.2; Figure 4).

- **Analyst inference:** Cross-model transfer is described as improving all four model–task combinations, but the numerical Table 3 contents and model identities are missing from the retrieved text (§6.1; Table 3).

## Open research questions

1. **Can RoMeRL estimate marginal memory contribution rather than observational return?** This remains open because outcome-level rewards preserve the credit gap (§7). A next experiment could compare task-level updates with paired memory-present/memory-absent rollouts or per-memory counterfactual rewards.

2. **Is the four-coordinate factorization optimal?** The theory supports bounded dimension generally, but does not prove that the particular 2×2 semantics are best. An experiment could compare four coordinates against learned slot counts and alternative role partitions under matched memory and call budgets.

3. **Does replacement remain reliable in open-ended, nonstationary tasks?** Current theory assumes stationary targets or stationary transition behavior, while evaluation covers finite benchmark runs. A next experiment could introduce task-distribution shifts and measure recovery, forgetting, and erroneous-coordinate residence time.

4. **How robust is RoMeRL to realistic memory corruption?** The reported stress test nulls a key field while preserving retrieval cues. Future tests could introduce stale procedures, subtly incorrect actions, adversarially plausible memories, and conflicting evidence at multiple training rounds.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text gives detailed method definitions, theoretical qualifications, benchmark scope, and several headline comparisons. However, the actual cells of Tables 1–6 are unavailable, including per-task scores, transfer results, model/API versions, hyperparameter values, data partitions, and several baseline efficiency figures.
