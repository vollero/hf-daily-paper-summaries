# Automata from Agent Traces: Failure and Next-Step Prediction

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.23670) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23670)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors show that merging agent-trace prefixes by their most recent activity yields small deterministic automata that retain near-perfect replay fitness and provide useful context for next-step prediction, failure prediction, and early runtime monitoring.

## Problem and motivation

LLM agents produce long, unstructured execution traces whose latent workflows are difficult to audit, debug, or monitor. The authors argue that prior approaches analyze individual traces, require manually specified workflows or task descriptions, or learn only from successful runs, missing shared topology across successful and failed executions (§1–2).

The challenge is underdetermined: a generating language cannot generally be identified from positive traces alone. The paper exploits the narrower empirical setting in which agent traces have bounded activity alphabets—6–42 symbols in the evaluated datasets—to recover a compact directly-follows abstraction rather than the true generating automaton (§1, §3.4).

## Approach and contributions

- **Deterministic FSM extraction:** Messages are mapped to activities using dataset-specific rules, inserted into a prefix tree, and then merged by incoming/last activity. Transitions observed once are removed unless they are the source’s only continuation (§3.2–3.3; Algorithm 1).
- **Structural guarantees:** Before rare-transition filtering, merging preserves training fitness and produces a deterministic FSM with one state per activity plus an initial state. A probabilistic convergence guarantee is given under i.i.d. traces and a minimum transition-observation probability (§3.4; Theorems 2–3, Proposition 5).
- **Unified prediction substrate:** FSM states and transition counts support smoothed next-action probabilities, LLM workflow context, per-state failure features, anomaly scores, and online monitoring (§3.5, §4.2–4.3).
- **Broad empirical comparison:** The method is evaluated on twelve public trace datasets against automata-learning, HMM, process-mining, and workflow-memory baselines (§4.1; Table 5).
- **Minimal LLM context format:** The paper identifies a compact representation containing next-action probabilities and the top 15 continuations; this format substantially outperforms more verbose FSM prompts in the selected tau2-bench retail setting (§4.2; Table 3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Compact replay models | Across all twelve datasets, FSMs have 7–43 states, at least 0.997 test replay fitness, and 15–3,036× fewer states than RPNI. | §4.2; Tables 1, 7–11 |
| Strong rejection of corrupted traces | On the eight labeled real-trace datasets, the FSM rejects 100% of uniformly random traces and at least 99.9% of permuted traces. | §4.2; Table 18 |
| Lower next-step cross-entropy | On the five datasets in Table 2, order-1 FSM prediction averages 0.934 bits versus 2.439 for unigram and 3.397 for RPNI; FSM-LR averages 0.729 bits. | Table 2; §4.2 |
| Controlled state-conditioning gain | With depth-5 absolute discounting, FSM conditioning reduces mean CE from 0.735 to 0.580 bits across six datasets, a reported 0.155-bit or 21% improvement. | §4.2; Appendix G.9 |
| Better LLM workflow context | With `gpt-4.1-mini`, the selected minimal FSM context beats AWM on all eight datasets, with gains from 0.8 to 25.3 percentage points; six gaps have \(p<10^{-8}\). | Table 4; §4.2 |
| Failure prediction | The main gradient-boosted FSM-feature pipeline reaches held-out AUROC 0.703–0.941 across nine labeled datasets; the maximum is 0.941 on tau2-bench telecom. | §4.3; Table 14 |
| Structural signal beyond length | On SWE-agent, structural features reach AUROC 0.790 versus 0.659 for length alone; adding length changes the full result by less than 0.001. | Appendix H.3 |
| Early runtime intervention | On SWE-agent, the combined monitor reports 85.9% precision, 95.5% recall, F1 0.904, and intervention at 32% mean completion, saving 68% of remaining computation. | §4.3; Table 40 |
| Fast construction and replay | Reported construction is 1–110 ms and per-trace replay 0.003–0.015 ms; the shown RPNI comparisons take roughly 7,000–36,000 ms. | Table 13; Appendix D.4 |
| Partial cross-model transfer | Across 36 off-diagonal tau2-bench model pairs, mean failure-prediction AUROC is 0.786, versus 0.877 for within-model evaluation. | Table 6; Appendix C.1 |

The evidence supports the authors’ claim that this last-activity abstraction is compact and predictive on the evaluated trace corpora. It does not show recovery of the agents’ true generating automata, universal applicability to large or open-ended action spaces, or reliable early stopping across every domain: the monitor performs substantially worse on tau2-bench retail and SWE-smith (Table 40).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** The FSM represents the directly-follows closure, not the generating language; adversarial traces preserving activity-bigram statistics may replay successfully (§6).
- **Author-stated:** Activity extraction is dataset-specific and needs some domain knowledge; automatic extraction remains future work (§6).
- **Author-stated:** Cross-model transfer covers only three tau2-bench suites, while broader cross-architecture and cross-domain transfer remains untested (§6).
- **Author-stated:** The workflow-memory comparison covers AWM but not concurrent success-and-failure memory approaches such as ReasoningBank (§6).
- **Author-stated:** Compactness and downstream observation density may degrade for agents with larger action spaces or weaker conditional structure (§5).
- **Author-stated:** The convergence result assumes i.i.d. traces, which the authors acknowledge is only approximate for iterative deployments (§3.4).
- **Analyst inference:** The 80/20 evaluation uses one fixed split seed, although repeated cross-validation is reported for failure-prediction stability; broader split sensitivity for all headline tasks is therefore not fully established (Appendix D.3, §4.3).
- **Analyst inference:** The minimal LLM-context format was selected on tau2-bench retail and that dataset remains in the eight-row comparison. The authors separately report a similar or larger average advantage on the seven held-out datasets, but the overall 8/8 claim is not wholly based on format-selection-independent datasets (§4.2; Tables 3–4).
- **Analyst inference:** Several appendix tables report different AUROCs for variants or evolving feature pipelines, so comparisons must be matched by model, feature set, and split rather than treating all reported values as interchangeable (Tables 14–15, 23, 26, 42).

## Open research questions

1. **Can activities be discovered automatically without losing interpretability or predictive value?** Extraction remains dataset-specific (§6). A next experiment could compare learned activity clustering against the hand-designed extractor on all twelve datasets using state count, replay fitness, and held-out AUROC.

2. **How does the method scale to open-ended tool and action vocabularies?** The claimed compactness depends on alphabets of 6–42 symbols, and §5 anticipates degradation for larger spaces. A controlled experiment could progressively split activity categories and measure state growth, observation density, CE, and failure AUROC.

3. **Can higher-order abstractions reject bigram-preserving adversarial traces while remaining compact?** The directly-follows closure is explicitly vulnerable (§6). A comparison with bounded-history or hierarchical states could measure adversarial rejection, replay fitness, and state complexity.

4. **When is automatic early stopping operationally beneficial?** Strong results appear on SWE-agent and tau2-bench airline, but the monitor over-triggers on smaller/coarser FSMs (Table 40). A prospective evaluation could tune rules on one deployment period and measure false-stop cost, compute savings, and task success on a later period.
