# WikiSkill: Compiling Agent Experience into Persistent Knowledge for Skill Evolution

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.27454) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27454)


## One-sentence takeaway

WikiSkill improves reusable agent skills by compiling execution traces into a persistent structured wiki, outperforming prior skill-evolution methods on average across five benchmarks and five models while enabling substantial—but sometimes negative—cross-model skill transfer.

## Problem and motivation

Agent skills package domain-specific procedures into reusable, parameter-free modules, but developing them manually is difficult. Existing automated approaches refine skills from execution histories, yet the authors argue that learned insights remain scattered across traces, proposal histories, and feedback rather than forming a separate, persistent knowledge representation. This weakens reuse across optimization iterations and can lead to repeated failed interventions (§1, pp. 2–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Three-layer architecture:** WikiSkill separates immutable execution traces, a persistent wiki of structured patterns and evolution logs, and active executable skills (§3.1; Figure 2, pp. 4–5).
- **Knowledge-driven evolution loop:** An Inference Agent generates training traces; a Wiki Maintainer consolidates successes and failures; a ReAct-style Skill Proposer creates or patches one skill; and validation gating accepts only score-improving changes while retaining the wiki even after rejected edits (§3.2, pp. 5–6).
- **Persistent auditability:** Pattern pages, evolution logs, proposal diffs, acceptance decisions, and links from skills to motivating patterns preserve both positive and negative experience (§3.1–§3.2).
- **Empirical study:** The authors compare WikiSkill with Trace2Skill, EvoSkill, SkillOpt, and no-skill baselines across five tasks and five models, including ablations and cross-model transfer experiments (§4–§5).

The results support the authors’ claim that persistent, proposer-facing knowledge improves skill evolution under the tested models, tasks, prompts, and validation procedure. They also show that procedural knowledge can transfer across model sizes and families.

They do not establish that gains will persist with automatic skill retrieval, much longer tasks, larger skill libraries, or other agent harnesses. The scale trend is demonstrated only within three Qwen models, so it is evidence of complementarity in that family rather than a general scaling law. The transfer results also rule out any simple claim that evolved skills are universally portable.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:**

- Skills are injected directly into prompts, so retrieval and triggering failures are excluded; scalability to large skill collections is untested (§7, p. 14).
- Strict validation gating rejects neutral edits that might enable later improvements (§7).
- The growing wiki has no automatic pruning mechanism (§7).
- The benchmark suite excludes tasks lasting hundreds of actions or multiple hours and does not test adaptation within one long rollout (§7).
- Validation splits are small—10 to 40 examples depending on the benchmark—which may make gating decisions noisy (Table 6; Appendix B).

**Analyst inferences grounded in the text:**

- The persistent-knowledge ablation uses only Gemini-3.5-Flash and excludes ALFWorld, limiting how broadly its +15-point result can be generalized (Table 3).
- Wiki access is ablated as a bundle: removing proposer access also removes the Wiki Maintainer. The experiment therefore does not isolate proposer access from knowledge construction itself (§5.1).
- Comparisons may reflect differing optimization budgets. WikiSkill uses roughly 10–20 proposer ReAct calls plus one maintainer call per iteration, while the baselines have different call structures; Table 7 analyzes complexity but does not provide a matched total compute or monetary-cost comparison.
- Only three complete evolution runs are averaged. Bootstrap tests resample test instances, but the supplied text does not report confidence intervals over evolution-run variability (§4.2; Appendix C).
- Exact dataset-level scoring definitions beyond the general domain-specific score are not supplied in the retrieved text (§2).

## Open research questions

1. **Can WikiSkill retain its gains with automatic skill retrieval?** Retrieval was intentionally excluded. A next experiment could build a growing multi-domain skill library and compare full injection, learned routing, and retrieval under fixed context budgets.

2. **How should the wiki be compressed or pruned over long runs?** The authors identify unbounded accumulation but evaluate only iterations 0–7. A longer evolution study could compare recency pruning, evidence-weighted merging, and learned consolidation while measuring accuracy, wiki size, and inference cost.

3. **Can gating recognize edits whose value is delayed?** Strict immediate-improvement gating rejects neutral stepping-stone proposals. A next experiment could compare multi-step lookahead or uncertainty-aware acceptance against the current rule using held-out test performance and regression rates.

4. **What predicts positive versus negative skill transfer?** Table 2 shows both strong gains and severe degradation. A controlled transfer experiment could vary skill abstraction, granularity, interaction budget, and source/target capability to estimate which factors predict transfer success.
