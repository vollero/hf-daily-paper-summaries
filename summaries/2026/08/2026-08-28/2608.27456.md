# UrbanGround: From Local Perception to Spatial Agency in a Real-Scale City

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.27456) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27456)


## One-sentence takeaway

UrbanGround shows that contemporary multimodal agents can recognize and explore nearby urban scenes, but their performance deteriorates sharply when they must preserve orientation, complete longer routes, replan around closures, or avoid pedestrians.

## Problem and motivation

The paper asks whether local visual understanding by multimodal large language models (MLLMs) can support sustained, goal-directed action in a physically constrained city. Existing game environments have artificial mechanics; indoor environments are spatially bounded; street-view and aerial benchmarks move between sampled viewpoints; and prior urban simulators do not preserve the full georegistered structure of an existing metropolis. The authors identify a gap in evaluating perception, navigation, and adaptation within one continuous real-scale geographic frame (Introduction, pp. 2–4).

## Approach and contributions

- **Real-scale interactive environment:** UrbanGround streams Hong Kong’s territory-wide textured 3D map and pedestrian network into Unity, with collision-aware continuous motion and trajectories recorded in geographic coordinates (Sections 3.2–3.3, pp. 5–6).

- **Closed-loop agent interface:** MLLMs act from first-person RGB observations and, where appropriate, an interactive map that exposes position and task markers but no computed route, distance-to-goal, or route API (Sections 3.1 and 3.4, pp. 4–7; Appendix B, pp. 23–25).

- **Five-level evaluation ladder:** The benchmark progresses from local recognition, orientation, and active question answering to explicit and implicit navigation, multi-goal planning, road-closure replanning, and pedestrian-aware navigation (Section 3.5, Figures 3–5, pp. 7–8).

- **Broad empirical evaluation:** Ten MLLMs are evaluated on 810 manually constructed and twice-verified base instances, using a common embodiment, interface, prompts, and 100-step limit (Section 4.1, pp. 8–9; Appendix A, p. 21).

- **Diagnostic trajectory analyses:** Endpoint success is supplemented with pedestrian-network adherence, goal-distance progress, partial multi-stop completion, retrospective checkpoint rescoring, and failure-type analysis (Figure 8, p. 12; Appendices D–E, pp. 34–35).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Local recognition is substantially stronger than orientation. | Across ten models, visual-recognition accuracy ranges from **75.0% to 93.8%**, while orientation accuracy ranges from **23.3% to 58.3%**; both are four-option QA tasks. | Table 1, p. 9 |
| Local task correctness does not guarantee pedestrian-compliant movement. | Overall local-QA pedestrian-network adherence ranges from **73.6% to 80.2%**, despite overall answer accuracy reaching **79.1%** for Claude-Opus-5; adherence is the fraction of executed action time on the registered network. | Table 1 and metric definition, p. 9 |
| Navigation success collapses with route length. | Short-navigation success ranges from **15.0% to 75.0%**, whereas long-navigation success is only **0.0%–3.8%** under the same 100-step limit; endpoint success requires finishing within 15 m and satisfying enforced constraints. | Table 2, p. 11; Section 4.1, pp. 8–9 |
| Long-route agents often make progress without completing the task. | Although full LongNav success is near zero, **52.5%–81.3%** of episodes across models end closer to the goal than they began. | Figure 8, p. 12 |
| Late regression is a material but incomplete source of LongNav failure. | For GPT-5.5, observed scoring gives **0.0% success**, **57.5% closer-than-start**, and **98.9% mean remaining-distance ratio**; rescoring the closest checkpoint—without changing actions—gives **0.0%**, **98.7%**, and **67.3%**, respectively. | Table 7, p. 34 |
| GPT-5.5 failures are heterogeneous. | LongNav failures comprise **22.50%** stopping outside the arrival radius, **27.50%** never making substantial progress, **31.25%** making and later losing progress, and **18.75%** retaining progress at timeout; “substantial” means at least 20% horizontal distance reduction. | Figure 11, p. 35 |
| Lower visibility generally harms local QA, but navigation effects vary by model. | For example, Claude-Opus-5 local-QA accuracy falls from **79.1% clear** to **66.4% dusk** and **69.6% night**, while its short-navigation success changes from **48.8% clear** to **46.3% dusk** and **40.8% night**; other models show different orderings. Conditions are matched by location and task. | Table 3 and RQ3 discussion, p. 13 |
| Dynamic adaptation and pedestrian safety are weak. | Road-closure success is **0.0%–3.3%**, with safe-progress rates of **10.0%–46.7%** despite **93.0%–96.4%** network adherence. Pedestrian collision rates are **76.3%–90.0%**, while goal success is **0.0%–3.8%**. | Table 4, p. 14 |

The evidence supports the authors’ claim that local perception and short-range action do not reliably compose into persistent, adaptive city-scale behavior under this benchmark. It also separates endpoint failure from total lack of progress. It does not establish performance in real physical cities, superiority over specialized navigation systems, or that any particular internal memory or reasoning defect causes the failures; the working spatial state is explicitly analytical rather than directly observed (Section 3.1, pp. 4–5).

## Limitations and validity risks

The supplied text has no dedicated author-stated limitations section. Relevant scope constraints and risks are:

- **Author-stated scope:** UrbanGround is a simulated, georegistered replica of Hong Kong rather than deployment in the physical city; the authors frame it as a foundation for broader study, not proof of real-world capability (Conclusion, p. 14).

- **Author-stated interface limits:** Models receive screenshots, instructions, and interaction history but not current coordinates, remaining distance, shortest paths, or a route API. Results therefore characterize this restricted interface and prompting protocol (Sections 3.1 and 4.1, pp. 4, 8; Appendix B, pp. 23–25).

- **Analyst inference — geographic validity:** Evaluation is distributed across Hong Kong districts, but the text provides no experiments in another city. Generalization to different urban layouts, signage systems, languages, or pedestrian conventions is unestablished.

- **Analyst inference — simulator validity:** Collision, pedestrian motion, weather, and visual appearance are simulated. The text does not quantify correspondence between simulated sensing/dynamics and physical-world conditions.

- **Analyst inference — baseline coverage:** The experiments compare general-purpose MLLMs with one another, but no human performance numbers or specialized navigation-agent baselines are reported in the supplied main results. Human testers establish feasibility, not a performance ceiling (Section 4.1, p. 8).

- **Analyst inference — causal diagnosis:** The paper associates failures with unstable orientation, state persistence, and correction, but reports no component ablations—such as explicit memory, route planners, alternative visual-history sizes, or map access removal—that isolate these causes.

- **Analyst inference — evaluation horizon:** Every model receives 100 interaction steps. The authors argue tasks are human-feasible within that limit, but longer budgets could change timeout-related outcomes; GPT-5.5 retains substantial progress at timeout in 18.75% of LongNav failures (Figure 11, p. 35).

## Open research questions

1. **Can explicit spatial memory prevent progress loss?** The paper analytically attributes many failures to unstable spatial state but does not test a memory module. A next experiment could add a structured landmark-and-pose memory and compare endpoint regression and LongNav success against the fixed-history protocol.

2. **Which capability matters most: map interpretation, local path planning, or recovery?** LongNav agents often move closer without arriving, but the current evaluation does not causally separate these components. A factorial ablation could independently provide route waypoints, expanded visual history, and recovery feedback.

3. **Does performance transfer beyond Hong Kong and simulation?** All benchmark instances use one metropolitan region and simulated embodiment. A matched evaluation across another georegistered city—and a small physical-route validation—could test geographic and simulator transfer.

4. **Can safety improve without sacrificing goal progress?** High pedestrian-network adherence coexists with high collision rates and weak closure adaptation. A follow-up could introduce collision-aware control or explicit constraint-state tracking and measure the trade-off among success, path efficiency, closure compliance, and contact rate.
