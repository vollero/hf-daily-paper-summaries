# TTPO: Test-Time Policy Optimization

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.27448) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27448)


## One-sentence takeaway

TTPO enables label-free test-time adaptation for mathematical reasoning by distilling majority-agreeing rollouts and selectively penalizing disagreeing ones, improving Qwen3 models beyond existing label-free baselines and roughly matching label-supervised self-distillation.

## Problem and motivation

Existing reinforcement learning and on-policy self-distillation methods for LLM reasoning depend on ground-truth answers, which are unavailable when a model trains directly on test problems. Majority voting can supply pseudo-labels, but wrong votes can reinforce incorrect trajectories or corrupt every token supervised by an answer-conditioned teacher (§1).

The authors identify an asymmetry: although pseudo-labels are wrong for roughly 85% of prompts in their Qwen3-1.7B AIME 2026 analysis, about 79% of rollouts disagreeing with a wrong pseudo-label are also wrong. Thus, disagreement can support a comparatively robust negative signal, whereas directly distilling a corrupted pseudo-label is riskier (§3.2; Figure 1a).

## Approach and contributions

- **Asymmetric rollout routing:** TTPO partitions sampled trajectories by majority-vote answer. Agreeing trajectories receive on-policy self-distillation via forward KL; disagreeing trajectories receive negative GRPO advantages (§3.1–§3.5; Figure 2).

- **Token-level selection:** The distillation branch emphasizes tokens with high student entropy or teacher–student divergence. The RL branch penalizes only the top half of tokens ranked by an anomaly score combining negative log-probability and normalized certainty (§3.3–§3.4).

- **Answer-conditioned self-teacher:** The same model serves as teacher, with thinking mode enabled and the pseudo-label inserted as privileged context; the student operates without thinking mode (§3.1; Appendix A–B).

- **Empirical contribution:** The paper evaluates label-free TTPO across three Qwen3 scales, five competition-math benchmarks, both conventional training and direct test-set training, cross-benchmark transfer, and non-thinking inference (§4; Appendix D).

These results establish strong performance under the paper’s Qwen3 competition-math protocol and support the value of asymmetric routing and both token-selection components. They do not establish superiority for other model families, non-mathematical domains, smaller rollout budgets, or real-world deployment. Cross-benchmark improvements support transfer within the tested math suite, but do not by themselves prove domain-general reasoning.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:**

- TTPO depends on majority-vote quality. Small rollout budgets or problems for which no rollout is correct make both branches noisy (Appendix F).
- Evaluation is restricted to mathematical reasoning with automatically extractable, verifiable final answers; code and open-ended reasoning remain unexplored (Appendix F).
- The objective uses a fixed RL/distillation balance even though the appropriate balance may change during training (Appendix F).

**Analyst inferences:**

- The reported peak is selected across checkpoints—every 25 steps for TTPO/OPSD and across all checkpoints for GRPO/TTRL—so comparisons may be affected by unequal checkpoint-selection granularity (§4.1).
- Majority voting uses \(K=64\) trajectories of up to 16,000 tokens per problem and 4×H20 GPUs for TTPO, implying substantial test-time compute; no accuracy–compute sweep over \(K\) is reported (§4.1; Appendix A).
- Training gradients cover only the first 1,024 completion tokens despite sampling up to 16,000 tokens. Table 10 suggests this choice favors short trajectories, but the text does not report an ablation of the gradient-window length (Appendix A; Table 10).
- Results cover only Qwen3 models. The supplied experiments do not show whether the method transfers to other model families (§4.1).
- Figure 1’s noise statistics come from Qwen3-1.7B on AIME 2026; their stability across model scales and benchmarks is not quantified (§3.2).

## Open research questions

1. **How does TTPO behave under smaller rollout budgets?** The paper uses \(K=64\) and explicitly warns that small \(K\) degrades voting. A next experiment could sweep \(K\) while reporting accuracy, pseudo-label precision, and inference/training cost.

2. **Can routing adapt to consensus quality during training?** The authors use a fixed 50/50 update subset and \(\lambda=0.1\), despite changing positive fractions. An experiment could condition these quantities on vote margin or calibrated confidence and compare them with the fixed policy.

3. **Does the method extend beyond extractable math answers?** Open-ended and code domains are not evaluated. A next experiment could replace mathematical equivalence clustering with execution-based verification for code and measure both reward reliability and downstream performance.

4. **How important is the 1,024-token gradient window?** The paper argues that shorter rollouts work better because decisive steps fit inside this window, but does not vary it. A controlled sweep of gradient-window lengths could test accuracy, memory cost, and which reasoning stages receive useful updates.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the method description and reported within-paper comparisons because the supplied text includes the main experiments, appendices, equations, and configurations.

Material missing information includes independent replication, statistical uncertainty or variance across training runs, results on model families other than Qwen3, a compute-normalized comparison, a sweep over the 64-rollout voting budget, an ablation of gradient-window length, and evaluation outside competition mathematics.
