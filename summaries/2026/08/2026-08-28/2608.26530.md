# PILOT in the Loop: Live Self-Improvement for Long-Horizon Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.26530) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26530)


## One-sentence takeaway

PILOT separates execution from oversight so a supervisor can redirect active workers and persist reusable experience, achieving stronger long-horizon task performance and improving success-per-token across repeated Terminal-Bench 2.0 iterations.

## Problem and motivation

Long-horizon agent runs expose useful procedures, failures, and inefficiencies, but most self-improvement methods process this evidence only after execution ends. They therefore cannot rescue the run producing the evidence or immediately test a correction.

The authors identify an architectural gap: single-agent correction combines execution and oversight within one limited context, while conventional subagent delegation usually reports results only after the subagent finishes. They seek self-improvement that is both live and organizationally separate from task execution (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Live self-improvement formulation.** The paper defines a closed loop in which emerging execution evidence can both correct the current trajectory and update a persistent harness for future workers (Section 2; Figure 2).

2. **Supervisor–worker architecture.** PILOT gives isolated workers responsibility for execution while a separate supervisor receives notifications, questions, results, errors, and inactivity alerts. It can send queued steering guidance or abort a worker (Sections 2 and 2’s channel description, pp. 3–4).

3. **Persistent harness evolution.** The supervisor distils procedures, conventions, and failure modes into a skill library and memory. Model parameters remain frozen; “self-improvement” means changing this harness state, not training the backbone (Section 2, pp. 3–4).

4. **Empirical evaluation.** The authors compare PILOT with coding-agent harnesses across two frozen backbones and three benchmarks, then study repeated-task improvement, skill growth, token efficiency, and trace-level steering cases (Sections 3–4).

The results support the authors’ claim that PILOT is competitive across the tested harness–backbone settings and that its persistent state becomes more effective and token-efficient over repeated Terminal-Bench sweeps. The trace cases also demonstrate that live corrections can affect successful execution paths.

They do not establish that every aggregate gain is caused specifically by steering or skill distillation, that performance will improve on unseen task distributions, or that the approach is cost-effective after accounting for wall-clock, compute, and supervisor overhead. “Best observed” iterative results also do not show monotonic improvement or statistical significance.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations**

- Iterative evaluation is expensive, restricting experiments to three benchmarks and two open-weight backbones; proprietary models and broader domains were not tested (Limitations, p. 9).
- Supervisor and worker always share the same backbone, leaving heterogeneous pairings and their quality–cost trade-offs unexplored (Limitations, p. 9).

**Analyst inferences**

- No component ablation directly compares full PILOT with steering disabled, self-evolution disabled, or a matched supervisor–worker architecture lacking those mechanisms. Consequently, aggregate causal attribution to each component remains uncertain.
- Repeated iterations revisit the same 89 Terminal-Bench tasks, and verifier outcomes select which run-generated updates survive. This tests reuse within a recurring benchmark pool, not transfer to held-out tasks (Section 3.1).
- Reporting the best observed pass rate across 20 iterations can overstate typical performance; trajectories and uncertainty intervals are not numerically supplied beyond the plotted curves.
- Each configuration is run twice, but variance, confidence intervals, and significance tests are not reported (protocol, p. 5).
- Manual judgments of whether steering aided success may be subjective. The paper supplies a strict criterion but does not report multiple annotators or agreement statistics (Section 4.2).
- SWE-bench exclusions are large—43 Multilingual and 198 Pro tasks—and the complete lists were only promised for later release in the supplied version (Appendix A.1).
- Skill counts show accumulation, not the quality, redundancy, or individual causal contribution of the stored skills.
- The paper reports generation-token efficiency, but not total compute, latency, or monetary cost of maintaining both supervisor and worker sessions.

## Open research questions

1. **How much does each mechanism contribute independently?** The paper lacks matched steering-only and evolution-only ablations. A factorial experiment could enable or disable live steering and persistent updates while holding the supervisor–worker runtime constant.

2. **Does accumulated knowledge transfer to unseen tasks?** Iterations repeatedly sweep the same benchmark. A next experiment could learn skills on one partition or benchmark and evaluate a frozen evolved harness on held-out tasks without further updates.

3. **What supervisor–worker pairing gives the best quality–cost trade-off?** Only identical backbones are tested. A controlled matrix of smaller and larger supervisor/worker models could measure pass rate, latency, tokens, and compute cost.

4. **Are retained skills genuinely responsible for later successes?** Skill growth correlates with performance and efficiency but is not causally isolated. Skill-level leave-one-out removal, randomized retention, or replay analyses could measure which stored procedures change outcomes.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported design and numerical results because the supplied text includes the complete main paper, tables, figures, protocol, and appendices.

Material missing information includes statistical uncertainty and per-run scores; full iteration-by-iteration numerical results; component ablations; total compute, latency, and monetary cost; annotation agreement for steering audits; detailed skill-quality analysis; held-out transfer evaluation; and the promised complete SWE-bench exclusion lists.
