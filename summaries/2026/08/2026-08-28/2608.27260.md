# What Makes Good Agentic Data? An ACE Lens on Data Generation for LLM Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.27260) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27260)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors argue that useful agentic data should be modeled jointly as environment, task, interaction, and verifier, with validity enforced first and learner-relative complexity and behavioral diversity optimized only within that valid support.

## Problem and motivation

LLM agents need interaction data linking actions to observations and state changes, but such data are expensive to collect, difficult to verify, and harder to scale than conventional instruction–response pairs (Section 1, pp. 3–4). The authors identify two gaps: domain-based surveys obscure mechanisms shared across agent settings, and pipeline descriptions often conflate candidate construction with verification, selection, and learner allocation. This makes results and data-generation strategies difficult to compare.

## Approach and contributions

- **Common data representation:** Agentic data are factorized as \(d=(E,q,\tau,v)\): environment specification, task signal, interaction realization, and optional verifier or reward interface (Sections 2.3–2.4, pp. 5–7).
- **Mechanism-oriented taxonomy:** Pipelines are classified as forward generation or as task-first, trajectory-first, and structure-first reverse generation, according to their anchor and dependency structure (Sections 3.1–3.3, pp. 7–11; Figure 3).
- **ACE framework:** Accuracy is an admission gate; complexity is calibrated to a declared learner and execution configuration; diversity rewards valid, appropriately difficult behavioral coverage while penalizing redundancy (Section 3.4, p. 11).
- **Cross-domain synthesis:** The survey maps verification, difficulty control, and coverage mechanisms across tool use, software engineering, web/GUI, embodied, social, scientific, and formal agents (Sections 4–6).

The supplied evidence supports a conceptual framework, taxonomy, and qualitative synthesis of prior literature. It does **not** establish through the paper’s own experiments that ACE-based generation improves agent performance, nor does it provide a controlled comparison against another taxonomy, a meta-analysis with effect sizes, or a universally validated ACE score.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** ACE is not an exhaustive dataset-governance framework; cost, efficiency, safety, and related concerns remain outside its three central generation-time properties (Section 1, p. 3).
- **Verifier limitations:** Executable checks cover only represented conditions; model judges can be inconsistent, and repeated optimization against a fixed verifier can reward loopholes or reject legitimate alternative solutions (Sections 4.3.3–4.4, pp. 15–16).
- **Calibration cost:** Behavioral difficulty estimates require repeated executable rollouts and depend on the model, scaffold, inference budget, and stochasticity; structural proxies may not predict actual difficulty (Section 5.5, p. 21).
- **Adaptive-loop bias:** Learner-aware generation can overfit current failures, simulator dynamics, or verifier-friendly strategies, causing curriculum drift and reduced coverage (Sections 5.5, 6.5, and 7.4, pp. 21, 29, 31).
- **Analyst inference:** Because this is a survey and framework paper without reported first-party experiments in the supplied text, the practical benefit of its factorization and ACE objective remains empirically unvalidated here.
- **Analyst inference:** Comparisons among cited systems are largely qualitative and heterogeneous; the supplied text does not show a systematic evidence-selection protocol or standardized re-evaluation.
- **Source limitation:** The collector explicitly omitted 3,589 characters from the references, so bibliographic coverage cannot be treated as complete. The source is truncated even though the main body through the conclusion appears present.

## Open research questions

1. **Does ACE-guided selection improve learning under a fixed data budget?** The paper argues for effective support over raw quantity but reports no direct controlled test. A next experiment could hold generation cost and sample count fixed while comparing random, accuracy-only, and full ACE-conditioned selection.

2. **How should behavioral redundancy be represented across domains?** Section 6.1 leaves factor representations, similarity functions, and weights domain-specific. A next experiment could compare action graphs, state-transition signatures, and learned trajectory embeddings by their ability to predict marginal transfer gains.

3. **How robust are learner-relative complexity estimates?** Difficulty depends on the solver, scaffold, tools, and inference budget, and may reflect a single solver artifact. A next experiment could evaluate matched tasks across several configurations and measure whether structural interventions preserve difficulty rankings.

4. **How can self-evolving generators avoid shared verifier–learner failure loops?** Section 7.4 warns that jointly adapting components may reinforce the same errors. A next experiment could compare fixed independent audits, periodically refreshed verifiers, and fully co-adaptive loops on held-out real environments and retention anchors.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The main framework, taxonomy, definitions, and qualitative conclusions are clearly described. Materially missing or unavailable are first-party experimental results, quantitative ACE validation, implementation details for computing a unified ACE objective, a formal literature-search or inclusion protocol, systematic quality assessment of cited studies, and complete untruncated source text.
