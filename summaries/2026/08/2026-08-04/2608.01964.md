# LongHorizon-Harness: Advancing Long-Horizon Agents for Real-World Tasks

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-04.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.01964) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.01964)


## One-sentence takeaway

LongHorizon-Harness improves long-horizon agent reliability by replacing a single growing execution history with explicit task state advanced only through independently audited environment changes.

## Problem and motivation

Long-horizon tasks require agents to preserve goals, intermediate artifacts, constraints, and discovered facts across many dependent actions. The authors identify three recurring failures: compounding errors and goal drift, degradation in effective use of growing contexts, and loss of task state (Section 1).

Their central gap claim is structural: existing harnesses commonly keep execution and state tracking in the same expanding context while allowing the executor to assess its own completion. An erroneous completion claim can therefore become persistent state and misdirect later actions (Section 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Task-state reformulation:** Execution is represented as a sequence of audited state transitions. Requirements, artifacts, and environmental facts are stored outside the executor’s trajectory, with evidence-linked statuses such as pending, completed, blocked, or untrusted (Sections 2.1–2.2).

- **Manage–Execute–Audit loop:** A manager selects one bounded subtask and its acceptance criteria; a fresh-context executor modifies the environment; and a separate, read-only auditor verifies the resulting state. Only audit-supported findings persist across rounds (Sections 2.1–2.4; Figure 2).

- **Backend interoperability:** A lightweight `AgentAdapter` wraps existing agent systems while retaining their native planning and tool-use loops. The paper reports support for interchangeable model and harness backends (Sections 1 and 2.3).

- **Cross-domain evaluation:** The framework is evaluated on hybrid GUI–CLI workflows, desktop workflows, and command-line tasks using Qwen 3.7-Plus and, in a smaller experiment, Claude Opus 4.7 (Sections 3.1–3.2).

The matched WeaveBench and Terminal-Bench comparisons support the claim that explicit state management and auditing can improve a fixed model/backend system. Results across two backbones also suggest—but do not prove broadly—that the effect is not unique to Qwen.

The evidence does not isolate which MEA component causes the gains. OSWorld comparisons also change the interaction mode from single-action GUI to hybrid GUI+CLI, so they do not establish that state management alone explains the improvement. Aggregate gains further coexist with category- and task-level regressions.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope limitation:** Auditing can identify errors and initiate recovery but cannot supply missing primitive capabilities such as visual perception, mathematical reasoning, coding, or algorithm design (Section 3.3).

- **Author-stated verification limitation:** Performance remains weak when correctness depends on hidden thresholds, temporal or spatial precision, ambiguous semantics, or conditions not recoverable from visible environment state (Appendix B.2).

- **Analyst inference — missing component ablations:** The supplied text does not separately ablate explicit state, fresh contexts, independent auditing, bounded contracts, or additional inference. Consequently, their individual causal contributions are unresolved.

- **Analyst inference — confounded OSWorld comparison:** LongHorizon-Harness uses hybrid GUI+CLI tools while the baseline uses single-action GUI operation (Table 2; Appendix A.2). Tool access and harness structure therefore change together.

- **Analyst inference — unequal WeaveBench reference conditions:** The authors’ runs use root privileges, whereas official results use regular-user privileges; the paper appropriately treats official rows as references rather than matched comparisons (Table 1 caption).

- **Analyst inference — cost and budget sensitivity:** The system permits up to 25 MEA rounds with substantial per-role time budgets, and token costs sometimes increase sharply. Performance under equal token, wall-clock, or monetary budgets is not reported (Section 3.1; Figures 4–5).

- **Analyst inference — reporting inconsistency:** The abstract reports the Opus subset as **20.0%→34.3%**, whereas Table 3 and Appendix Table 5 report **20.6%→35.3%**. The supplied text does not reconcile these values.

- **Analyst inference — reproducibility uncertainty:** Versions and inference settings are documented, but the supplied text does not establish public availability of implementation code, prompts, task-state schemas, or audit policies.

## Open research questions

1. **Which MEA component produces the improvement?**  
   The paper evaluates the combined framework without component ablations. A factorial experiment could independently toggle external task state, context resetting, bounded contracts, and auditing under a fixed inference budget.

2. **Do gains remain under equal compute and tool access?**  
   Token use can rise substantially, and OSWorld interaction modes differ. A matched experiment should equalize GUI/CLI permissions, tokens, wall-clock time, and maximum actions across baseline and harness.

3. **How reliable is the auditor itself?**  
   The method depends on audit conclusions, yet auditor false-accept and false-reject rates are not reported. Human-labeled environment transitions could measure verification calibration and compare same-model versus independent-model auditors.

4. **How should the framework handle conditions that are not directly observable?**  
   Hidden thresholds and temporal or spatial requirements remain difficult. A next experiment could add uncertainty-aware audit statuses and targeted evidence acquisition, then measure whether this reduces confidently verified failures without excessive extra rounds.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the described method and reported benchmark values because the supplied text includes detailed methods, tables, appendices, and evaluation settings.

Material missing or unresolved information includes public code and artifact availability, exact prompts and task-state serialization, auditor error rates, equal-compute comparisons, component ablations, statistical uncertainty across most benchmarks, selection criteria for the 34-task Opus subset, monetary and wall-clock costs, and reconciliation of the conflicting Opus subset figures in the abstract versus Tables 3 and 5.
