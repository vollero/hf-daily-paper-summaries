# FM-Bench: A Benchmark for Long-Horizon Management with Competing Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.18423) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18423)


## One-sentence takeaway
FM-Bench evaluates LLM agents over 20 simulated years and finds that sustained managerial behaviors—especially horizon-aware investment, cash deployment, and early contract renewal—track performance better than token use, model scale, or early success.

## Problem and motivation
Existing agent benchmarks typically test either long-horizon execution in largely stationary environments or short-lived competition, leaving unclear whether agents can manage an organization when decisions compound and rivals adapt. The authors define management through four demands: hidden information, cumulative consequences, a counter-adaptive environment, and multi-objective pressure (§1; Table 1).

This matters because short evaluations may conceal delayed failures and misrank agents: on one solo seed, rank correlation with the final order was only 0.19 at year 5, rising to 0.78 at year 15 (§4.2).

## Approach and contributions

1. **A deterministic long-horizon benchmark.** An agent manages a football club for 20 in-game years through 26 tools and roughly 340–400 decision stops. The environment includes hidden player attributes, delayed investments, financial and sporting objectives, adaptive market prices, firing, and insolvency (§3.1; Figure 1; Tables 1, 10–13).

2. **Matched solo and competitive tracks.** The solo track evaluates each of 15 models against fixed scripted opponents over three seeds; the Arena places the same models plus a scripted anchor in one shared, contested economy (§4.1).

3. **Mechanism-computed evaluation.** A deterministic composite score combines cumulative honors, real value added, and final squad value, with penalties for early settlement and excess cash hoarding; it uses neither an LLM judge nor human rating (§3.3).

4. **Behavior-level analysis.** The authors derive six reproducible behavioral measures covering endgame awareness, credit assignment, proactive control, price discovery, memory curation, and compute efficiency (§4.2.1; Appendix J).

The results establish that FM-Bench differentiates models over a long simulated horizon and exposes behavioral patterns associated with higher scores in this environment. They do not establish that those behaviors causally produce higher performance, that close model pairs are reliably ordered, or that rankings transfer to other management domains. Arena conclusions are especially provisional because they come from one shared world.

## Limitations and validity risks
Author-stated limitations include:

- Solo results use only three seeds, which the authors say is insufficient for inferential claims about individual model pairs; adjacent scores may therefore be indistinguishable (Appendix A).
- The Arena uses one seed and has no error bars, so differences of a few points should be treated as ties (Appendix A).
- Solo opponents are scripted rather than adaptive managers; the Arena provides competition but is not claimed to be a realistic model of human rivals (Appendix A).
- Construct validity is limited to one synthetic football-management domain, and ranking transfer to other long-horizon settings is untested (Appendix A).
- Behavioral metrics are correlational over only 15 models and may be confounded; proposed causal tests such as notebook ablation and plan injection were not performed (Appendix A).
- Arena behavioral timing is approximate because stop indices are mapped linearly onto tenure, unlike replay-exact solo timing (Appendix J).

**Analyst inference:** The privileged oracle is a strong reference policy but not an optimal ceiling, so the 90.94-versus-95.54 comparison cannot quantify distance from optimal management (§4.1). Also, the human study’s six first-play participants on one seed are too few to support broad human–model comparisons, consistent with the authors’ own caution in Appendix D.

## Open research questions

1. **Do the identified behaviors causally improve performance?** Correlations may reflect other capabilities. A next experiment could inject matched notebook plans or constrain renewal and investment timing while holding the base model fixed.

2. **How stable is the Arena ranking across worlds?** Only seed 7 was evaluated. Running multiple Arena seeds and aggregating per-world ranks would test whether competition-induced reshuffling is reproducible.

3. **Do FM-Bench rankings transfer beyond football?** Cross-domain validity was not tested. The same models could be evaluated in another deterministic organizational simulation implementing the four stated management demands.

4. **Can human–agent teams outperform either participant alone?** Human reports suggest strengths in rule formation and policy repair that models often lack, but no collaboration trial was run. A controlled study could compare human-only, model-only, and assisted play on matched seeds.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the benchmark design and reported within-benchmark results because the supplied text includes the full methods, tables, appendices, and stated limitations.

Material missing information includes independent replication, multi-seed Arena results, inferential uncertainty for most behavioral correlations, causal ablations of memory or planning, broader participant details for the human study, and evidence of transfer to non-football domains.
