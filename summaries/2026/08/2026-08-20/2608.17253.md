# Co-RL: Unsupervised Reasoning Emerges from Diverse Cohort in Multi-agent RL

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.17253) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17253)


## One-sentence takeaway

CO-RL trains parameter-decoupled language or vision-language agents on unlabeled prompts using peer majority votes as rewards, with heterogeneous cohorts generally improving reasoning and stability over self-rewarding baselines.

## Problem and motivation

Reasoning-focused reinforcement learning commonly depends on ground-truth answers or external verifiers, which are costly and may become unavailable for capabilities that humans cannot reliably assess. Existing self-rewarding methods avoid labels but derive supervision from the same model being optimized, risking correlated mistakes, homogenized rewards, and training collapse. The authors seek a sufficiently independent learning signal that requires neither labels nor an external judge (Sections 1 and 3.2, pp. 1–4).

## Approach and contributions

- **Peer-supervised RL:** Each agent samples \(K\) responses to an unlabeled prompt. A designated peer’s majority-vote answer becomes a binary reward target for the agent’s responses. Agents share neither parameters nor gradients and are updated independently using GRPO; the formulation also permits other sequence-level policy optimizers (Section 4.1, Equations 3–6; Algorithm 1).
- **Cohort diversification:** The framework decorrelates errors through independently updated policies, different model families and sizes, and answer-preserving prompt rewrites (Section 4.2; Appendix C).
- **Theoretical characterization:** Under a binary-answer, infinitesimal-update model with odd \(K\), the analysis predicts that self-rewarding reinforces whichever answer already has probability above one half, whereas two-agent CO-RL converges correctly when the agents’ initial correct-answer probabilities sum to more than one (Propositions 1–2 and Theorem 1, pp. 8–9).
- **Cross-domain evaluation:** Experiments cover text reasoning and multimodal mathematics, including matched-budget comparisons intended to separate cross-agent supervision from extra training or inference compute (Sections 6–7; Appendix D.4).

The evidence supports the authors’ claim that peer supervision can improve several tested LLM and VLM families without ground-truth training rewards, and that cross-family error complementarity is associated with stronger results. It does not establish universal superiority: some individual benchmarks and one small-model multimodal average favor TTRL or GT-Reward. The experiments also do not establish that diversity alone causes every gain, because family, pretraining data, architecture, and capability can vary together.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** The number of agents, cohort diversity, interaction topology, and adaptive assignment of supervision remain future work (Conclusion, pp. 12–13).
- **Theory–experiment mismatch:** The theory reduces answers to correct versus aggregate-incorrect outcomes, assumes independent rollouts, an odd \(K\), symmetric two-agent dynamics, infinitesimal updates, and omits clipping and KL regularization. Experiments use even \(K\) with deterministic tie handling (Section 5, pp. 7–8).
- **Analyst inference — narrow task coverage:** Evaluation is concentrated on mathematical, coding, science QA, and multimodal-math benchmarks. It does not demonstrate effectiveness for open-ended tasks where exact answer extraction or majority voting is unreliable.
- **Analyst inference — comparison sensitivity:** The CoMAS-setting coding evaluation replaces its aggregation step with execution-clustered majority voting because the authors identify a pass@5-like loophole. This may improve validity, but means the evaluation is not identical to the originally reported CoMAS procedure; prior-method numbers are taken from the CoMAS paper (Section 6.2; Appendix D.5).
- **Analyst inference — grader dependence:** VLM evaluation uses rule-based matching followed by Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct to recover false negatives. Although this judge is not used for training and cannot overturn rule-accepted answers, the supplied text reports no judge-agreement study (Appendix D.5).
- **Analyst inference — resource and reproducibility burden:** Runs use eight H100 GPUs, four per agent. Gemma-3 and Qwen2.5-VL additionally require model-specific corrections or library patches, although the authors state that these fixes ship with the code (Section 6.1; Appendix D.5).
- **Analyst inference — prompt-rewrite validation:** Only a sample of 300 rewritten MATH pairs was checked for preserved answers and row alignment, so full-dataset semantic equivalence is not established (Appendix C).

## Open research questions

1. **How should peers be selected or routed dynamically?** Fixed cyclic supervision may waste complementary expertise. A next experiment could compare the ring with confidence-aware routing, random peers, and ensemble-derived targets while holding rollouts and compute fixed.

2. **Which type of diversity actually drives improvement?** Model family changes architecture, tokenizer, pretraining, and post-training simultaneously. A controlled factorial experiment could vary family, size, initialization seed, and prompt form separately and relate each factor’s error overlap to downstream gain.

3. **Does CO-RL work when answers cannot be exactly extracted?** The current binary agreement reward assumes a normalized final answer. A next experiment could test constrained semantic equivalence or process-level peer rewards on open-ended reasoning, with independently audited reward quality.

4. **How robust is the method to systematically weak or adversarial peers?** The theory permits convergence to the incorrect consensus when combined initial competence is insufficient. Experiments could inject peers with controlled error rates and correlated biases, then test peer weighting, abstention, or filtering.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the method description and reported results because the supplied text includes the main paper, proofs, tables, protocols, and engineering notes.

Material missing or unresolved information includes statistical uncertainty across training seeds, significance tests, per-run variance, complete hyperparameters beyond those summarized, full ablations of agent topology and peer assignment, independent verification of the released code, comprehensive validation of all prompt rewrites, and human validation of the VLM grading pipeline.
