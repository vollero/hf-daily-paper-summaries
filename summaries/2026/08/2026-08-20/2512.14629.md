# Evaluating Music Context Preservation: A Multi-facet Framework for Music Editing Systems

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2512.14629) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.14629)


## One-sentence takeaway

MuseCPEval evaluates whether music-editing systems preserve non-target musical context through facet-specific metrics, with controlled edits and a small listening study supporting its diagnostic value but revealing weaker perceptual alignment for melody and motif.

## Problem and motivation

Music editors may successfully change a requested attribute while unintentionally altering harmony, rhythm, structure, or melody. The authors call the retention of attributes not targeted by an edit “Music Context Preservation” (MuseCP). They argue that prior evaluations are missing or facet-limited, preventing systematic diagnosis of editing systems (Introduction; Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Evaluation framework:** MuseCPEval organizes preservation into harmony, rhythm and meter, structure, and melody and motif, measured by 10 tailored metrics (§2; Figure 1). Appendix A additionally proposes two timbre metrics, although the conclusion still describes a four-facet, 10-metric benchmark.
- **Controlled validation:** Eight facet-targeted symbolic edits are applied to 50 selected Lakh MIDI samples, then rendered under a shared synthesis setup to test metric sensitivity and specificity (§3.1; Appendix B; Tables 2 and 6).
- **Perceptual validation:** A pairwise listening study compares metric rankings, intended edit-strength labels, and judgments from 11 retained participants (§3.2; Table 3; Appendix C).
- **Diagnostic case studies:** The framework is applied separately to MusicMagus, ZETA, Audio Prompt Adapter, and Instruct-MusicGen under their respective evaluation protocols (§4; Figure 2).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Controlled edits generally moved the targeted metrics in the expected direction. | Across 50 selected Lakh MIDI samples, +50% tempo scaling produced ΔBPM \(26.10\pm12.96\), BeatF \(0.24\pm0.08\), and IG \(0.19\pm0.11\); a 150 ms beat displacement produced BeatF \(0.03\pm0.07\) but IG \(0.63\pm0.13\). | §3.1; Table 2 |
| Structural metrics are imperfect under nominally structure-preserving transformations. | For +7-semitone transposition, StructPairF was \(0.67\pm0.14\) and ARI \(0.30\pm0.29\), despite expected values near 1; the authors attribute this to segmentation sensitivity to non-structural spectral changes. | §3.1; Table 2 |
| Human and metric agreement varied materially by facet. | Metric–human agreement was 100.0% for rhythm and meter, 65.9% for harmony and structure, and 45.5% for melody and motif; results use 11 retained responses and four questions per facet. | §3.2; Table 3; Appendix C |
| Metric rankings closely matched intended edit-strength labels except for some facets. | Metric–gold agreement was 100.0% for rhythm and meter, 93.2% for harmony, and 72.7% for both structure and melody/motif. | §3.2; Table 3 |
| Timbre metrics responded to instrument replacement, but not monotonically with intuitive instrument identity. | Grand Piano→Electric Piano yielded SKLSim \(0.16\pm0.15\) and MMCos \(0.78\pm0.07\); Grand Piano→Acoustic Guitar yielded \(0.35\pm0.12\) and \(0.90\pm0.04\), over 50 rendered samples. | Appendix A; Table 5 |
| Audio Prompt Adapter retained high-level context but showed substantial tempo deviation. | Under 40 in-domain samples with three instrument edits each, ChromaSim was 0.95, StructPairF 0.88, and ΔBPM \(20.856\pm20.584\). | §§4.1–4.2; Table 4 |

The controlled experiments establish that many metrics react plausibly to known synthetic changes, while the listening study supplies limited evidence of perceptual relevance. They do not establish universal perceptual validity, comparative superiority among the four editing systems, or reliable behavior on unrestricted real-world audio.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Objective validation uses a limited collection of curated edits and cannot by itself guarantee alignment with perception (§3.2).
- **Author-stated:** Structure scores are affected by a segmentation pipeline sensitive to spectral changes beyond structural boundaries (§3.1).
- **Author-stated:** Melody and motif have relatively low metric–human agreement and may be more subjective (§3.2).
- **Author-stated:** The four systems cannot be compared directly because their evaluation setups differ (Figure 2).
- **Analyst inference:** The listening evidence is narrow: only 11 of 33 responses passed sanity checks, with four questions per facet; the text does not report uncertainty intervals or participant-level statistical tests (Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference:** The validation set is deliberately selected for clear melody, meter, and form, then synthesized from MIDI using one soundfont. This improves experimental control but leaves generalization to complex, expressive, or recorded music untested (Appendix B.2).
- **Analyst inference:** The paper presents four facets and 10 metrics as the main framework, while Appendix A adds timbre and two more metrics. The supplied text does not clarify whether timbre is officially part of MuseCPEval.
- **Analyst inference:** No ablation isolates the benefit of combining multiple metrics, and the case-study explanations linking preservation behavior to model architecture are interpretations rather than controlled causal findings (§4.2).

## Open research questions

1. **Do these metrics remain reliable on real recorded music?** Validation is based on selected symbolic MIDI rendered with a shared soundfont. A next experiment could repeat the targeted-edit protocol on multitrack studio recordings with independently annotated facets.

2. **Can melody and motif preservation align better with listeners?** Metric–human agreement is only 45.5% for this facet. A larger listening study could compare the proposed metrics with note-transcription-based or learned perceptual alternatives.

3. **How should facet metrics be calibrated across editing tasks?** Some valid edits, such as instrument removal, may intentionally alter the main melody (§4.2). A next experiment could collect task-specific preservation annotations and test instruction-conditioned metric weighting.

4. **Can editors be compared under a common protocol?** Current case studies use different datasets, tasks, and sample counts. A shared source set and matched instructions could enable controlled system-level comparisons with confidence intervals.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the framework, reported experiments, and qualifications above because the complete retrieved text includes methods, tables, and appendices.
