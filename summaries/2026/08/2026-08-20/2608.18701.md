# SoftVTBench: A Deformation-Aware Visuo-Tactile Dataset and Benchmark for Deformable-Object Manipulation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.18701) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18701)


## One-sentence takeaway

SoftVTBench pairs policy-visible vision and touch with hidden FEM deformation states, revealing that task success can conceal excessive object deformation and that tactile input improves robustness under simulated distribution shifts more consistently than in-distribution performance.

## Problem and motivation

Most robotic-manipulation benchmarks score whether a task finishes, not whether the object was handled acceptably throughout the interaction. For deformable objects, this can equate gentle transport with successful but excessive compression, while vision may lose contact information when the gripper occludes the interface.

The authors identify a data gap: existing resources do not jointly offer complete manipulation tasks, volumetric deformable objects, tactile observations available to the policy, and independent physical states hidden from the policy for deformation-aware scoring (Introduction; Table 1). Such independent ground truth is needed because tactile observations cannot also serve as their own evaluation target.

## Approach and contributions

1. **Dataset:** SoftVTBench contains 4,000 expert demonstrations across 40 simulated pick-and-place tasks and four diagnostic suites. It includes 10 deformable objects, visually matched rigid twins, and more than 50 total assets (Sections 3 and 3.1; Table 2).

2. **Synchronized interfaces:** At 20 Hz, each trajectory records multi-view RGB, dual-finger tactile RGB and marker motion, proprioception, language, and binary and continuous gripper actions. Evaluator-only FEM nodes, object poses, contacts, and drop events are stored separately (Section 3.2).

3. **Deformation-aware evaluation:** The benchmark removes rigid-body motion from FEM node displacement, normalizes displacement by object size, and calibrates a fixed tolerance per object using scripted grasps performed before policy training. DSR credits only episodes that both complete the task and never exceed this tolerance (Sections 3.5 and 4.2).

4. **Controlled diagnostics:** Matched rigid/deformable objects, crossed tactile/control settings, and paired ID/OOD protocols isolate deformability, input modality, gripper granularity, and single-factor shifts (Sections 3.1, 3.3, and 4.3).

5. **Baseline study:** Diffusion Policy, \(\pi_{0.5}\), and FastWAM are evaluated with vision-only and visuo-tactile inputs; a \(\pi_{0.5}\) ablation additionally crosses binary and continuous gripper control (Section 5; Table 5).

These results establish that deformation-aware scoring contributes information beyond terminal success within this simulator and protocol. They also provide evidence that touch is associated with more consistent OOD performance, but do not establish that touch itself caused every difference: several comparisons are small, the five-of-six DSR direction result is not strong under the reported sign test, and some modality variants differ in model size or training batch size.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** All experiments use simulated tactile sensing and FEM dynamics. The paper performs no physical GelSight comparison and makes no sim-to-real claim for its assets or contact conditions (Supplementary Section 8).

- **Author-stated interpretation:** The calibrated tolerance is an operational simulation criterion, not a material-failure or real-world damage threshold (Section 3.5).

- **Author-stated optimization confound:** Diffusion Policy and FastWAM use smaller effective batch sizes and more trainable parameters in their tactile variants. Each configuration has one training run and a final fixed-schedule checkpoint, without a shared hyperparameter sweep; modality and policy-family comparisons can therefore reflect optimization recipes as well as architecture or sensing (Supplementary Section 5; Table 13).

- **Author-stated baseline caveats:** Rigid visuo-tactile rows decode binary execution from continuous checkpoints rather than using native training. Diffusion Policy lacks language conditioning and cannot resolve the instruction-designated identical target in Spatial suites (Section 5.3).

- **Analyst inference:** The task scope is narrow—parallel-jaw, single-object pick-and-place with 10 deformable assets—so validity for other manipulation skills, grippers, materials, or long-horizon contact patterns is untested (Tables 8 and 10).

- **Analyst inference:** OOD evaluation isolates illumination, mass, or stiffness one at a time; robustness to compound shifts is not established (Tables 11 and 14–16).

- **Analyst inference:** The supplied text mentions sensitivity analysis for the calibration percentile but does not provide its numerical results, limiting assessment of how rankings depend on the 90th-percentile choice.

## Open research questions

1. **Can tactile fusion improve compliance without enlarging the TSR–DSR gap?** Touch sometimes raised completion while increasing deformation violations, so the fusion mechanism remains unresolved. A next experiment could train an explicitly deformation-regularized or contact-state-predictive policy and compare TSR, DSR, and \(R_{\max}\) against matched tactile baselines.

2. **Do the benchmark’s conclusions transfer to physical sensors and objects?** The paper explicitly lacks asset-specific sim-to-real tactile validation. A plausible experiment would replay matched grasps on physical soft objects with GelSight sensors and compare tactile fields, deformation traces, and policy rankings.

3. **How robust is DSR to the calibration rule?** The released traces permit alternative thresholds, but numerical sensitivity results are absent from the supplied text. A next study could rescore every policy across several globally fixed calibration percentiles and report ranking stability.

4. **What happens under compound or novel-task shifts?** Evaluation changes only one factor at a time and retains pick-and-place structure. A next experiment could combine illumination, mass, and stiffness shifts or introduce different contact-rich tasks while preserving matched initial conditions.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the dataset design, metric definition, reported evaluation settings, and tabulated results because the supplied text includes the main paper and substantial supplementary material.
