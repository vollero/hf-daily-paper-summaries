# SkillGate: Training In-Policy Skill Selection in Long-Horizon Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.18852) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18852)


## One-sentence takeaway

SkillGate improves long-horizon agents by assigning task outcomes only to execution tokens and separate, action-local credit to skill-name tokens, raising a 9B model’s pooled trial success from 47.0% with outcome-only RL to 53.2% across five benchmarks.

## Problem and motivation

Agents increasingly choose procedural “skills” during an episode from names and short descriptions, but ordinary outcome-rewarded RL broadcasts one trajectory-level advantage across both the few skill-selection tokens and thousands of execution tokens. The authors identify **selector credit starvation**: selection tokens receive little loss weight, and a correct selection can receive negative credit when later execution fails. An audit reports that skill-identity tokens carry a median 0.14% of trajectory loss weight, with dilution and wrong-sign credit worsening as trajectories grow, despite correct reads being associated with substantially higher success (Figure 5; Section 4.6).

The claimed gap is that prior routing or skill-learning methods do not directly train the policy’s own mid-episode, multiway choice of which skill to open using a distinct, length-independent credit channel (Introduction; Section 5).

## Approach and contributions

- **Failure diagnosis:** The paper defines selector credit starvation and audits 12,800 trajectories from an outcome-only RL run, measuring selection-token loss share, wrong-sign credit, and within-group signal quality by trajectory length (Figure 5; Section 4.6).

- **Disjoint credit assignment:** SkillGate removes the entire skill-read call from the task-loss mask, so terminal outcomes update only execution tokens. A separate selector channel updates only the tokens naming the selected skill (Sections 3.1–3.3; Figure 2).

- **Selection utility:** A read receives utility 1 only when it is the trajectory’s sole read and selects the oracle; wrong or multiple reads receive 0. Utilities are centered over read actions in the prompt group, and tied groups provide no selector update (Equation 1).

- **Length-invariant weighting:** Each channel is normalized to the original batch’s total token-weight mass, while each credited read action receives equal total weight regardless of trajectory length or tokenizer segmentation (Equation 2).

- **Empirical contribution:** The method is compared with outcome-only RL, supervised and preference-based selection, credit-placement ablations, external routers, and larger reference models on five agentic benchmarks under a 16-skill mixed slate (Sections 4.1–4.4).

- **Released artifacts:** The supplied text gives links for code and a SkillGate-9B model, although their contents and reproducibility are not independently verified here (page 1).

The controlled results support the claim that token-local selector credit improves both selection behavior and pooled trial success relative to outcome-only RL at the tested scale and protocol. The ablation further supports the importance of placing credit on identity tokens and penalizing multiple reads.

The evidence does not establish universal superiority across agent architectures, slate constructions, or task distributions. Per-benchmark bootstrap intervals cross zero, pass@4 improvement is not statistically established, and the +11.2-point matched-rollout association is not itself causal. Larger frontier systems are explicitly capability references rather than controlled baselines.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Every training configuration is a single run, so seed-to-seed variability is unknown (Section 6; Section 4.1).

- **Author-stated:** Training requires tasks whose correct skill is known, limiting applicability where oracle skill labels cannot be supplied (Section 6).

- **Author-stated:** The action-local selector cannot credit abstention; groups without credited disagreement produce no selector signal (Sections 3.1 and 6).

- **Author-stated:** The controlled trial-success interval excludes zero, but the task-level pass@4 interval does not; per-benchmark intervals are wide and cross zero (Appendix E).

- **Author-stated:** Frontier-model comparisons use adapted native interfaces, omit models unable to sustain tool interaction, and are not controlled comparisons (Appendix B).

- **Analyst inference:** The utility assumes that exactly one oracle read is desirable. This may misfit tasks requiring multiple complementary skills, repeated consultation, or legitimate exploratory reads; such settings were not evaluated.

- **Analyst inference:** Oracle skills were task-specific and verified, while slates always contained exactly one oracle, five misleading skills, and ten bystanders. Performance under imperfect, multiple, or absent oracles is therefore unresolved (Sections 2 and 4.1; Appendix C).

- **Analyst inference:** The method depends on reliable token-span alignment and explicit textual skill paths. Applicability to latent routing, API-managed tool selection, or interfaces without stable identity spans is not demonstrated (Sections 2 and 3.2; Appendix F).

- **Analyst inference:** The paper includes credit-placement ablations, but the supplied text reports no independent ablations over selector coefficient λ, slate size, rollout-group size, or alternative utility calibration.

## Open research questions

1. **Can selector credit handle zero or multiple correct skills?** The current utility assumes one known oracle and cannot reward abstention. A next experiment could construct slates with zero, one, and several valid skills and compare set-valued or counterfactual utilities.

2. **How robust is SkillGate to noisy oracle labels?** Correct skill supervision is assumed and evaluation oracles were manually verified. Injecting controlled label noise or partially valid skills during training would test sensitivity and possible confidence-weighted objectives.

3. **Does the method remain effective across larger slates and longer horizons?** Experiments fix the slate at 16 candidates and cap episodes at 30 turns and 850 seconds. A factorial study over slate size and trajectory budget could measure scaling of success, selection purity, and credit signal.

4. **How much of the gain is stable across random seeds and architectures?** Each configuration is represented by one 9B training run. Replicating the controlled comparison across seeds, model sizes, and tool-call interfaces would test variance and architectural dependence.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the method description and reported results because the supplied text includes the full main paper, appendices, raw main-result counts, protocol details, and uncertainty analysis.

Material missing information includes multi-seed results; sensitivity to λ and other training hyperparameters; evaluation with absent or multiple valid skills; results for slate sizes other than 16; detailed benchmark-by-benchmark task definitions; and independent verification that the linked code and model reproduce the paper’s results.
