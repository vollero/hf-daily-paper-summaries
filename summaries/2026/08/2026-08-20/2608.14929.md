# Training Leaves Traces: Centered Residual Signatures for Language Model Lineage Verification

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.14929) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14929)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors propose a data-free, white-box score based on centered residual branch products that perfectly separates descendants from unrelated checkpoints in two controlled benchmarks and remains invariant to tested neuron-permutation and reciprocal-rescaling laundering.

## Problem and motivation

The paper addresses pairwise weight-lineage verification: determining whether two model checkpoints share a weight ancestor after operations such as fine-tuning, pruning, quantization, or LoRA merging. This matters because open-weight model provenance may be undocumented or obscured, while behavioral similarity alone cannot distinguish inherited weights from independent training or distillation.

The identified gap is that hashes break after weight changes; metadata and logs require trust; watermarks require prior insertion; behavioral methods need data and forward passes; and ordinary weight similarities can fail under function-preserving reparameterization (Introduction; Table 1). The target is therefore retrospective verification from weights alone, without claiming ownership or direction of descent (Sections 2.1 and 5).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Centered residual signatures.** For each residual block, the method composes its linear factors into a branch product \(M\), subtracts the generic identity-aligned component \(\mathrm{tr}(M)I/d\), and normalizes the vectorized traceless remainder into a checkpoint-specific signature (Section 3.3, Eq. 4).

2. **Symmetric lineage scoring.** Cross-checkpoint block signatures are matched with the Hungarian algorithm, then their cosine similarities are averaged into a symmetric model-level score \(L(A,B)\) (Sections 3.3–3.4, Eqs. 5–6).

3. **Empirical-null verification.** A suspect is labeled related when its score exceeds the maximum score against independently trained checkpoints; the authors describe a conformal \(p\)-value whose effective sample size depends on independent roots (Section 3.4, Eq. 7; Appendix B.2).

4. **Algebraic robustness.** Because hidden-unit permutations and reciprocal rescalings cancel within the branch product, the signature is invariant to these tested function-preserving transformations without pairwise parameter realignment (Section 4.2).

5. **Empirical validation.** The paper evaluates controlled MLP and approximately 30M-parameter GPT-2 benchmarks, public LLaMA-2 checkpoints, laundering transformations, partial inheritance, a suppression attack, and projection pairing across several model families (Sections 4.1–4.3; Appendices D–E).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Perfect controlled-benchmark discrimination | Centered signatures achieve AUROC 1.00 and Gap-Z +53.0 on 52 MLP pairs, and AUROC 1.00 and Gap-Z +31.0 on 45 held-out GPT-2 pairs. | Table 3; Section 4.1 |
| Post-training descendants remain separated | On held-out GPT-2 roots, the minimum descendant score is 0.855 at 70% pruning versus a maximum non-descendant score of 0.004; quantized, LoRA-merged, fine-tuned, and pruned descendants are evaluated. | Table 7; Section 4.3 |
| Distillation does not create a weight-lineage signal | Distilled GPT-2 students score about 0.002 despite reaching 79% top-1 teacher agreement, 1.4 points above independent training. | Section 4.3; Figure 6; Appendix D.4 |

| Tested laundering leaves the proposed score intact | The method retains AUROC 1.0 across permutation, mild/strong reciprocal scaling, combined transformation, and combined transformation plus fine-tuning on MLP; it also retains AUROC 1.0 under GPT-2 permutation. | Section 4.2, Table 5 |

These results establish strong discrimination within the tested compatible residual architectures and transformations. They do not establish legal provenance, ownership, descent direction, calibrated real-world false-positive rates, robustness to every laundering attack, or applicability to incompatible architectures. The gradient-coupling explanation is explicitly presented as a plausible mechanism rather than a proven cause (Section 3.2).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Both checkpoints must be available white-box and must use residual architectures with matching depth and hidden dimension. API-only, plain feedforward, recurrent, state-space, and cross-architecture comparisons are unsupported (Limitations).

- **Author-stated interpretation limits:** The score is symmetric and cannot identify the ancestor without metadata. It compares pairs rather than reconstructing family trees, and under partial inheritance it reflects the shared-block fraction without locating the inherited blocks (Limitations).

- **Signal degradation:** The score falls to 0.58 under 85% MLP pruning and 0.336 for CodeLlama after extensive continued pretraining, although both remain above their evaluated nulls (Limitations).

- **Incomplete adversarial coverage:** In one MLP suppression experiment, reaching the null costs 1.5% evaluation-loss degradation and moving reliably below it costs 12%; the authors explicitly say this does not establish robustness across other attacks, architectures, utility measures, or optimization budgets (Appendix D.6, Table 17; Limitations).

- **Rotation vulnerability:** Arbitrary residual-stream orthogonal rotation destroys cross-checkpoint centered-signature similarity. The authors argue learned LayerNorm or RMSNorm parameters prevent this from being function-preserving in common tested architectures, but architectures with unparameterized normalization could remain vulnerable (Limitations).

- **Limited calibration:** The controlled experiments contain few independent roots—eight for GPT-2 overall—and descendants from one root are not exchangeable null samples. Consequently, AUROC supports ranking discrimination but not precise operational false-positive guarantees (Section 3.4; Appendix B.2; Ethical Considerations).

- **Limited ecosystem validation:** The real-world case study covers three LLaMA-2 derivatives and seven independent architectural clones; broader model-family coverage remains untested (Table 4; Limitations).

- **Analyst inference — baseline interpretation:** Several simple weight baselines also reach AUROC 1.0 on clean benchmarks, so the main demonstrated advantage is invariance and margin under the particular laundering transformations, not superior clean-case discrimination (Tables 3 and 5).

- **Analyst inference — reproducibility:** Experimental configurations and hardware are described, but the code and per-pair scores were only promised for release upon acceptance; their present availability is not established by the supplied text (Reproducibility Statement).

## Open research questions

1. **Can lineage be verified across different architectures or dimensions?** The present score rejects mismatched \(L\) or \(d\). A next experiment could train known teacher–descendant pairs linked by architecture surgery or width/depth changes and test learned or invariant cross-architecture signature mappings.

2. **How reliable is null calibration at ecosystem scale?** The paper has few independent roots per architecture. A larger study could collect many independently trained, architecture-matched roots and estimate threshold stability and false-positive rates on held-out families.

3. **Which adaptive laundering attacks defeat the signature without harming utility?** Only selected algebraic transformations and one MLP suppression attack are tested. A next experiment could jointly optimize functional preservation and signature suppression on full-scale transformer checkpoints under multiple compute budgets.

4. **Can the method recover direction and multi-hop genealogy?** The current score is symmetric and pairwise. A next experiment could combine checkpoint timestamps or training metadata with pairwise scores across known model trees and evaluate ancestor direction and family-tree reconstruction.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the paper’s stated method, experimental settings, and reported results because the supplied text includes the main paper, limitations, and appendices.

Material missing or unavailable from the supplied text includes released source code and raw per-pair results; independent replication; operational false-positive rates from large exchangeable null sets; broad public-checkpoint validation outside the reported families; and evidence for robustness against untested adaptive attacks or cross-architecture transformations.
