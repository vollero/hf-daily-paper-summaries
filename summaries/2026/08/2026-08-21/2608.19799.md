# SWE-bench Science: Can Coding Agents Resolve Engineering Tasks in Science?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-21.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.19799) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19799)


## One-sentence takeaway

SWE-bench Science evaluates repository-level repairs across 119 tasks and 20 scientific domains, finding that the best tested configuration reaches only 47.90% Pass@1 and that auxiliary scientific guidance has model-dependent—not uniformly positive—effects.

## Problem and motivation

Scientific software increasingly functions as part of the experimental instrument: defects can compromise both program behavior and the evidence supporting scientific conclusions. The authors argue that existing coding-agent benchmarks emphasize aggregate success and offer limited repository-level coverage across scientific domains or insight into failures involving scientific abstraction, exploration, integration, and generalization (Introduction, pp. 1–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Benchmark:** 119 manually inspected tasks from 98 GitHub repositories across 20 scientific domains, divided into Issue-driven, Expert-exploratory, and Engineering-integration paradigms (Section 3; Figure 2).
- **Evaluation protocol:** Frozen repository snapshots, isolated public and private tests, hidden semantic and boundary-condition validators, and metrics for exact success, repair progress, and regression preservation (Sections 3.1, 4, and 5).
- **Agent evaluation:** Eight model–harness configurations evaluated on the common 119-task set (Section 5; Table 2).
- **Failure analysis:** Four mutually exclusive scientific failure mechanisms, with runtime or evaluation-path failures tracked separately (Section 6.1; Table 3).
- **Scientific-information ablation:** Paired conditions on 91 eligible tasks remove auxiliary scientific guidance while holding required context, repository evidence, execution environment, and validators fixed (Sections 4.2 and 6.2).

The evidence establishes that all tested configurations leave more than half the tasks without exact private-test success and that visible-test performance can diverge sharply from complete repair. It also provides descriptive evidence that scientific guidance interacts differently with the two ablated configurations. As the authors explicitly caution, the ablation does not establish statistical significance or a causal effect, and it does not isolate the model from repository-intrinsic engineering clues (Sections 4.2 and 6.2).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Domain-level samples remain small, reducing the reliability of comparisons between scientific domains (Section 7). Six domains have only one task each (Section 3; Table 4).
- **Author-stated:** Analysis of how agents use scientific knowledge, and how that knowledge can reliably aid completion, is preliminary (Section 7).
- **Author-stated:** Broader model coverage is needed to understand scientific-information effects (Conclusion).
- **Author-stated:** The paired differences are descriptive and do not demonstrate statistical significance or causality (Section 6.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Scientific-information conclusions have narrow external validity because the ablation covers only 91 tasks and two model–harness configurations at reasoning settings different from the main table’s stated configurations (Section 6.2 versus Section 5).
- **Analyst inference:** Because results are reported for model–harness pairs, the supplied experiments do not separate model capability from harness effects (Table 2).
- **Analyst inference:** Manual failure categorization may involve judgment; the supplied text defines the categories but does not report annotator agreement or category-label reliability (Section 6.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Pass@1 is exact success against the benchmark’s private validators, not direct evidence that a patch is correct for every scientifically valid use outside those validators (Sections 3.1 and 5).

## Open research questions

1. **Which forms of scientific guidance help rather than anchor an agent?** The ablation aggregates several information types and yields opposite Pass@1 effects across two configurations. A next experiment could independently vary equations, expert diagnoses, expected invariants, and repair suggestions while holding all executable evidence fixed.

2. **How robust are repairs to broader scientific distribution shifts?** Hidden validators vary parameters, representations, and boundaries, but exact coverage is not summarized across tasks. A next experiment could construct standardized stress-test families for scale, topology, coordinate ordering, and equivalent representations.

3. **Can failure mechanisms be detected during an agent run?** The paper labels failures after unsuccessful attempts but does not test online detection. A next experiment could train or prompt a monitor to identify abstraction, exploration, integration, or generalization risks and measure whether targeted recovery actions improve Pass@1.

4. **How much performance comes from the model versus its harness and budget?** Each main configuration uses a particular harness, and token volume alone does not explain outcomes. A controlled factorial study could run the same models across multiple harnesses with matched tool access, reasoning depth, and token budgets.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the benchmark design and reported aggregate results because the supplied text includes the complete methods, tables, figures, limitations, and task inventory.

Material missing information includes statistical uncertainty or significance tests; repeated-run variance; annotator counts and agreement for failure labels; detailed per-domain agent results; task-level main-evaluation outcomes; contamination-tier results; precise token values for Figure 5; compute/runtime costs; and controlled separation of model, harness, reasoning-depth, and token-budget effects.
