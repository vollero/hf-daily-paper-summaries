# NARU: A Benchmark for NARrative Evolution and Cultural Nuance Understanding in Japanese Extreme Long Video

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-21.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.13210) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13210)


## One-sentence takeaway

NARU evaluates long-video MLLMs on jointly tracking evolving narratives and interpreting implicit Japanese cultural meaning, revealing large model-family gaps and different sensitivities to temporal evidence across the two capabilities.

## Problem and motivation

Existing long-video benchmarks typically test retrieval, temporal grounding, or explicit storyline structure separately from culturally situated reasoning. The authors argue that context-rich media instead requires both maintaining narrative state over hours and interpreting brief interactions whose meaning depends on accumulated social context.

Japanese long-form media provides the paper’s focused setting because the evaluated interactions can involve indirect intent, conversational backchannels, shared atmosphere, and culturally specific conventions. Conventional manual annotation is costly for such videos, while direct automated processing is described as vulnerable to context-window limits, hallucination, and narrative inconsistency (Sections I and III).

## Approach and contributions

- **Problem formulation:** Defines context-rich long-form video by meaningful narrative and social progression, rather than duration alone, and frames understanding as joint narrative-state tracking and culturally situated inference (Section I).
- **Benchmark:** NARU contains 1,481 four-choice questions grounded in 155 Japanese videos lasting 30–240 minutes and totaling 146.8 hours. It covers four narrative and five cultural categories (Sections I, III-A; Table I).
- **Construction pipeline:** Approximately five-minute chunks are converted into structured event records, merged into a global timeline, reorganized into semantic chapters, and enriched with narrative-thread and cultural-cue annotations. An MLLM then generates MCQs and supporting evidence (Sections III-C1–III-C2).
- **Shortcut reduction and validation:** A blind solver, diagnostic agent, and revision agent iteratively target text-only shortcuts. Two native-speaker review stages involving 40 and 28 annotators correct or remove invalid items (Sections III-C2 and III-D; Figure 2).
- **Evaluation:** Eight proprietary and open-source model configurations are compared through full-benchmark MCQ accuracy, a controlled frame-budget sweep, and open-ended atomic-fact recall (Section IV).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Proprietary models substantially outperform the evaluated open-source configurations. | Full 1,481-item MCQ benchmark: Gemini-3-Flash 76.2%, Gemini-3-Pro 70.0%, Gemini-2.5-Flash 51.4%; open-source models range from 29.6% to 39.8%. | Table II; Section IV-B |
| The strongest model remains much weaker on cultural than narrative questions. | Gemini-3-Flash scores 84.2% narrative average versus 68.2% cultural average; C.3 subtext interpretation is 57.4%. | Table II; Section IV-B |
| More sampled frames help Gemini models consistently, but benefit open-source models less uniformly. | On the fixed 500-question subset from 8 to 128 frames: Gemini-3-Pro approximately 64%→71%, Gemini-3-Flash 53%→64%, and Gemini-2.5-Flash 39%→51%; Qwen3VL-8B improves about 31%→38%, while several other open models remain near 30%. | Figure 3; Section IV-C |
| Narrative accuracy is generally more sensitive to additional frames than cultural accuracy. | Across 8→128 frames, narrative changes range from +1.4 to +20.5 percentage points, while cultural changes range from −3.9 to +10.0 points. | Figure 4; Sections IV-C |
| Removing answer choices preserves model ordering but changes the apparent capability profile. | On the 500-question open-ended subset, Gemini models obtain 0.66–0.78 atomic-fact recall versus 0.21–0.56 for open-source models; N.2 becomes the weakest narrative category for seven of eight models. | Table III; Section IV-D |
| Cultural answers receive partial credit more often than narrative answers in the open-ended setting. | Across models, 78.9% of cultural responses recover at least one reference fact, versus 66.3% of narrative responses. | Section IV-D |
| Human review found substantial defects requiring correction during construction. | Initial review: 178/1,500 items flagged invalid, 161 repaired and 17 removed. Post-refinement: 949/1,483 accepted unchanged, 532 revised, and two removed. | Section III-D |
| The automated open-ended judge broadly agrees with a small human check. | Three annotators assessed 50 Gemini-3-Flash responses: 90.0% acceptance across 150 verdicts, 96.0% majority acceptance, and 82.7% mean pairwise agreement. | Section IV-D |

These results establish relative performance for the eight tested configurations under NARU’s Japanese long-video settings. They also show associations between frame density, question format, and measured capability. They do not prove the authors’ proposed causal explanations—such as pretraining knowledge bottlenecking cultural inference or encoder design explaining Gemini’s frame scaling—because those explanations are explicitly presented as interpretations rather than isolated experimental findings.

## Limitations and validity risks

The paper does not provide a dedicated limitations section.

- **Analyst inference — geographic and linguistic scope:** All benchmark content is Japanese-language media, so validity outside Japanese culture or across other high-context communities is not established (Sections I and III-B).
- **Analyst inference — source selection:** Videos originate from a YouTube candidate pool and undergo manual quality screening plus title/description embedding-based diversity selection. This may not represent private interactions, other distribution channels, or the full diversity of Japanese society (Section III-B).
- **Analyst inference — construction-model dependence:** Gemini 2.5 Pro is used throughout annotation, question generation, and shortcut refinement. Although native speakers verify final items, the text reports no comparison against independently constructed questions or another generator (Sections III-C2 and III-D).
- **Analyst inference — extensive post-generation repair:** The large number of human corrections shows that automatic construction alone was insufficient; the paper does not report inter-annotator reliability for benchmark-item validation (Section III-D).
- **Analyst inference — incomplete shortcut guarantees:** Blind-solver refinement targets textual leakage, but open-ended results still indicate that MCQ options provide structural scaffolding. No independent adversarial shortcut audit is reported (Sections III-C2 and IV-D).
- **Analyst inference — metric comparability:** MCQ accuracy and open-ended atomic-fact recall apply different correctness criteria, so cross-format score magnitudes are not directly comparable (Section IV-D).
- **Analyst inference — judge validation breadth:** Automated-judge validation covers only 50 responses from Gemini-3-Flash, leaving reliability across other models, categories, and answer styles uncertain (Section IV-D).
- **Analyst inference — controlled subset uncertainty:** Frame sweeps and open-ended tests use a stratified 500-question subset, but the supplied text does not report uncertainty intervals or repeated subset sampling (Section IV-A).

## Open research questions

1. **Does the benchmark generalize across cultures and languages?** NARU evaluates only Japanese media. A next experiment could reproduce the taxonomy and verification process for several cultural settings, then test whether model rankings and narrative–cultural gaps persist.

2. **Which modalities actually support culturally implicit judgments?** The benchmark includes dialogue, prosody, visuals, and social context, but reports no modality ablation. A next experiment could compare full video against transcript-only, audio-only, visual-only, and temporally shuffled inputs by cultural category.

3. **Can better memory mechanisms close the frame-utilization gap?** Several open-source models gain little from additional frames, but the study does not isolate whether this reflects sampling, encoding, or cross-frame integration. A next experiment could hold the backbone and frame count fixed while comparing uniform sampling, retrieval, hierarchical memory, and event-based compression.

4. **How much performance depends on MCQ scaffolding rather than reconstructed understanding?** N.2 changes from the easiest MCQ narrative task to the weakest open-ended task for seven models. A next experiment could use matched MCQ, ordering, short-answer, and evidence-citation formats with human scoring.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the benchmark design, reported results, and stated evaluation settings because the supplied text includes the complete construction and evaluation sections.

Material missing information includes the exact diversity threshold, full prompt templates and refinement budget, detailed algorithmic pseudocode hosted externally, video genre distribution, annotator agreement statistics for benchmark construction, per-item temporal evidence spans, confidence intervals or significance tests, compute cost, licensing/access conditions, and broader demographic representativeness of the Japanese content and annotators.
