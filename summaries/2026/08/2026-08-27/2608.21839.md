# FIRM-Video: Check Before You Score for Reliable Text-to-Video Reward Modeling

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.21839) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21839)


## One-sentence takeaway

FIRM-Video distills dimension-specific, evidence-verified checklists into an end-to-end video reward model that improves agreement with expert scores and best-of-8 candidate selection across three text-to-video generators.

## Problem and motivation

Text-to-video reward models must assess prompt compliance, physical and temporal coherence, and perceptual quality accurately enough for benchmarking, filtering, candidate selection, and alignment, while remaining efficient at inference. The authors argue that existing holistic judges lack sample-specific, verifiable evaluation contracts, causing incomplete inspection, post-hoc or unfaithful explanations, and duplicated penalties across dimensions (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

Multi-stage reasoning can improve scrutiny but is expensive; direct reward models are efficient but depend on reliable supervision. FIRM-Video addresses this gap by running structured verification offline and distilling its outputs into a single-pass model (Sections 3.2–3.3, pp. 5–7).

## Approach and contributions

- **Check-before-score supervision:** Construct a checklist for each evaluation dimension, verify individual criteria against temporally ordered visual evidence, and aggregate only those verified decisions (Section 3.2; Figure 3).
- **Dimension-specific evaluation:** Instruction Following uses weighted atomic prompt requirements; World Coherence grounds visible entities and actions before generating target-specific checks; Perceptual Quality uses a manually curated defect taxonomy (Sections 3.1–3.2).
- **Efficient distillation:** Structured decisions and predetermined scores are converted into rationales to train 8B vision-language models that evaluate eight sampled frames in one forward pass (Sections 3.3 and 4.1).
- **Training asset:** FIRM-Video-90K contains 88,044 dimension-specific instances from 29,348 videos and 3,012 prompts (Section 3.3; Table 5).
- **Evaluation asset:** FIRM-Video-Bench contains 750 expert annotations across 250 videos, balanced across five-point ratings and disjoint from the training set (Sections 3.4; Table 5).
- **Models and evaluation:** Two models are fully fine-tuned from Qwen3-VL-8B and InternVL3-8B, then tested on point-wise scoring, out-of-domain preference ranking, and best-of-N selection (Sections 4.1–4.5).

The evidence supports stronger agreement with expert five-point ratings than the listed base models, effective candidate ranking, and gains across three generators. It does not establish that the reward improves a generator through training, that explanations are independently faithful, or that performance generalizes beyond the reported benchmarks, frame-sampling protocol, and video distributions. VBench selection is also dimension-aware rather than based on one universal aggregate reward (Section 4.4; Table 6).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** The study evaluates reward signals and best-of-N sampling but does not use FIRM-Video to optimize a text-to-video generator through reinforcement learning or preference optimization (Section 5).
- **Author-stated scope:** Audio-only requirements are excluded, and evaluation is confined to IF, WC, and PQ (Section 3.2; Appendix A.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Training supervision ultimately depends on proprietary multimodal verification, limiting independent reproduction and preventing evaluation of verifier-specific biases from the supplied text (Sections 3.2–3.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Eight uniformly sampled frames may miss brief events, fine temporal order, or transient artifacts, despite those being evaluation targets; no frame-count ablation is reported (Section 4.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Checklist versus holistic supervision is not isolated in a controlled training-data ablation. Table 4 tests aggregation weights, while Table 1 compares trained models and direct pipeline scoring, so the contribution of each pipeline component remains unclear.
- **Analyst inference:** FIRM-Video-Bench has only 250 videos, and the text gives no inter-annotator agreement statistic or number of annotators per sample beyond “multiple” (Section 3.4).
- **Analyst inference:** Best-of-N results rely on a hand-defined mapping from FIRM rewards to individual VBench dimensions, which may not transfer to tasks without such a mapping (Section 4.4; Table 6).

## Open research questions

1. **Does FIRM-Video improve the generator itself?** This remains open because the paper tests evaluation and selection, not generator optimization. A next experiment could train the same T2V model with FIRM-based preference optimization and compare generation quality, reward hacking, and human judgments against an unaligned control.

2. **Which checklist stages produce the gains?** The paper does not independently ablate prompt decomposition, video grounding, criterion verification, or rationale conversion. A factorial ablation holding videos, model initialization, and supervision volume fixed could measure each component’s effect on MAE and ranking accuracy.

3. **How robust is evaluation to temporal sampling?** All trained models use eight uniformly sampled frames, leaving transient-event coverage unresolved. An experiment varying frame count and sampling policy could stratify results by video duration and brief-action frequency.

4. **Are rationales faithful to the model’s actual score computation?** The offline rationales verbalize verified decisions, but the distilled model jointly generates rationale and score without explicit checklists at inference. Intervention tests could remove or alter cited evidence and measure whether both rationale and score change consistently.
