# Rubrics as Visual-Repair Context for Self-Evolving UI-to-Code Generation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.24138) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24138)


## One-sentence takeaway

RubSE improves iterative screenshot-to-HTML/CSS generation by selecting one typed visual-repair rubric per round and retaining rubric history, producing generally more stable and effective refinement than free-form self-evolution across six VLMs and three benchmark settings.

## Problem and motivation

UI-to-code systems can render a draft and compare it with a target screenshot, but naïve generate–critique–revise loops are unstable. The authors attribute this to **visual repair coupling**: a localized code change can propagate through CSS layout, styling, and component dependencies, fixing one region while damaging others (Sections 1 and 3.2; Figure 1).

Existing work largely emphasizes training-time improvements, while test-time feedback remains comparatively underexplored. The identified gap is therefore a feedback representation that specifies both what to repair and, implicitly, what should remain undisturbed, instead of inviting broad rewrites through free-form critiques (Section 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Failure-mode formulation:** The paper identifies and formalizes visual repair coupling as the non-local relationship between code differences and rendered-pixel differences (Section 3.2).

- **RubSE framework:** Each refinement round uses a rubric represented by a title, description, and one of five visual types: layout/geometry, spacing/density, typography/text, styling/visual, or completeness (Sections 3.3 and 5).

- **Three-stage repair context:** `EVOLVE` generates 3–8 typed candidates, `SELECT` chooses one prioritized repair, and `HISTORY` records selected rubrics as an avoid list for later rounds. The chosen rubric conditions the next HTML/CSS revision (Section 3.3; Figure 3; Tables 14–16).

- **Empirical analysis:** The study compares RubSE with direct generation and naïve self-evolution across six VLMs and three benchmark settings, including ablations, collapse/recovery analysis, human comparison, judge-variability checks, and transfer of GPT-5.4-generated rubrics to Qwen code improvers (Sections 4–5 and Appendix B).

| Best-round performance also generally improves. | RubSE beats naïve self-evolution on both metrics in **14/18 settings**, averaging **+1.13 overall points** according to the paper’s summary. Best rounds are selected by overall score, with the aspect score taken from that same round. | Section 1; Section 4.2; Table 1 |
| Benefits are stronger for frontier executors. | At round 10, the frontier-model average advantage is **+1.90 overall** and **+0.16 aspect**, versus **+0.50 overall** and **+0.06 aspect** over the nine Qwen settings. | Section 4.2; Table 1 |

These results support the authors’ claim that structured, scoped feedback often improves final fidelity, trajectory ceilings, and recovery—especially with stronger executors. They do **not** establish monotonic improvement, universal collapse reduction, or consistent superiority for every model: Qwen3-VL-32B has multiple settings where naïve refinement achieves a higher overall score (Table 1), and RubSE increases average collapse among the Qwen executors (Table 2). Best-round results are oracle references rather than deployable checkpoint-selection results.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:**

- Evaluation covers only three frontier and three Qwen-family models, with HTML/CSS webpage generation as the sole output language (Limitations, page 9).
- RubSE does not guarantee syntactic or runtime correctness; refinement can cause rendering failures, and no explicit debugging loop is included (Limitations, page 9).
- VLM judgments can be unstable or disagree with humans on subtle late-round differences. The human study covers only 60 UI2Code-Real examples and two models (Limitations; Appendix B.1).
- Rubric effectiveness is model-dependent: Qwen3-VL-32B overproduces spacing rubrics and underweights completeness, and selection does not fully correct that imbalance (Section 4.2; Appendix B.4).

**Analyst inferences supported by the text:**

- The primary judge is GPT-5.2, which is also one evaluated generator; the supplied text does not report evaluation using a distinct non-GPT judge. Additional GPT-5.2 runs assess variability but not cross-judge robustness (Section 4.1; Appendix B.2).
- Human labels were produced by two authors and reconciled through discussion; independent or blinded external annotators are not reported (Appendix B.1).
- The ablation is narrow—UI2Code-Real with GPT-5.2—and Figure 4’s extracted text gives qualitative trajectories rather than exact ablation scores (Section 5.1).
- The evaluation uses fixed 1920×1080 pages and forbids external assets, limiting evidence about responsive interfaces or production webpages with dependencies (Tables 13 and 16).
- Cost measurements use only 50 UI2Code-Real samples and specific serving configurations, so they may not generalize to other workloads or infrastructure (Appendix B.3).

## Open research questions

1. **Can rubric guidance reduce collapse for weaker executors rather than merely improve recovery?** RubSE raises average Qwen collapse from 14.8% to 18.1%. A next experiment could compare executor-aware rubric taxonomies, calibrated selection, and GPT-generated rubrics across all three benchmarks.

2. **Can a system select its best checkpoint without oracle access to judge scores?** The reported best-round results select retrospectively using the overall score. A plausible experiment would train or prompt a stopping policy from trajectory history and compare it with fixed-round and oracle selection.

3. **How robust is RubSE under independent evaluation?** Automated scoring uses GPT-5.2, while the human study is author-conducted and narrow. A preregistered study with blinded external raters and multiple unrelated visual graders could test whether rankings persist.

4. **Can correctness constraints complement visual rubrics?** The authors acknowledge syntax and runtime failures. A factorial experiment could compare RubSE with and without validation, browser-error feedback, and rollback after failed renders.
