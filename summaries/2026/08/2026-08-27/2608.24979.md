# FrontierChallenge: Evaluating Scientific Workflow Completion

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.24979) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24979)


## One-sentence takeaway

FrontierChallenge shows that frontier agents often make substantial partial progress yet rarely complete all required artifacts in fixed, cross-domain scientific workflows, with the best configurations passing only 20.6% of 97 released tasks.

## Problem and motivation

Scientific agents must increasingly execute multi-stage analyses and deliver mutually consistent code, tables, figures, simulation products, and reports—not merely plausible answers. Existing benchmarks often evaluate a final answer, interaction trace, single program, or one scientific discipline, leaving end-to-end workflow completion undermeasured (Introduction, pp. 1–2; Related Work, pp. 2–3).

The authors target the narrower problem of executing a fully specified workflow with fixed objectives, inputs, tools, and output requirements. They explicitly exclude autonomous agenda-setting and problem formulation (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Cross-domain benchmark:** A curated pool of 300 scientific workflows across six reporting domains and 21 workflow families. The paper evaluates and releases 97 non-GPU-evaluated tasks while retaining 203 internally (Sections 3.1–3.2; Figure 1).
- **Artifact-contract task design:** Each package includes fixed inputs, available tools, required deliverables, a stepwise rubric, and an executable task-specific Grader. Success depends on the complete, mutually consistent artifact bundle (Section 3.1).
- **Strict completion evaluation:** Pass Rate counts tasks scoring at least 99.9/100; Avg. Score measures partial completion. The threshold accommodates variation from averaging three Judge passes rather than relaxing rubric requirements (Section 4.2, Equations 1–2).
- **Frontier-system study:** Twelve models were evaluated through three agent scaffolds on the same 97 task objectives and visible inputs (Section 4.1).
- **Failure analysis:** The authors examine contract-breach signatures, completion claims, and tool errors, with trajectory-level analysis restricted to 970 Claude Code runs (Section 4.6).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strict completion remained low. | Across 13 evaluated model–scaffold configurations, Pass Rate ranged from 3.1% to 20.6%, while Avg. Score ranged from 67.5 to 87.9 on 97 tasks. | Section 4.3; Figure 2; Table 2 |
| Two configurations tied for the highest completion rate. | GPT-5.6 Sol with Codex and Grok 4.6 with Claude Code each passed 20 of 97 tasks, or 20.6%; their Avg. Scores were 87.9 and 86.6, respectively. | Abstract; Sections 1 and 4.3 |
| Difficulty strongly separated performance. | GPT-5.6 Sol passed 39.1% of Medium versus 14.9% of Hard tasks; Grok 4.6 passed 43.5% versus 13.5%. The release contains 23 Medium and 74 Hard tasks. | Section 3.2; Table 2 |
| Domain completion varied sharply. | Highest Pass Rates were 60% in quantum chemistry, 38% in molecular dynamics, 9% in materials characterization, 4% in analytical chemistry, 20% in life science, and 0% in electrochemistry/environment. | Section 4.4; Figure 3 |
| High partial scores could coexist with no complete task. | Electrochemistry/environment reached an Avg. Score of 94.9, yet every configuration had 0% Pass Rate; analytical chemistry reached 87.6 with a maximum Pass Rate of 4%. | Section 4.4; Figure 3 |
| Completion claims were unreliable. | Of 849 non-passing Claude Code runs, 641 (75.5%) used lexical completion language; only 13 (1.5%) explicitly said work remained underway. | Section 4.6; Figure 5b |
| Raw tool errors alone did not distinguish success. | At least one tool error appeared in 80.7% of non-passing and 94.2% of passing Claude Code runs; median rates were 4.4 and 5.6 errors per 100 tool results. | Section 4.6; Figure 5c |
| Resource use varied substantially. | Recorded mean runtime ranged from 21.8 to 112.8 minutes per task; reported input tokens among configurations with records ranged from 2.183 million to 13.730 million per task. | Section 4.5; Figure 4 |

The evidence supports the authors’ claim that partial rubric completion, self-reported completion, and the mere occurrence of tool errors are inadequate proxies for contract-level delivery on this released suite. It does not establish intrinsic rankings of scientific disciplines, causal effects of particular scaffolds or models, hardware-normalized efficiency, or generalization to the 203 held-out tasks.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Results cover only the 97 released tasks, evaluated configurations, and single runs; trajectory analysis is limited to Claude Code (Conclusion, pp. 9–10).
- **Author-stated:** The six domains are descriptive slices rather than probability samples, are not balanced, and cannot support claims about intrinsic disciplinary difficulty (Section 3.2).
- **Author-stated:** The 97 tasks were sampled from workflows whose official evaluation does not require GPUs; the 203-task held-out set includes GPU-dependent workflows and was not evaluated in the paper (Section 3.2).
- **Author-stated:** Avg. Score aggregates heterogeneous task-specific rubrics and is not a calibrated cross-task scale (Section 4.2).
- **Author-stated:** Provider-specific tokenization, caching, and reporting prevent hardware-normalized efficiency comparisons (Section 4.5).
- **Author-stated:** Contract-breach signatures overlap and are descriptive rather than unique causal diagnoses (Section 4.6).
- **Analyst inference:** Scaffold effects remain confounded with model choice because most models use only one scaffold; the authors explicitly caution that the two Apodex configurations do not isolate a pure scaffold effect (Section 4.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Judge-based semantic criteria may introduce evaluator dependence. The text identifies GPT-5.6 Sol as the Judge and three repeated assessments, but supplies no independent human-agreement analysis (Section 4.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Public reproducibility cannot be assessed for the held-out collection because its tasks and reported results are unavailable in the supplied text.

## Open research questions

1. **Can explicit contract tracking improve full completion without merely raising partial scores?** The paper recommends contract tracking but does not test it. A next experiment could add a deliverable checklist and pre-submission verification stage, then compare Pass Rate and omission types against the existing scaffolds.

2. **Which scaffold features causally improve scientific workflow completion?** Model and scaffold are largely confounded. A factorial experiment could run the same models across multiple scaffolds under matched budgets and environments.

3. **How stable are results across repeated runs?** The study reports single runs, leaving variance unknown. Repeating each model–task configuration with controlled stochastic settings could estimate confidence intervals for Pass Rate and failure categories.

4. **Do the findings generalize to GPU-dependent and held-out workflows?** The evaluated release excludes tasks requiring GPU-based official evaluation. Testing preregistered configurations on the 203-task held-out set would measure generalization across task and resource regimes.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the benchmark design and reported results because the supplied text includes methods, metric definitions, tables, and domain-level findings.

Material missing information includes per-task scores and rubrics, full grader and Judge-validation details, human–grader agreement, repeated-run variance, statistical uncertainty, exact sampling procedure beyond random selection from the non-GPU subset, model configuration and budget details, and all outcomes on the 203 held-out tasks.
