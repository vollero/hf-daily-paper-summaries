# Skill Issue: Are Skills Language-Invariant in LLMs?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.25832) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25832)


## One-sentence takeaway

Controlled multilingual self-play shows that identical LLMs can realize substantially different reasoning and decision-making abilities through different language interfaces, with some—but not all—performance recovered by changing only the intermediate reasoning language.

## Problem and motivation

The paper asks whether multilingual LLMs merely access knowledge unevenly across languages or also express different skills—such as planning, spatial reasoning, and strategic decision-making—depending on the interaction language. Existing multilingual benchmarks largely use fixed questions and answers, so they do not isolate language-conditioned policies in evolving environments. This matters because average multilingual competence may conceal unreliable or inequitable behavior during real interactions (Introduction; Secs. 6.1 and 7).

## Approach and contributions

- **Controlled multilingual self-play:** Two instances of the same model compete while receiving equivalent interfaces in different languages. Model, rules, states, actions, and opponent are fixed, isolating language-interface effects more directly than comparisons across different tasks or models (Secs. 1 and 3).
- **Multilingual TextArena:** The authors extend 65 games to 193 languages using tiered translation and verification. The experiments use native-speaker-verified versions of six games in eight languages; code and data are reported as public (Secs. 1–2; App. E).
- **Large-scale evaluation:** Gemma-4-E4B-it, Qwen3-4B, and Ministral3-3B-Instruct-2512 play 518,400 games spanning spatial reasoning, optimal-strategy execution, resource allocation, hidden information, and repeated interaction (Secs. 3–4; App. B).
- **Process-stage intervention:** Interface and intermediate reasoning languages are separated to test whether stronger-language reasoning can recover performance without translating observations (Sec. 5.3).
- **Behavioral diagnosis:** The study analyzes spatial defeat types, card-conditioned poker behavior, invalid actions, and retrieval versus execution of Nim’s optimal strategy (Sec. 5.2; App. G).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Language-conditioned playing strength is systematic. | Across all six games, English has the strongest mean margin for Gemma (+0.10), Ministral (+0.19), and Qwen (+0.20); Hebrew is weakest or near-weakest at −0.07, −0.12, and −0.23, respectively. | Fig. 2 |
| Sensitivity depends strongly on game and model. | Average max–min language gap is 1.07 for Colonel Blotto versus 0.13 for Kuhn Poker; model-wide game averages are 0.28 for Gemma, 0.54 for Qwen, and 0.56 for Ministral. | Table 2 |
| Spatial failure patterns vary by interface language. | In Gemma TicTacToe losses, English is split 28.5% rows/34.8% columns/36.7% diagonals, whereas Arabic is 20.3%/34.4%/45.3%. In SimpleTak, Gemma’s column-loss share is 46.8% in English versus 57.2% in Arabic and Hebrew. | Tables G.1–G.2 |
| Poker strategies and execution errors shift across languages. | Qwen’s weakest-card bluff rate ranges from 22.5% in Spanish to 47.4% in Arabic; Ministral’s invalid-action rate ranges from 8.8% in Chinese to 20.9% in Hebrew. | Table G.3 |
| Optimal-strategy execution is not language-invariant. | Qwen’s optimal first Nim move occurs in 80.8% of English cases but 24.6% of French, 10.5% of Arabic, and 4.0% of Hebrew cases. Gemma remains between 99.2% and 99.9% across languages. | Table G.4; Sec. G.3 |
| Stronger-language reasoning can recover much of a weak interface’s loss. | For Gemma TicTacToe, German-interface performance rises from −0.22 with German reasoning to +0.20 with English reasoning, reported as 89.4% recovery toward the +0.25 English-interface ceiling. SimpleTak improves from −0.21 to +0.05, a 60.5% recovery. | Table 3; Secs. 5.3–5.4 |
| Static multilingual scores explain part, but not all, of interactive strength. | Across eight languages, mean game margin correlates with 5-shot Global MMLU at \(r=0.73\)–0.92 and Belebele at \(r=0.71\)–0.79, depending on model. | Fig. 4a; Sec. 5.4 |
| Web-text availability is associated with language strength but is insufficient as an explanation. | Per-model correlations with the FineWeb/FineWeb-2 proxy are \(r=0.68\), 0.84, and 0.86; Chinese and Malay are highlighted as model- or script-dependent deviations. | Fig. 4b; Sec. 5.4 |

The evidence establishes within-model associations and performance differences under a controlled suite of translated game interfaces. It does not establish that language alone explains every difference: translation quality, tokenization, script, training composition, and model-specific factors remain possible mechanisms. The reasoning-language intervention supports a reasoning-stage contribution in selected Gemma games, but its limited and non-monotonic Kuhn Poker recovery argues against a single universal cause.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Only 3B–4B models are evaluated, so the findings should not be treated as scale-invariant (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** Training-language distributions are unavailable; FineWeb/FineWeb-2 counts are only proxies for actual exposure (Sec. 5.4; Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** The main experiments cover only eight languages and six games, despite the released resource being larger (Secs. 1–3).
- **Author-stated:** Lower-tier localizations rely on automated verification and may contain residual errors; these languages are not part of the main evaluation (App. E).
- **Analyst inference:** Language-independent boards, numbers, card labels, coordinates, and action syntax reduce linguistic variation but also limit generalization to tasks whose state and action spaces are fully linguistic (Sec. 3).
- **Analyst inference:** The reasoning-language recovery experiment is reported only for Gemma on Kuhn Poker, SimpleTak, and TicTacToe, leaving its cross-model and cross-task robustness untested (Table 3).
- **Analyst inference:** Pearson correlations use only eight languages and are observational; they cannot identify whether benchmark competence, data availability, script, or another factor causes game performance (Sec. 5.4; Fig. 4).
- **Analyst inference:** Table G.4’s “strategy mentions” counts are much larger than the reported game totals, but the supplied text does not define the counting unit precisely enough to interpret their magnitude confidently.

## Open research questions

1. **Do larger models retain the same language hierarchy?** The study evaluates only 3B–4B models. A next experiment could repeat the identical pairwise protocol across parameter scales within one model family.
2. **Which decision stage causes each language gap?** Reasoning-language changes recover performance in some games but not Kuhn Poker consistently. A factorial experiment could independently translate observations, reasoning, memory summaries, and final-action generation.
3. **How much variation is caused by script versus training exposure?** Web-text correlations and Malay/Chinese deviations leave these factors entangled. Controlled continued pretraining with matched token counts across scripts could test their separate effects.
4. **Do the findings transfer beyond abstract games?** Preserved numeric and symbolic state representations may simplify cross-language equivalence. A next experiment could use multilingual conversational or tool-use environments whose observations and actions are predominantly natural language.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported experimental setup and numerical results because the supplied text includes the main paper, methods, tables, and relevant appendices.

Material missing or unclear: exact confidence intervals for pairwise game margins; significance tests for most language gaps and interventions; full details of the reasoning-language experimental protocol and sample sizes; the precise counting definition for Nim strategy mentions; per-run random seeds; complete per-game results underlying Fig. F.1 beyond displayed rounded values; and evaluations of larger or closed-weight models.
