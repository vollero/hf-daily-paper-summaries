# Real-TurnTurk: A Multimodal Turkish Corpus for Turn-Taking Prediction

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.22071) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22071)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors introduce a 4.23-hour multimodal Turkish conversation corpus and an interpretable genetic-algorithm rule that predicts turn changes with 57.0% mean F1, though speaker overlap and limited baselines constrain the generalization claim.

## Problem and motivation

Synchronous dialogue systems often infer turn completion from fixed silence thresholds, which can cause premature interruptions or excessive latency because conversational pauses vary across speakers and contexts. The authors identify a specific resource gap: a lack of naturalistic Turkish corpora with primary turn-taking annotations, synchronized modalities, and speaker-attributed overlapping speech (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Corpus:** 11 unscripted dyadic conversations containing synchronized video, mostly separated per-speaker audio, and time-aligned Turkish transcripts. The corpus totals 253.6 minutes, 5,383 speech segments, and 35,728 words (Section II, Table I).
- **Turn labels:** A semi-automatic procedure identifies speaker transitions and filters short, filler-like backchannels, producing 1,750 positive events and 3,500 sampled negatives (Section II-A; Fig. 1).
- **Multimodal representation:** Each example uses 28 outgoing-speaker features—9 visual, 12 acoustic, and 7 linguistic—extracted from the two seconds before the candidate event (Section III; Table III).
- **Interpretable prediction:** A genetic algorithm searches rules containing 3–7 threshold conditions connected through a hybrid AND–OR representation, optimizing training-partition F1 (Section IV-B; Table IV).
- **Conversation-level evaluation:** Five-fold cross-validation keeps each conversation wholly within either training or test, although recurring speakers remain shared across partitions (Section IV-C).

The evidence establishes that the evolved rule outperforms simple random, always-positive, and silence-threshold references on these conversation-level folds. It does not establish statistical significance, superiority to modern learned classifiers, or generalization to unseen speakers; the authors explicitly characterize the 7-point margin as modest and make no significance claim (Section V).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Speaker-disjoint five-fold evaluation is impossible under the recording design. Two recurring interlocutors occur in every conversation, so reported results may be an upper bound for unseen-speaker generalization (Sections II and IV-C).
- **Author-stated:** The feature vector excludes listener video cues such as gaze and nodding (Section III-D).
- **Author-stated:** Only a dataset subset is reported as publicly available, although the text does not specify its exact coverage (Section II).
- **Author-stated:** The comparison omits Random Forest, XGBoost, transformer models, and other black-box classifiers; these are deferred to future work (Conclusion).
- **Author-stated:** Acoustic features are not speaker-normalized, and the recording design is imbalanced around two hub speakers (Conclusion; Table II).
- **Author-stated:** The authors make no statistical-significance claim because the corpus has only 11 conversations (Section V).
- **Analyst inference:** Label validity depends on fixed duration, filler, and text-length heuristics. The paper explains how incoming-segment labeling is separated from outgoing-window features, but supplies no manual annotation agreement or validation study for those heuristics (Section II-A).
- **Analyst inference:** The final rule is retrained on all data and discussed qualitatively, so Eq. 2 should not itself be read as independently tested performance evidence (Section V).

## Open research questions

1. **How well does the method generalize to entirely unseen speakers?** Speaker identity crosses the current folds. A next experiment could collect a non-hub corpus and run speaker-disjoint or leave-one-speaker-out evaluation.

2. **Do listener-side cues improve prediction?** Listener gaze and nodding are excluded despite being identified as relevant cues. An ablation could compare outgoing-speaker-only features with two-participant visual and acoustic features.

3. **How competitive are interpretable GA rules against learned models?** Evaluation includes only random, always-positive, and silence baselines. A controlled comparison with tree ensembles and multimodal sequence models could use identical folds and the same ±0.5-second tolerance.

4. **How robust are the turn labels?** No human agreement or sensitivity analysis is reported for the duration, filler, and text-length filters. Independent manual annotation followed by threshold-sensitivity experiments could quantify label reliability.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported corpus composition, method, evaluation protocol, and numerical results because the supplied text includes the complete methods, tables, and conclusions.

Material missing information includes participant demographics and recruitment, conversation topics or prompts, recording environment, consent and privacy procedures, exact contents and license of the released subset, fold assignments, per-fold variance, statistical uncertainty, human validation of turn labels, feature-extraction failure rates, computational cost, and results from speaker-disjoint or stronger learned baselines.
