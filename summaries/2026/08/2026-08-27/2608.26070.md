# Prefix Sliding for efficient test-time scaling

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.26070) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26070)


## One-sentence takeaway

Prefix Sliding retains a model’s instruction prefix and only a recent reasoning window, bounding per-token attention cost while matching or approaching full-attention reasoning accuracy at substantially higher generation speeds, though tasks needing older intermediate state require larger windows.

## Problem and motivation

Sequential test-time scaling lets language models reason longer, but full attention retains the entire reasoning trace: memory grows with sequence length, and every new token becomes more expensive. This makes very long reasoning traces prohibitively costly (Introduction, pp. 2–3).

The authors observe that attention concentrates on the initial prompt and recent tokens, while most intermediate reasoning tokens receive little attention (Figure 2, p. 2). They identify a gap between full attention, which is unbounded in per-token cost, and bounded alternatives such as ordinary sliding windows or periodic summarization, which may discard instructions, reprocess tokens, or add latency (Sections 5–6, pp. 6–8).

## Approach and contributions

- **Prefix Sliding:** Preserve a fixed prefix containing instructions and the task prompt, plus a fixed-size window over the latest reasoning tokens; evict older intermediate tokens. After warm-up, memory and attention cost per new token are constant with respect to total generated length (Section 2 and Figure 3, pp. 2–3).

- **Training-free deployment:** Apply the attention pattern directly to pretrained generative language models. The implementation uses continued positional indices and custom FlashAttention kernels that mask partially overlapping tiles and skip irrelevant tiles (Sections 2–3, pp. 3–4).

- **Long-trace RL training:** Combine Prefix Sliding with chunked or truncated backpropagation. In the reported training experiments, loss is computed only over the final window while additional preceding tokens provide context for more accurate gradients (Section 2, pp. 3–4).

- **Empirical comparison:** Evaluate against full attention, ordinary sliding-window attention, periodic summarization, and “last-k” context restarts. The authors also ablate window size, positional-embedding handling, and the amount of context supplied during truncated backpropagation (Figures 8–9; Appendices C–G).

- **Released implementation:** The paper states that its Prefix Sliding code is public (Abstract and Reproducibility Statement, pp. 1 and 10).

These results establish a strong empirical speed–accuracy trade-off for the tested Qwen-family models, reasoning benchmarks, kernels, and hardware. They also show that the best window is task-dependent and that truncated backpropagation can work in a limited validation.

They do not establish universal equivalence to full attention, reliable improvement for short outputs, or scalability beyond 7B models and hundreds of thousands of reasoning tokens. Figure 7 supports the direction of the RL claim, but the supplied text does not report exact reward values.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations**

- Comparisons are restricted to methods that both work with existing pretrained transformers and have bounded per-token cost. Alternative architectures and many subquadratic or mixed-attention methods are excluded (“Limited comparisons,” p. 8).
- Intermediate-token eviction can lose necessary state. LiveCodeBench requires at least a 16,384-token window to match full attention because code written earlier may leave the window (Figure 11, p. 9).
- Speed gains are small for short generations that barely exceed the window’s warm-up period (Figure 12, p. 9).
- Large tool outputs, websites, or files can flood the recent window, while handling later user instructions in multi-turn interaction remains unresolved (“System outputs and multi-turn,” p. 9).
- Extremely long prefixes remain expensive because Prefix Sliding does not reduce prompt prefill or prefix KV-cache cost (Appendix A, p. 21).
- Experiments reach only hundreds of thousands of reasoning tokens and models up to 7B parameters (“Scale,” p. 9).
- The authors caution that the 7B truncated-backpropagation validation is short and that larger, longer runs may require chunked backpropagation (Appendix E, p. 22).

**Analyst inferences**

- The claim that discarded tokens are generally unimportant is task-sensitive: attention magnitude is suggestive but does not by itself prove causal dispensability, and the coding result demonstrates a counterexample.
- Some training claims have limited quantitative support in the supplied text: Figure 7 lacks exact reward values, and the 7B comparison is described as comparable without a numerical summary table.
- Hardware generality is unclear because the custom inference kernels target Nvidia Hopper and the headline speed evaluation uses one 80GB H100 (Sections 3–4, pp. 4–5).
- The summary comparison is sensitive to prompting and bookkeeping. The authors note that summary prompting changes results and that summary retains slightly more tokens, potentially giving it an advantage (Appendix G.2, pp. 23–24).

## Open research questions

1. **Can models learn to preserve or externalize intermediate state with smaller windows?** LiveCodeBench needs a large window because earlier code can be evicted. A next experiment could train Prefix Sliding on coding tasks while allowing selected tokens to be promoted into the prefix, then compare pass@1 and memory use across window sizes.

2. **How does Prefix Sliding scale beyond 7B models and 262K-token traces?** The authors explicitly limit their evidence to models up to 7B and hundreds of thousands of tokens. A controlled study could test several model sizes and million-token rollouts while reporting accuracy, wall-clock time, memory, and training stability.

3. **What is the best policy for multi-turn and tool-output contexts?** The paper leaves unresolved whether later instructions belong in the prefix and how to prevent large outputs from flooding the window. An agent benchmark could compare fixed prefixes, dynamically promoted instructions, output caps, and external file access.

4. **When is truncated backpropagation insufficient?** Its validation is short, and the authors suggest chunked backpropagation may be necessary for longer chains. A direct comparison across rollout lengths could measure reward, gradient similarity, KL mismatch, memory, and runtime for full, truncated, and chunked backpropagation.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the method, experimental setup, tabulated inference results, and stated limitations; **medium** for the strength of the RL conclusions because several results are presented only graphically or qualitatively.

Material missing information includes exact numerical values for Figures 1, 6–9, 12, and 15; complete RL hyperparameters and final rewards; uncertainty intervals for most reported benchmark scores; performance on broader model families and hardware; large-scale chunked-backpropagation results; and evaluations of realistic multi-turn or tool-using agents.
