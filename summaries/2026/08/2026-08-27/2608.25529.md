# Video-IFBench: Evaluating Instruction Following of Multimodal LLMs in Video Understanding Scenarios

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.25529) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25529)


## One-sentence takeaway

Video-IFBench shows that current multimodal models frequently fail to satisfy constraint-rich and conditional video instructions, with the best evaluated model reaching 76.5 TCSR but only 54.5 TISR.

## Problem and motivation

Video benchmarks usually measure whether an answer is correct, not whether it obeys user-specified semantic, formatting, and conditional requirements. The authors argue that real applications require both: a model may need to select a branch based on visual or audio evidence, answer multiple tasks, restrict evidence to particular moments, and follow an output schema. Prior instruction-following benchmarks primarily cover text or narrower multimodal settings, leaving diverse video-grounded instruction following under-evaluated (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Benchmark:** Video-IFBench contains 1.5K samples over 700+ public-source videos spanning ten domains, approximately 49 hours, and durations from 10 seconds to over 10 minutes (Section 3.2; Figs. 1–3).
- **Instruction taxonomy:** Four templates—Single, Multi, Selection, and Nested—cover 32 video tasks and 39 constraints: 22 semantic and 17 format constraints. Selection and Nested examples have exactly one valid branch or root-to-leaf path (Sections 3.1–3.2; Tables 5–6).
- **Construction pipeline:** MLLMs extract global descriptions and timestamped visual/audio facts; programmatic rules and MLLMs generate instructions and checklists; humans verify and clean the resulting samples (Section 3.2; Fig. 1).
- **Evaluation protocol:** Task coverage gates checklist-based constraint scoring. Semantic items use an LLM judge with video-grounded context, while mechanically verifiable constraints use deterministic checks. The resulting metrics are TCSR and the stricter, all-constraints TISR (Section 3.3).
- **Empirical study:** More than 20 proprietary and open-source MLLMs are compared across instruction structure, constraint type/count, task count, nested depth, branch count, branch position, model scale, and thinking mode (Sections 4.1–4.3; Appendix A).

These results establish performance on Video-IFBench under the paper’s checklist protocol and support the authors’ claim that constraint accumulation and conditional structure are major failure modes. They do not establish real-world deployment reliability, generalization beyond the benchmark’s videos and generated instruction patterns, or judge-independent absolute capability levels.

## Limitations and validity risks

The supplied paper does not contain a dedicated limitations section. Relevant risks are therefore mostly analyst inferences:

- **Analyst inference—judge dependence:** Absolute results shift when the LLM judge changes; all models score higher with the smaller judge, by up to 5.4 TCSR and 5.3 TISR points in Table 4. Trend consistency helps, but no human–judge agreement study is reported in the supplied text (Section 4.1; Appendix A.1).
- **Analyst inference—unequal model inputs:** Omni-modal models receive original video and audio, vision-only models receive frames plus Gemini-transcribed subtitles, Gemini and Doubao use 1 FPS, and GPT-5.4 receives 50 frames. These heterogeneous interfaces complicate attribution of differences solely to instruction-following ability (Section 4.1).
- **Analyst inference—synthetic-pattern sensitivity:** MLLMs and programmatic rules generate descriptions, facts, instructions, false conditions, and checklists. Human verification reduces errors, but annotator counts, agreement, correction rates, and sampling procedures are not provided (Section 3.2).
- **Analyst inference—conditional simplification:** Every Selection or Nested example is constructed to have exactly one valid branch or path. Ambiguous, overlapping, or multiple-valid-branch instructions are therefore outside the stated design (Section 3.2).
- **Analyst inference—source overlap:** Some videos come from existing benchmarks. The paper does not report whether evaluated models may have encountered those videos or annotations during training (Section 3.2).
- **Author-qualified claim:** Thinking mode improves Qwen models but provides little benefit or regressions for InternVL3.5; the proposed explanation involving reinforcement-learning rewards is explicitly a hypothesis, not experimentally verified (Appendix A.2; Fig. 7).

## Open research questions

1. **Can instruction-following scores be made less judge-dependent?** Absolute scores change with judge size. A next experiment could compare both automated judges against multiple blinded human annotators and report agreement by constraint category.

2. **Will models generalize to unseen constraint compositions?** The paper balances predefined constraint types but does not report a held-out-composition split. A next experiment could train or tune on selected combinations and test novel semantic–format compositions and unseen nesting structures.

3. **How much performance loss comes from perception versus instruction execution?** Task gating separates coverage from constraints but does not fully isolate incorrect video perception from failure to obey a correctly understood condition. A next experiment could compare video input against oracle timestamped facts for identical instructions.

4. **Can training remove branch-position bias without harming ordinary video QA?** Table 3 shows substantial sensitivity to where the correct branch appears. A next experiment could use branch-order augmentation or explicit condition verification, then evaluate all branch permutations alongside standard video-understanding tasks.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the benchmark design, metrics, and reported experiments because the supplied text includes the main paper, tables, taxonomies, examples, and appendix results.

Material missing information includes public-release status and licensing of the benchmark, exact train/development/test splits if any, annotator protocol and inter-annotator agreement, human validation of judge accuracy, per-domain results, inference hyperparameters and prompting details, computational cost, statistical uncertainty, and contamination analysis.
