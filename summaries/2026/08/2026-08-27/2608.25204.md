# LibriBrain100: One Hundred Hours of Broad and Deep MEG Data for Neural Speech Decoding at Scale

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.25204) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25204)


## One-sentence takeaway

LibriBrain100 releases 104.2 hours of annotated speech-listening MEG from 33 healthy English-speaking participants and shows that deep single-subject data can substantially improve 50-word classification for new subjects with as little as roughly 10 minutes of supervised fine-tuning.

## Problem and motivation

Non-invasive neural speech decoding lacks sufficiently large shared datasets, standardized splits, and common evaluation protocols, making reported progress difficult to compare or reproduce. The original LibriBrain dataset supplied unusually deep within-subject recordings but covered only one listener and one literary genre, limiting cross-subject evaluation and phonetic or semantic diversity. LibriBrain100 targets both gaps while retaining the long-term motivation of communication-restoring brain–computer interfaces (Introduction, pp. 2–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Deep and broad MEG dataset:** 80.5 hours from one subject and 23.7 hours across 32 additional subjects, totaling 104.2 hours. Participants listened to Sherlock Holmes audiobooks, TIMIT, MOCHA-TIMIT, or *The Moth* podcasts (Table 1, p. 4).
- **Controlled stimulus diversity:** TIMIT and MOCHA-TIMIT increase acoustic–phonetic coverage, while podcasts broaden semantic content and naturalistic speaking conditions; only the deep subject received all stimulus types (Sections 3.2 and B.2).
- **Standardized word-classification benchmark:** The authors evaluate a fixed 50-word vocabulary using balanced top-10 accuracy in within-subject and cross-subject settings, fine-tuning the existing MEG-XL model (Section 4, p. 7; Appendix F.2).
- **Reusable infrastructure:** Raw BIDS/FIF data, minimally preprocessed HDF5 data, time-aligned word and phoneme annotations, standard splits, and a Python loader are released for reproducible use (Section 3.3, pp. 6–7).
- **Data-efficiency evaluation:** Experiments test whether extensive data from one person transfers to 32 people with much less subject-specific data, including reductions to approximately 10 minutes per person (Sections 4.2–4.3).

These results support the authors’ claims that deep single-person recordings transfer usefully to shallow multi-person decoding and that reducing new-subject data from roughly 40 to 10 minutes causes little measured degradation in this benchmark. They do not establish brain-to-text decoding, practical communication, transfer to paralyzed patients, or decoding of attempted or imagined speech. Figure 2 also suggests corpus-dependent benefits from multi-subject training, but the supplied plot does not make every bar-to-condition mapping sufficiently clear for a complete numerical transcription.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** All recordings involve passive listening, whereas a functional communication BCI must decode attempted or imagined speech (Discussion, p. 9).
- **Author-stated:** Participants are healthy volunteers; clinical generalization is untested (Section 4.3).
- **Author-stated:** No brain-to-text baseline is supplied because the authors consider current open-ended decoding results insufficiently stable for standardized benchmarking (Discussion).
- **Author-stated:** Neural responses vary across sessions and participants because of attention, fatigue, physiology, anatomy, and recording conditions (Appendix D.1).
- **Author-stated:** Physiological artifacts such as eye blinks, cardiac activity, and muscle contractions are not explicitly removed in the provided minimal preprocessing (Appendix B.5).
- **Author-stated:** Forced alignment is vulnerable to names, out-of-vocabulary terms, atypical prosody, and spontaneous-speech phenomena; the authors report manual correction and recovery of word annotations but provide no quantitative boundary-error audit (Appendix B.6).
- **Analyst inference:** Claims about data-efficient adaptation depend on one unusually deep source subject and 32 target subjects hearing the same two Sherlock chapters; robustness to different source subjects, unseen genres, or independently collected sites is not established.
- **Analyst inference:** The 100%, 50%, and 25% conditions use different subject groups—12, 10, and 10 subjects—rather than repeated reductions for every subject, so subject-group variation may obscure small data-size effects (Section 4.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Most principal experiments use one model family, MEG-XL. Figure 14 adds a supervised comparison, but the paper does not provide a broad architecture or pre-training ablation.
- **Analyst inference:** Fine-tuning requires an NVIDIA H100 and approximately 20 hours per 100-hour run, which may limit independent replication (Appendix F.1).

## Open research questions

1. **Does deep-to-shallow transfer generalize beyond a single source participant?**  
   It remains open because all deep training data come from Subject 0. A next experiment could collect moderately deep recordings from several source subjects and compare single-source, multi-source, and leave-one-source-out transfer.

2. **Will the approximately 10-minute adaptation result hold for attempted or imagined speech and clinical users?**  
   The current data contain only passive listening by healthy volunteers. A next experiment could repeat the adaptation curve with attempted-speech recordings from the intended clinical population.

3. **Which stimulus dimensions drive cross-corpus transfer?**  
   Figure 2 indicates differing effects across Sherlock, TIMIT, MOCHA-TIMIT, and podcasts, but no controlled phonetic-versus-semantic ablation is reported. A next experiment could balance training hours and vocabulary across corpora, then remove acoustic, phonetic, or semantic diversity in turn.

4. **How robust are results across recording systems and preprocessing choices?**  
   All recordings use one 306-sensor MEGIN system and a common preprocessing pipeline. A next experiment could evaluate cross-site or cross-device transfer and compare raw, minimally processed, and physiology-cleaned inputs.
