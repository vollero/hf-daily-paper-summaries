# SWE Refactor Bench: Can Coding Agents Complete a Long-Horizon, Whole-Repository Stack Migration?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.23564) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23564)


## One-sentence takeaway

SWE Refactor Bench shows that current coding agents rarely complete genuine, behavior-preserving whole-repository stack migrations: only 28 of 520 runs (5.4%) passed migration audit, all fixed checks, and six-agent verification.

## Problem and motivation

Whole-repository migrations must satisfy two distinct conditions: the target stack must replace the source stack, and observable behavior must remain unchanged. Existing behavior-only evaluations cannot verify the first condition because an untouched repository already passes its tests, creating what the authors call “Blindness” (Sections 1 and 2.1). The paper addresses the resulting gap in evaluating long-horizon migrations across languages, frameworks, platforms, and build toolchains.

## Approach and contributions

- **Benchmark:** SWE Refactor Bench contains 20 migrations of real open-source infrastructure, spanning 7 language, 7 framework, 3 platform, and 3 build-toolchain tasks, with autonomous-work budgets of 6–30 hours (Table 1).
- **Three-stage evaluation:** Migration Audit checks that replacement occurred; 130,118 fixed behavioral checks require exact preservation; and six independent coding agents receive one hour each to seek executable differential counterexamples (Sections 2.2–2.3).
- **Failure-mode analysis:** The experiments separate failure to migrate from failure to preserve behavior and characterize the final correctness gap across 520 runs from 8 models and 26 model–effort configurations (Sections 3.1–3.3).
- **Evaluator validation:** The authors assess Migration Audit stability and human agreement, the complementary roles of fixed and agent-generated tests, and verifier strength and diversity (Section 3.4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Full success was rare. | 28/520 runs (5.4%) passed all three stages; 13/20 tasks had no accepted solution. | Section 3.2; Table 5; Table 9 |
| The best configuration remained below half of the maximum score. | Claude Opus 5 at xhigh effort scored 47.0/100, with 5 acceptances across 20 tasks and an average API cost of $74.9 per task. | Tables 2–3 |
| Migration and preservation failed on different runs. | 30 runs passed every fixed check without migrating; 252 migrated but failed fixed checks. | Section 3.3.1; Figure 3 |
| Near-perfect behavior did not reliably become perfect behavior. | Of 340 migrated runs, 58% reached at least 99% of fixed checks, 36% reached 99.9%, and 26% passed every check; 35/123 runs reaching 99.9% failed before 100%. | Section 3.3.2; Figure 5a |
| Fixed-suite perfection was insufficient. | Of 88 submissions that migrated and passed all fixed checks, 60 (68.2%) were broken by at least one verifier; only 28 survived all six. Median counterexample time was 17.0 minutes, versus 32.8 minutes for a survival. | Section 3.3.2; Figure 5b |
| Results varied sharply by migration class. | Mean scores were 31.4 for build toolchains, 17.2 for platforms, 12.0 for frameworks, and 5.6 for languages; framework tasks supplied 14/28 acceptances. | Tables 4–5 |
| Migration Audit was mostly stable and aligned with humans. | 3,405/3,536 criterion judgments (96.3%) were unanimous; on 156 runs from 6 tasks, judge–human agreement was 140/156 (89.7%, κ=0.795). | Section 3.4.1; Table 6 |
| Verifier capability materially affected acceptance. | The two Claude Opus 5 verifiers broke 55.7% and 53.4% of rounds, while the other four broke 21.6%–26.1%; removing the two strongest would raise accepted submissions from 28 to 46. | Section 3.4.3; Figure 6 |

These results establish a substantial capability gap on the benchmark’s 20 tasks and demonstrate that its three stages filter distinct failure modes. They do not prove semantic equivalence for accepted migrations, establish the intrinsic difficulty of migration categories generally, or isolate model ability from harness, effort, cost, and verifier-panel effects.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** The category results apply to these 20 tasks and do not rank the inherent difficulty of all migration projects (Section 1).
- **No proof of equivalence:** Passing six verifiers supplies evidence that no counterexample was found, not a demonstration of behavioral equivalence (Section 2.3).
- **Verifier dependence:** Acceptance depends materially on the selected verifier panel; removing the two strongest verifiers would increase acceptances from 28 to 46 (Section 3.4.3).
- **Audit conservatism:** Human comparison found 14 apparently too-strict versus 2 too-lenient audit decisions among 156 inspected runs, potentially undercounting up to two runs that passed all behavioral checks (Section 3.4.1).
- **Partial human validation:** Human audit comparison covers 6 of 20 tasks and 156 of 520 runs, not the complete benchmark (Section 3.4.1; Table 6).
- **Analyst inference — configuration confounding:** Models use different harnesses and differing numbers of effort configurations, so cross-model scores do not isolate the model itself; the paper reports these factors but does not provide a fully controlled common-harness comparison (Section 3.1; Tables 2–3).
- **Analyst inference — single migration attempts:** Each model–effort configuration runs each task once, leaving run-to-run variability unmeasured for migration performance (Section 3.1).

## Open research questions

1. **How stable are migration success rates across repeated attempts?** Each configuration receives one run per task. Repeating every model–task pairing with multiple seeds would quantify variance and confidence intervals.
2. **How much do harness and effort independently affect performance?** Harness differs by model family and effort settings are uneven. A factorial experiment using common models across matched harnesses, budgets, and effort levels could separate these effects.
3. **Can agents systematically close the final 1%?** Many migrated runs reach at least 99% but fail complete preservation. An experiment could add iterative differential self-testing against State A and measure movement through the thresholds in Figure 5a.
4. **How does verifier-panel scaling change acceptance?** Verifier strength substantially affects results. Varying panel size, model capability, search directions, and time budgets could estimate detection curves and marginal verifier value.
