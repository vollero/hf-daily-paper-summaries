# Capek 0.5: An Execution-Centric Vision-Language Model for Embodied Intelligence

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.06756) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06756)


## One-sentence takeaway

Capek 0.5 trains four execution-oriented vision-language specialists and consolidates them with weight merging and routed on-policy distillation, improving most matched offline benchmarks and simulated closed-loop task success over its Qwen initialization.

## Problem and motivation

Embodied agents repeatedly need to perceive changed scenes, reason over time, select visually grounded actions, and verify outcomes. The authors argue that existing post-training pipelines organize heterogeneous capabilities around individual datasets or tasks, making optimization interference and post-integration capability loss difficult to diagnose. Their goal is a single inference-time VLM whose capabilities are organized and audited by their roles in execution rather than by dataset origin (Sections 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Execution-centric taxonomy:** Four capability families—Spatial Reasoning, Temporal Understanding, Action Guidance, and State Verification—with typed, verifiable outputs such as relations, time spans, points, boxes, trajectories, predicates, and progress estimates (Sections 1–3).
- **Specialization:** Four parameter-compatible specialists start from a shared backbone and are independently optimized with GRPO, capability-specific parsers, and verifiable rewards (Section 4.1; Table 1).
- **Consolidation:** TIES merges specialist task vectors, after which routed multi-teacher on-policy distillation supervises student-generated prefixes; only one checkpoint is needed at inference (Section 4.2; Figure 6).
- **Two capacity tracks:** A 35B-A3B MoE model activating about 3B parameters per token and a dense 2B model (Section 2.1).
- **Evaluation assets:** Capek-StateBench adds physical-state and task-state verification tracks, while the broader evaluation covers offline benchmarks, controlled consolidation, and simulated closed-loop execution (Sections 5.1–5.4).

The matched Qwen comparisons support the authors’ claim that their complete post-training recipe improves many targeted capabilities under controlled inference settings. The consolidation study further supports retaining complementary specialist behavior more effectively than the reported alternatives.

The evidence does **not** isolate the contribution of every data source, reward component, or taxonomy choice. It also does not establish physical-robot performance, superiority on every benchmark, or causal attribution of closed-loop gains specifically to any one capability.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Capek 0.5 is an embodied reasoner, not a low-level controller. Tool orchestration, executable long-horizon recovery, and physical-robot evaluation are deferred to future work (Section 6).
- **Simulation-only closed loop:** EmbodiedBench and VIGIL provide interactive evidence, but both are simulated; transfer to real sensing, actuation error, and safety constraints is untested (Section 5.4). *Analyst inference.*
- **Remaining regressions:** The 35B-A3B model declines on 6/34 matched rows, including OmniSpatial, All-Angles-Bench, ERQA, MMVet, IFEval, and LiveCodeBench; the 2B model declines on 4/34, including QVHighlights-TimeLens and several general controls (Tables 2–3).
- **Incomplete component attribution:** Table 4 compares Mix-RL, TIES, MOPD-only, and TIES+MOPD, but the supplied text reports no ablation of individual reward terms, specialist datasets, routing quality, or the four taxonomy branches within closed-loop execution. *Analyst inference based on Sections 4–5.*
- **Benchmark construction concentration:** Both State Verification training data and Capek-StateBench derive from BEHAVIOR-1K trajectories, limiting evidence for cross-environment state verification despite described split and duplicate controls (Sections 3.4, 5.1.1, Appendix A.2). *Analyst inference.*
- **Metric comparability:** Offline rows mix accuracy, F1, IoU, normalized scores, RMSE, and DFD. The authors appropriately use directional row counts, but those counts weight heterogeneous tasks equally and do not measure effect magnitude uniformly (Section 5.2; Tables 2–3). *Analyst inference.*
- **Judged outputs:** Open-ended rewards and some evaluation rows rely on frozen semantic judges; the supplied text does not provide judge reliability or sensitivity analyses (Sections 4.1.2, 5.1.4, Appendix A.2). *Analyst inference.*

## Open research questions

1. **Does the approach transfer to physical robots?** This remains open because closed-loop tests are simulated. A next experiment could evaluate matched Qwen and Capek checkpoints on held-out real-robot manipulation tasks with sensing noise, execution failures, and safety reporting.

2. **Which specialization and consolidation components drive closed-loop gains?** The paper does not map individual specialists or TIES/MOPD components to interactive success. A factorial evaluation could remove one specialist or consolidation stage at a time and rerun identical EmbodiedBench and VIGIL episodes.

3. **How well does state verification generalize beyond BEHAVIOR-1K?** Training and the new benchmark share that environment family. A plausible experiment is a zero-shot state/progress benchmark built from another simulator or real robot, with non-overlapping tasks and state predicates.

4. **Can capability retention scale to additional tool-use specialists?** Section 6 proposes perception, code, and action tools but does not test them. A next experiment could add one tool-routing specialist at a time and measure both original-capability retention and long-horizon recovery.
