# DuplexGen: Adaptive Synthesis of Human-AI Turn-Taking Dialogues

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.26178) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26178)


## One-sentence takeaway

DUPLEXGEN uses small, scenario-specific human preference distributions to calibrate when an AI should listen, backchannel, or take the floor, producing synthetic training data that better matches human judgments and improves full-duplex dialogue behavior.

## Problem and motivation

Full-duplex assistants must continually decide whether to listen, acknowledge the speaker, or claim the conversational floor. The appropriate choice depends on the assistant’s role and interaction: cooperative settings may favor patient listening and backchannels, whereas competitive settings may invite more floor-taking (Introduction; Figure 1).

The authors argue that existing data miss this dependency. Generic human–human speech corpora contain natural timing but lack controlled roles and scenario-specific supervision, while heuristic or prompted synthesis can insert turn-taking actions without validating them against user preferences in the target setting (§1; §2.2). DUPLEXGEN targets this gap between natural but weakly grounded data and controllable but uncalibrated synthesis.

## Approach and contributions

- **Four-stage synthesis pipeline:** Convert source dialogues into spoken-style text, identify plausible action slots, calibrate action predictions with human judgments, and insert predicted floor-taking or backchannel markers during turn-by-turn synthesis (Figure 2; §3.2; Algorithm 1).

- **Distributional human calibration:** Five annotators judge incrementally revealed utterance prefixes at each slot using `[LISTEN]`, `[BACKCHANNEL]`, and `[TAKE_FLOOR]`. A context-aware Qwen3 predictor is fine-tuned to minimize KL divergence from the empirical vote distribution rather than a majority label (§3.2.3; §E.1).

- **Scenario-controlled study:** The method is instantiated for six tasks: teaching, planning, interview, negotiation, persuasion, and social chat, divided into cooperative and competitive settings (Table 1; §3.1).

- **Downstream full-duplex training:** PersonaPlex is fine-tuned on 1,623 hours of synthesized dialogue, producing six task-specific PP-DG checkpoints whose realized speech behavior is evaluated in single- and multi-turn settings (§4.3.1; §E.2).

These results establish improved agreement with the study’s collected preference distributions and modestly higher average naturalness for one fine-tuned full-duplex backbone in six prescribed settings. They do not establish generalization to unseen scenarios, real users interacting live, other languages or populations, or multimodal timing cues. Figure 4 supports relative scenario variation but does not supply exact plotted values in the retrieved text.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** The synthesized data cover only six interaction types, limiting claims about broader task generalization (Limitations).

- **Author-stated:** Full-duplex outcomes are sensitive to data and training choices such as turn-switching latency, pause duration, and learning rate, so stable training recipes remain unresolved (Limitations).

- **Author-stated:** Calibration is transcript-based and excludes prosody, pauses, intonation, speaking rate, overlap, and visual signals. The downstream audio study does not verify agreement between text-conditioned and audio-conditioned preferences (Limitations).

- **Analyst inference:** The preference sample is demographically and linguistically narrow: 248 English speakers residing in the US or UK. Preferences may not transfer across languages, cultures, or accessibility needs (§3.2.3; §C.2).

- **Analyst inference:** Full-duplex evaluation uses PersonaPlex as the user simulator rather than live users, so the results may not capture adaptation or feedback loops in real conversation (§4.3.3).

- **Analyst inference:** Instruction following is judged by GPT-4.1, while naturalness is human-rated. Thus only the latter directly supports a human-preference claim, and the paper does not report human validation of the instruction-following judge (§4.3.3; Table 8).

- **Analyst inference:** Six separately trained PP-DG checkpoints demonstrate task-specific specialization, but do not show that one model can infer and switch norms across scenarios (§4.3.1).

- **Analyst inference:** The calibration ablation is non-monotonic and covers only the 4B SWBD+DUPLEXGEN configuration; it supports diminishing returns in that setting, not a universal annotation-budget threshold (Table 9; Appendix D).

## Open research questions

1. **Can one calibrated model generalize to unseen roles?** The paper trains per-scenario predictors and full-duplex checkpoints, leaving cross-scenario transfer untested. A next experiment could hold out one task or an entire interaction type during calibration and evaluate its slot-level KL and human-rated behavior.

2. **Would acoustic or multimodal calibration change preferred actions?** The supervision exposes only incremental text, even though timing judgments may depend on prosody, pauses, overlap, gaze, or gesture. A paired experiment could collect text-only, audio, and audio-visual judgments for identical slots and compare their distributions.

3. **Do synthesized preferences hold during live human interaction?** Multi-turn tests use a model as the user. A controlled live-user study could compare PP-DG and PersonaPlex on naturalness, task success, interruption repair, and user adaptation over complete conversations.

4. **Can training become less sensitive to timing and rendering choices?** The authors report sensitivity to latency, pause, and optimization settings. A factorial study varying these parameters across multiple random seeds and scenarios could separate data effects from speech-rendering and training effects.
