# Can MLLMs Decode the Creative Leap? Introducing C4 for Cross-Concept Understanding

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.06501) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06501)


## One-sentence takeaway

C4 evaluates whether multimodal models can recover Chinese idioms from indirectly related visual concepts, finding that even the strongest tested model achieves only 50.7% exact-match accuracy and that unconstrained answer search is a major bottleneck.

## Problem and motivation

The paper addresses receptive creative understanding: recovering intended meaning from non-obvious but meaningful relations between concepts. The authors argue that existing creativity evaluations often involve open-ended outputs, multiple valid interpretations, and weak reward signals, making scalable and reproducible measurement difficult. They identify a gap in benchmarks that combine creative visual interpretation with fixed answers and inspectable reasoning paths (§1, §2.3).

Chinese four-character idioms, or chengyu, provide fixed targets while supporting phonetic, semantic, object-based, and culturally grounded substitutions. This lets the authors frame construction as cross-concept encoding and model inference as reverse decoding (§1; Figure 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **C4 framework:** A cognition-inspired formulation of cross-concept encoding and decoding built around target idiom slots, imageable substitute concepts, and manually reviewed bridge paths (§1, §3.1–3.2).

- **Structured difficulty:** Four synthetic levels vary bridge count and depth: L1 has one one-step path; L2 adds a second one-step path; L3 deepens one of two paths; and L4 deepens both (§3.2, Equation 3).

- **C4-Eval:** A benchmark of 184 generated images and 37 human-created web figures spanning 84 chengyu. Five task views produce 1,105 cases, including 884 answer-bearing T1–T4 cases (Appendix B.1).

- **Reusable annotations:** The synthetic network covers 47 targets, 168 anchored slots, and 758 deduplicated bridge chains, produced through independent annotation, cross-checking, and third-party review (§3.1; Appendix C).

- **Diagnostic evaluation:** Ten MLLMs are tested through open recovery, general bridge hints, four-way candidate recognition, answer-plus-explanation, and known-answer explanation (§3.5, §4.1).

The results establish that the tested models struggle on C4-Eval and benefit greatly from a reduced answer space. The authors interpret this as evidence that open answer-space search is the dominant bottleneck and that relation information is often partially available.

They do not establish that perception is generally intact in a causal sense: candidate options can provide additional semantic cues, not merely remove search effort. Likewise, the level results are associations across different targets and images, rather than controlled causal estimates of slot count or bridge depth; the authors explicitly acknowledge this (§4.4, Appendix A).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ stated scope:** L1–L4 encode construction parameters and are not an intrinsic psychological difficulty scale (Appendix A).

- **Authors’ stated ambiguity:** Annotated paths represent intended constructions but do not exclude other plausible interpretations. Exact match credits only the canonical target (Appendix A; Appendix E).

- **Confounded difficulty comparison:** Adjacent levels contain different targets and rendered images, so the claimed slot-versus-depth effect is not causal (§4.4).

- **Narrow cultural and linguistic domain:** The evaluation is restricted to Chinese four-character idioms and Chinese prompts. This limits direct generalization to other languages or forms of creative understanding (analyst inference from §1 and Appendix E).

- **Synthetic-image dependence:** All 184 synthetic images were generated with one named image generator, GPT Image 2; generator-specific visual conventions could affect results (analyst inference from Appendix D).

- **Single-run evaluation:** Each model result comes from one complete pass, with temperature zero only where exposed. No run-to-run uncertainty or statistical confidence intervals are reported (Appendix F).

- **Incomplete quantitative detail in supplied text:** Captions for Tables 1–3 and S1–S5 are present, but their cells are missing, preventing verification of per-model, per-task, and per-level values.

- **Release not yet evidenced as available:** The authors say resources “will” be released upon publication, so current accessibility and exact reproducibility are not established by the supplied text (Appendix B).

## Open research questions

1. **Does candidate prompting remove search difficulty or introduce answer-specific semantic cues?** The paper attributes T3 gains mainly to collapsed search, but candidates may also guide relation inference. A follow-up could compare random candidates, semantically matched distractors, character-overlap distractors, and retrieval-only hints.

2. **What is the causal effect of slot count and bridge depth?** Current levels vary targets and images alongside these parameters. A factorial experiment could generate matched images for the same targets while independently manipulating slot count and depth.

3. **How well does C4 transfer beyond chengyu?** The fixed-answer design is culturally and linguistically specific. A next experiment could construct parallel benchmarks for conventional expressions in other languages using comparable annotation and difficulty rules.

4. **Can models be trained to reverse intended bridge paths faithfully?** T5 shows that fluent explanations may not reproduce the encoded route. A follow-up could train or prompt models with explicit path supervision and measure both answer recovery and step-level bridge alignment.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The retrieved text gives detailed design, assumptions, and aggregate findings, but omits the contents of several central tables. Material missing information includes per-model T1–T5 scores and counts, exact source/level accuracies, paired outcome counts, annotation agreement statistics, reviewer qualifications, confidence intervals or repeated-run variance, detailed explanation-fidelity metrics, and confirmation that the promised dataset and code are publicly available.
