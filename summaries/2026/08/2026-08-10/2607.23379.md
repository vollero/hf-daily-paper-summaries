# When Activation Oracles Learn Not to Read: Concept-Specific Blind Spots in Fine-Tuned Oracles

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.23379) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.23379)


## One-sentence takeaway

Fine-tuning Activation Oracles on subjects that repeatedly represent a hidden concept can selectively impair verbalization of that same concept, even though it remains internally decodable.

## Problem and motivation

Activation Oracles (AOs) translate model activations into natural-language answers, potentially exposing information absent from visible behavior. Because AOs are learned models rather than neutral decoders, their training may also induce reporting blind spots. The authors test the previously unresolved question of whether subject-specific AO training creates better specialist readers or instead teaches an oracle not to report concepts persistently present during training (Sections 1 and 4.2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Controlled hidden-information testbed:** The authors LoRA-fine-tune Qwen3-8B subjects to use one of five hidden concepts while avoiding disclosure, varying cooperative versus strict behavior and pure versus 50/50 Taboo–UltraChat training mixtures (Section 4.1).
- **Own-versus-cross AO comparison:** Base AOs, matching-concept “own” FT-AOs, and different-concept “cross” FT-AOs are evaluated across HINT, REFUSAL, SAMETEXT, THINK, and OFFTOPIC activation regimes (Sections 4.2–4.4).
- **Separation of secrecy notions:** Behavioral leakage, linear decodability, target probability/rank, exact recovery, and judged semantic recovery are measured separately (Sections 3 and 4.4; Appendix F).
- **Mechanistic localization:** AO-internal probes, layerwise LogitLens analysis, entropy measurements, and layer-range LoRA ablations indicate that the blind spot arises in learned readout routing rather than from complete loss of target information (Section 7; Appendices D, G, and H).
- **Multi-concept robustness check:** Single-concept FT-AOs are tested on a subject containing both `leaf` and `moon`, showing that suppression follows the AO’s training concept rather than the subject as a whole (Section 8; Appendix I).

Together, these results support the authors’ claim that behavioral disclosure, representational decodability, and AO verbalization are distinct. The probes establish decodability, not semantic identity or causal use of the concept; the layer ablations partially restore accessibility and localize mediation, but do not identify the exact suppressive computation. The experiments also do not establish that the phenomenon occurs in naturally hidden knowledge or in other model families.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** The Taboo task is a synthetic model organism and may not capture backdoors, latent goals, or other realistic concealed information (Appendix A).
- **Author-stated:** Evaluation uses one backbone, five concepts, and LoRA-based subjects and AOs; generalization across architectures, scales, objectives, injection mechanisms, and fine-tuning methods is unknown (Appendix A).
- **Author-stated:** The mechanistic diagnostics localize the effect but do not identify its exact circuit or computation (Appendix A).
- **Author-stated:** Multi-concept evidence covers only the pair `leaf` and `moon`; the main figure evaluates only the cooperative α=1.0 two-concept subject (Appendix A; Appendix I.2).
- **Author-stated:** Main subject-probe cross-validation is not grouped by checkpoint, so high accuracy may partly exploit stable checkpoint signatures; OFFTOPIC accuracy does not prove active use of the concept (Section 5.2; Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference:** Exact matching is substring-based, while semantic recovery relies on a Claude Sonnet judge with only 25 judged generations per cell, introducing metric-specific false positives and judge dependence (Appendix F).
- **Analyst inference:** The causal ablation study is narrow—cooperative α=1.0 own FT-AOs, HINT only, 20 captures per concept—and removes six-layer LoRA ranges rather than isolating individual components (Appendix H).
- **Analyst inference:** The supplied text does not establish public availability of checkpoints, activation corpora, code, or prompt files, limiting independent reproducibility despite detailed procedures.

## Open research questions

1. **Does anti-reading arise for naturally occurring concealed information?** The paper tests synthetic Taboo concepts only. A next experiment could train and compare base, own, and cross AOs on independently verified backdoor or trigger-dependent subjects.

2. **Which computation implements target suppression?** Layer-range ablations implicate L12–23 but do not identify a circuit. Component-level ablation or activation patching across attention heads and MLPs could test where own-target information is rerouted.

3. **Can AO training objectives prevent concept-specific blind spots?** Only one AO training recipe is studied. A controlled comparison could add explicit concept-reporting supervision, contrastive own-versus-cross examples, or calibration losses and measure diagonal suppression.

4. **How does the effect scale with concept diversity?** The multi-concept test contains only two concepts. Training subjects and AOs with larger, overlapping, or changing concept sets could determine whether blind spots remain concept-specific or become global degradation.
