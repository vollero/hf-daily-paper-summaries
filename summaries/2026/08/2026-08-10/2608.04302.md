# CLIP-CC-Bench: Evaluating Paragraph-Level Video Descriptions in Video-Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.04302) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04302)


## One-sentence takeaway

CLIP-CC-Bench evaluates paragraph-level descriptions of 90-second movie clips using five embedding judges and finds that VideoLLaMA3 ranks first unanimously, while all tested models match overall meaning better than fine details.

## Problem and motivation

Existing video-language evaluations emphasize short clips, single-sentence captions, independently scored events, or discrete QA, leaving holistic paragraph-level description of minute-scale video weakly assessed. The authors argue that n-gram metrics miss semantic equivalence and narrative structure, timestamp-dependent methods are awkward for densely overlapping actions, and LLM judges can be opaque and inconsistent (Sections 1 and 2).

This matters because a useful long-form description must capture both the overarching narrative and specific actions, objects, relationships, and scene changes—not merely recognize isolated events.

## Approach and contributions

- **Curated benchmark:** 200 approximately 90-second clips totaling five hours, drawn from more than 140 films and series, each paired with one expert-written English paragraph. References exclude proper nouns and cultural identifiers to reduce reliance on memorized associations (Sections 3.1–3.4; Figure 1).
- **Multi-granular metric:** Five long-context embedding models independently score whole-paragraph cosine similarity and bidirectional best-match sentence-level F1; their harmonic mean balances holistic alignment and detail coverage (Section 4.3, Equations 1–5).
- **Consensus leaderboard:** Per-judge rankings of 17 VLMs are combined through Borda count to reduce sensitivity to judge-specific score scales (Section 4.3.3, Equation 6).
- **Reliability analysis and release:** The paper measures inter-judge agreement and bootstrap rank stability, and reports releasing the dataset-related evaluation scripts, model outputs, and aggregation tools (Abstract; Section 5.3).

These results establish internal consistency among the five embedding judges and stability of this particular 17-model ranking under resampling of the benchmark clips. They do not establish agreement with human quality judgments, causal superiority of any architecture family, performance outside narrative-film English descriptions, or explicit sensitivity to correct temporal and causal ordering.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:**

- The dataset is bounded to 200 movie/series clips, excluding instructional, surveillance, and user-generated video (Section 6).
- It is English-only and deliberately removes proper nouns, limiting conclusions to that controlled setting (Section 6).
- Each clip has a single reference, which cannot represent every valid description of a complex scene (Section 6).
- The metric compares semantic content but does not explicitly assess temporal or causal event ordering (Section 6).
- External validity against human judgments remains untested; the authors identify targeted human meta-evaluation as future work (Section 6).

**Analyst inferences:**

- Strong correlations among five embedding models demonstrate internal agreement, but the judges may share biases because they belong to the same broad method family; the supplied text provides no comparison against human ratings or substantially different automated metrics.
- The claim that removing proper nouns reduces memorization is plausible but not experimentally isolated: no ablation compares references or prompts with and without names.
- Architecture-family conclusions are observational. Models differ in size, training, frame capacity, and architecture, while inference uses each model’s maximum supported frames (Table 2); therefore the experiment does not isolate architecture as the cause of ranking differences.
- Each reference was produced by one annotator and then cleaned by GPT-4o. Manual transcript verification is described, but no independent reference-quality agreement or audit statistics are reported (Section 3.3).
- One clip lacked outputs for every model, so results cover 199 rather than all 200 clips; comparisons remain paired, but the omitted clip’s effect is unknown (Section 4.1 note).

## Open research questions

1. **Do HM-CF and Borda rankings align with human judgments of factuality, completeness, and coherence?** This remains open because the paper measures internal judge agreement only. A next experiment could have multiple blinded human raters score a stratified sample and correlate those ratings with each judge and the ensemble.

2. **Can the metric detect incorrect event order and causal relationships?** The authors state that ordering is not explicitly modeled. A useful experiment would create controlled descriptions with swapped, deleted, or causally reversed events and test whether an order-aware extension separates them from correct paraphrases.

3. **How well does the benchmark generalize beyond English narrative films?** Instructional, surveillance, user-generated, and multilingual videos are absent. A next study could reproduce the protocol across several domains and languages with matched clip lengths and multiple references.

4. **How much do proper-noun removal and single-reference annotation affect rankings?** Neither design choice is ablated. An experiment could collect named and anonymized versions plus several independent references per clip, then compare score variance and rank changes.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the benchmark design, reported rankings, and reliability statistics because the supplied text is extensive and includes explicit experimental settings and locations.

Material missing information includes the full numeric contents of Tables 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 10; human meta-evaluation results; annotator agreement or reference-quality measurements; contamination tests; compute cost and evaluation runtime; exact inference implementations for each VLM; and controlled ablations of judge count, aggregation method, proper-noun removal, reference multiplicity, frame capacity, or architecture family.
