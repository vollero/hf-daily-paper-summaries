# Multi-Agent Forensic Reasoning for Generalizable Deepfake Video Detection

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.06865) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06865)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors pair a 100,000-video, explanation-annotated benchmark with four specialized forensic MLLM agents and a judge, achieving 53.28% F1 on generator-disjoint testing—5.83 points above the strongest reported single-model baseline.

## Problem and motivation

The paper addresses explainable deepfake-video detection that generalizes to unseen synthesis methods. The authors argue that existing benchmarks cover too few recent generators and mostly provide binary labels, while conventional detectors may learn dataset-specific shortcuts and single MLLMs may overlook weak or distributed artifacts. This limits both cross-generator transfer and the auditability of authenticity decisions (Introduction; Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

1. **FaceVid-Forensics-100K:** A face-centric dataset of 100,000 videos—21,075 real and 78,925 fake—covering 33 synthesis methods and face swapping, reenactment, and entire-face synthesis. Each video receives observations for texture, lighting, motion, and physics plus a verdict-consistent explanation (Dataset Overview; Tables 1 and 6).

2. **Automated annotation pipeline:** Five MLLMs independently annotate videos; DeepSeek-V4 Pro aggregates their reports using ground-truth labels to resolve conflicts and align explanations with verdicts (Label Generation; Figure 3).

3. **Specialized multi-agent detector:** Four independent Qwen2.5-VL-7B agents inspect sampled frames for dimension-specific cues, after which a judge reconciles their reports, optionally consulting the frames directly (Multi-Agent System; Figure 4).

4. **Staged optimization:** All agents undergo supervised fine-tuning; the judge is subsequently refined with GRPO using binary classification reward while observation agents remain frozen (Training Pipeline).

These results establish improved performance within the paper’s curated, generator-disjoint benchmark and show additive benefits from specialization, joint training, GRPO, and judge access to frames. They do not establish robustness to unconstrained real-world video, non-face manipulations, post-processing attacks, or datasets outside FaceVid-Forensics-100K. The explanation experiment measures agreement with model-generated reference rationales and model judges, not human-verified forensic correctness.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated efficiency cost:** The method is slower than one-pass Single and CoT inference because it invokes several agents, though parallel observation agents keep it faster than sequential multi-turn inference (Section C.1; Table 11).

- **Author-stated explanation caveat:** GRPO optimizes classification rather than the explanation rubric, and the fake-versus-real explanation-score pattern comes from only 200 sampled videos and should not be generalized (Section B.4).

- **Analyst inference—label-conditioned annotation risk:** DeepSeek-V4 Pro uses the ground-truth label to resolve observation conflicts and even performs reverse inference when every annotator is wrong. Consequently, the textual supervision may encode label-confirming rationales rather than independently verified visual evidence (Label Generation; Figure 3; Listings 7–8).

- **Analyst inference—evaluation confounding:** Fake generators are held out, but real OOD videos come only from GenVidBench and ViF-Bench, while training real videos come from other mixtures. Source cues could therefore affect OOD classification; no source-controlled ablation is reported (Table 6).

- **Analyst inference—class imbalance:** FaceVid-Forensics-100K contains 78,925 fake versus 21,075 real videos, whereas the OOD set contains more real than fake videos. Accuracy, recall, and F1 are reported, but the supplied text does not define the positive class or provide precision, specificity, calibration, or confidence intervals (Tables 2 and 6).

- **Analyst inference—limited independent validation:** Explanation references and evaluations are produced by MLLMs, including DeepSeek-V4 Pro in both annotation and evaluation. No human forensic assessment, inter-annotator reliability, statistical significance testing, or confidence intervals are reported.

- **Reproducibility gap:** Prompts and major training hyperparameters are supplied, but the number and temporal coverage of sampled frames, LoRA configuration, GRPO clipping/KL coefficients, and dataset licensing or release details are not established in the retrieved text.

## Open research questions

1. **Do label-conditioned annotations reflect genuine visible evidence?** This remains open because ground truth guides conflict resolution and reverse inference. A next experiment could compare these labels with blinded human-forensic annotations and measure cue-level agreement and hallucination rates.

2. **How much OOD performance comes from generator generalization versus source recognition?** Real and fake samples have different source compositions. A source-matched, generator-held-out evaluation or an adversarial source-classification probe could isolate the two effects.

3. **Can explanation quality improve without sacrificing GRPO’s classification gains?** Table 9 shows lower explanation scores after GRPO despite higher detection F1. A plausible experiment is multi-objective optimization combining verdict reward with human-rated evidence-grounding reward.

4. **Does the method remain effective under operational distortions?** Compression, resizing, frame dropping, and other deployment conditions are not evaluated in the supplied text. A controlled corruption suite could measure detection, calibration, and explanation stability across severity levels.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported method, dataset composition, and tabulated results because the supplied text includes the main paper and appendices.
