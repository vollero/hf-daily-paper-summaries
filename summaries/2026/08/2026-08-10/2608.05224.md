# Small Foundation Models of Human Cognition and Behaviour

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.05224) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05224)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors report that small language models fine-tuned on human-choice data nearly match a reproduced 70B model within familiar psychological paradigms, while larger models transfer better to unseen experiments and prompt diagnostics indicate reliance on task content rather than choice history alone.

## Problem and motivation

Large language models fine-tuned on behavioural data can predict human choices across cognitive tasks, but it is unclear whether their apparent generality requires tens of billions of parameters or comes from shortcuts in recorded choice sequences. The authors address two gaps: prior work had not independently varied base-model size, adapter capacity, and behavioural-data quantity, nor systematically separated the contributions of instructions, stimuli, feedback, and choice history (Introduction, pp. 1–2; Section 4.2.1, pp. 6–7).

This matters because such models can serve as empirical noise-ceiling estimators only if they capture predictable task-relevant structure. The authors explicitly do not present them as mechanistic theories of cognition (Introduction, p. 2; Discussion, pp. 9–10).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Controlled scaling study.** Fourteen base language models spanning 135M–14B parameters and four families are fine-tuned on Psych-101 using response-token-masked supervised learning and rank-stabilized LoRA. Model size, LoRA rank \(r\in\{4,8,16,32,64\}\), and nested training-set fractions are varied separately (Sections 3 and 4.1, pp. 3–5).

2. **In- versus out-of-distribution comparison.** Performance on held-out Psych-101 participants is contrasted with transfer to 18 held-out Psych-201-RT experiments, using mean response negative log-likelihood (Section 4.1, pp. 4–6).

3. **Prompt-channel diagnostics.** The prediction context is decomposed into instructions, stimuli, feedback, and choice history. Structural ablations distinguish informational content from formatting, including a new content-masked condition (Section 4.2.1 and Figure 3, pp. 6–7).

4. **Order-sensitivity diagnostic.** Fifty context permutations per participant–target pair test whether models are invariant for an independent-trial task but sensitive for a response-adaptive task (Section 4.2.3, pp. 8–9).

5. **Released artifacts.** The paper states that training/evaluation code and fine-tuned LoRA adapters are publicly released (Reproducibility Statement, p. 11).

The results support the authors’ claim that in-distribution prediction can be competitive at sub-billion scale when adapter capacity is sufficient, whereas transfer remains scale-sensitive. The ablations reject history or prompt format as sufficient explanations for average performance over the tested tasks.

They do not establish that these models implement human cognitive mechanisms, attain a true irreducible noise ceiling, or generalize reliably beyond the represented paradigms. The order-permutation evidence covers only two deliberately contrasting experiments, and the paper acknowledges that the observed in-distribution plateau might partly reflect the supervised next-choice objective rather than the dataset’s intrinsic predictability (Sections 4.1.2 and 5–6).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Training-data reductions preserve all 160 paradigms, so they measure additional participants per experiment rather than the value of broader paradigm coverage (Section 6, p. 10).
- **Author-stated:** Participants are nested within experiments; no individual is observed across multiple tasks, preventing person–task decomposition and strong claims about individual predictability (Section 6, p. 10).
- **Author-stated:** Only low-rank adaptation is tested. Full fine-tuning could reveal whether the apparent ceiling arises from the learnable signal or adapter constraints (Section 6, p. 10).
- **Author-stated:** All four families are dense decoder-only transformers; conclusions may not transfer to mixture-of-experts, state-space, or hybrid architectures (Section 6, p. 10).
- **Author-stated:** Context-window truncation differs substantially: 2.5% of trials for 32,768-token models, 25.0% for SmolLM2, and 39.6% for OLMo-2. Some within-family scaling therefore confounds size, generation, and context length (Section 3, p. 4).
- **Author-stated:** Reliability under outcomes more extreme than those in Psych-101 remains untested (Ethics Statement, p. 11).
- **Analyst inference:** The permutation result provides a useful directional check but weak broad evidence for task-adaptive order processing because it uses only one exchangeable task and one adaptive control; the authors note that only one exchangeable Psych-101 task was available (Sections 4.2.1–4.2.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Comparisons with Centaur-70B are not perfectly controlled because the reproduced baseline uses quantized base weights while the smaller models use higher precision, as acknowledged in Section 4.1.1.
- **Analyst inference:** The text alternates between 32 ablation experiments and 27 analyzed experiments; Figure 4 explains that five mixed/continuous-response experiments were excluded, but this narrows the aggregate shortcut conclusion.

## Open research questions

1. **How much does adding new paradigms improve transfer?** Participant-level subsampling holds paradigm coverage fixed, leaving breadth unmeasured. A next experiment could train on nested subsets of entire experiments and evaluate on fixed unseen Psych-201-RT tasks.

2. **Is the Psych-101 plateau caused by data stochasticity, LoRA, or supervised next-choice training?** The current design cannot separate these possibilities completely. Full fine-tuning of the smallest models, crossed with adapter rank and data size, would test whether the plateau moves.

3. **Does task-adaptive order sensitivity generalize beyond two experiments?** Only one exchangeable and one adaptive task are tested. A purpose-built benchmark containing multiple independent, Markovian, and response-contingent tasks could compare permutation variance across dependency structures.

4. **Can models predict stable person-level behavior across paradigms?** Psych-101 and Psych-201 do not observe the same participants across tasks. Collecting a cross-task panel and evaluating held-out tasks for known individuals would separate person-specific from task-specific prediction.
