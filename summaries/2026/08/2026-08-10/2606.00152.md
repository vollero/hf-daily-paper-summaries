# PrivacyPeek: Auditing What LLM-Based Agents Acquire, Not Just What They Say

> [← Daily summary](../2026-08-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.00152) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00152)


## One-sentence takeaway

PrivacyPeek shows that all ten evaluated tool-using agents acquired unnecessary sensitive data in benign tasks, and that output-only privacy audits and prompt-level safeguards miss much of this acquisition-stage risk.

## Problem and motivation

LLM agents decide which tools to invoke and which records or fields to retrieve, so sensitive information can enter their context even when it is unnecessary for task completion. The authors argue that existing evaluations largely inspect outgoing responses or actions, overlooking this earlier acquisition stage; once retained in context, excess data may later be disclosed or affect reasoning. PrivacyPeek addresses the missing definition and measurement of whether an agent respects a task-conditioned minimum data scope (Introduction; Preliminaries: Agentic Setup).

## Approach and contributions

- **Acquisition-stage benchmark:** PrivacyPeek contains 1,182 executable, synthetic cases covering seven acquisition behaviours and 16 application domains. Each case has a human-reviewed minimum scope and out-of-scope acquisition and disclosure targets (Task Design and Data Generation; Tables 1, 6–8).
- **Acquisition Inspection:** The evaluator searches structured tool observations—not merely tool arguments or final answers—for case-specific, out-of-scope items. It reports Content Exposure Rate (CER) and task-completion-conditioned HCER (Evaluation, Equations 1–2).
- **Probe Elicitation:** After task completion, tools and network access are disabled and a case-specific probe tests whether the retained context supports disclosure. A fixed GPT-4o judge produces PLR, OLR, and task-conditioned HPLR verdicts (Evaluation, Equations 3–4; Appendix I).
- **Empirical audit and mitigation study:** Ten agents from four model families are evaluated under a common Smolagents environment, followed by confidentiality-marker analysis and prompt-level mitigation experiments (Experiments; Analysis; Mitigation Methods).

The evidence establishes substantial acquisition and probe-disclosure rates within this controlled benchmark and shows that task completion, acquisition, and disclosure must be reported separately. It does not establish that acquired data caused later disclosures, that probes occur at the measured frequency in deployment, or that the observed cross-agent correlations are causal.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** The threat model excludes malicious users, compromised tools, and prompt injection, so the results concern agent-initiated over-acquisition during benign tasks (Preliminaries).
- **Author-stated measurement boundary:** Probe Elicitation is a post-task stress test, not an estimate of real-world probe frequency or an acquisition detector. Joint CER/PLR positives show co-occurrence, not causality (Evaluation; Appendix F).
- **Author-stated policy boundary:** Minimum scopes are case-specific annotations, not universal organizational policies; deployed settings would require workflow owners and relevant experts to define them (Appendix B).
- **Author-stated marker caveat:** The marker analysis compares only 44 marked cases with 1,138 different unmarked cases. It is non-paired and descriptive, and one marked case shifts the rate by about 2.3 percentage points (Appendices E and K).
- **Analyst inference:** Synthetic records, a fixed eight-tool interface, a six-step budget, and one agent framework limit ecological validity for longer or less constrained deployments (Setup; Appendices A and C).
- **Analyst inference:** Disclosure labels depend partly on a fixed GPT-4o judge, with human review only for ambiguous cases; the supplied text does not report judge-agreement statistics or comparative judge ablations (Evaluation; Appendix I).
- **Analyst inference:** Prompt mitigation was tested on five of ten agents, while the three-variant comparison was performed fully on only Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct; broader mitigation generalization is therefore uncertain (Mitigation Methods; Appendix L).

## Open research questions

1. **Can pre-tool enforcement prevent acquisition without sacrificing task completion?** Prompting left substantial residual CER. A next experiment could compare field projections, scoped views, and policy gates against prompting while jointly measuring TCR and CER.
2. **How well does PrivacyPeek transfer to real workflows?** Its records are synthetic and access is restricted to eight audited tools. A next study could replay consented, de-identified enterprise workflows with domain experts defining minimum scopes.
3. **What causally links acquisition to later disclosure?** CER-positive probe results establish co-occurrence only. A paired intervention could selectively remove an acquired item from retained context and measure the resulting change in PLR.
4. **How robust are scope and disclosure judgments?** The text does not report inter-annotator or alternative-judge agreement. A next experiment could independently re-annotate minimum scopes and compare multiple blinded judges with human adjudication.
