# OR-License: Evaluating LLM Agents as Real-World Operations Research Practitioners

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.24087) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24087)


## One-sentence takeaway

OR-License evaluates agents on original industrial optimization engagements and finds that unreliable feasible delivery remains the main barrier to matching client-accepted expert solutions, despite competitive objective quality on successful submissions.

## Problem and motivation

Professional operations research requires turning incomplete business materials into requirements, mathematical formulations, solver implementations, and validated decisions. The authors argue that existing benchmarks underrepresent original client complexity, ongoing information acquisition, and comparison with previously accepted professional deliveries (§1).

The benchmark therefore tests whether agents can establish the optimization problem as well as solve it.

## Approach and contributions

- **Industrial benchmark:** 31 instances across 10 industrial scenarios, derived from paid engagements totaling $3.68 million in project value. De-identification preserves original requirements, inputs, deliverable formats, and information boundaries (§3.2; Appendix G).
- **Controlled client interaction:** ORSim selects business facts from an expert-reviewed catalog, enforces disclosure through a deterministic controller, and renders authorized replies. Agents remain responsible for formulation and algorithm design (§3.3; Appendix C).
- **Delivery evaluation:** Hidden checks assess feasibility, independently recomputed objectives are compared with original client-accepted expert solutions, and Deployment-Adjusted Quality (DAQ) assigns zero credit to infeasible deliveries (§§3.1–3.4). Specialized bike-sharing tasks additionally receive human semantic review (Appendix D.4).
- **Process analysis:** Expert annotations examine clarification timing, error origins, and complete hypothesis–explanation–experiment–interpretation–update chains (§3.5; Appendix B).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| No evaluated primary configuration matches expert DAQ | Across 11 models under Claude Code, 31 tasks and five runs per model–task pair, Grok 4.6 leads with 88.39% feasibility, 97.92% conditional quality, and 86.55% DAQ; expert reference is 100%. | Table 1; §4.1 |
| Feasible delivery explains most of the leading configuration’s deficit | Of Grok’s 13.45-percentage-point DAQ gap, 11.61 points arise from infeasible deliveries and 1.84 from net objective shortfalls on feasible deliveries. | §4.1 |
| Conditional objective quality varies less than feasibility | Under the primary setup, feasibility ranges from 45.16% to 88.39%, while conditional quality ranges from 96.92% to 101.01%; feasible subsets differ between models. | Table 1; §4.1 |
| Harness choice materially changes performance | With matched endpoints, tasks, budgets, and run counts, GPT-5.6 Sol’s DAQ rises from 85.02% under Claude Code to 93.59% under Codex; GLM-5.3-Flash falls from 63.21% to 34.42%. | Tables 1, 7; Appendix D.7 |
| Complete investigations remain uncommon | Task-equalized complete research-chain proportions range from 24% to 45% across models on evaluable tasks. | §4.1; Figure 4 |
| Clarification is predominantly initial | Before-solver-code questions account for 86.7%–98.3% of questions across models. Confirmed-resolution proportions are 10.6%–37.0% at that stage, versus approximately 56% for DeepSeek in S4 and 71% for Hy4 Preview in S5. | §4.2; Figure 5 |
| Flat selection improves simulator reliability in the tested setting | On 119 standard and 85 adversarial queries across five instances, with five runs per query, flat selection achieves reply success of 95.46% and 98.82%, respectively; all-five-run success is 94.12% in both categories. | Appendix C.2; Table 3 |
| Failures extend beyond implementation | Implementation/delivery is the largest annotated error category for only two of eleven models; other profiles emphasize formulation errors. | §4.3; Figure 5 |

These results establish a gap against the benchmark’s historical expert references under the tested configurations. They do not establish comparable optimization ability from conditional quality alone, because each model succeeds on a different subset. Later clarification results are observational associations, not evidence that requiring later questions would improve delivery. Reported Table 1 spreads describe mean within-task variation, not standard errors (§4).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-qualified comparisons:** Conditional quality uses model-specific feasible subsets; error-event shares do not provide additive attribution of failure probability; incomplete research chains do not necessarily imply unresolved issues (§§4.1–4.3).
- **Simulator imperfections:** The default simulator fails its success criterion on 32 of 1,020 tested replies. Reliability testing covers five benchmark instances, leaving broader generalization uncertain (Appendix C.2).
- **Analyst inference—configuration dependence:** The harness ablation shows that results characterize model–harness systems. It covers only three models, so harness robustness across the complete model set remains unestablished (Appendix D.7).
- **Analyst inference—reference scope:** Client acceptance establishes professional relevance, but does not certify globally optimal solutions or equalize historical expert resources with agent budgets (Appendices D.3, G.1).
- **Analyst inference—metric dependence:** DAQ permits sufficiently large quality gains to compensate for failures, and objective ratios depend on the prescribed normalization. The reliability profile exposes compensation sensitivity but does not eliminate it (Appendices A.2–A.4).
- **Reporting gaps:** The supplied trajectory protocol does not clearly specify retained-run selection, evaluable-task denominators, or inter-annotator agreement for trajectory coding. Simulator annotation agreement is reported separately (Appendices B, C.2).

## Open research questions

- **Does feedback-triggered clarification improve feasibility?** Later questions sometimes resolve issues, but the evidence is observational. Compare otherwise matched agents with and without a policy that requests clarification after diagnostic feedback.
- **How much failure comes from missing facts versus incorrect formulation?** Error profiles identify categories without causally separating their sources. Compare ORSim-mediated access, complete business facts, and an expert-specified formulation under matched budgets.
- **Can structured investigation improve final delivery?** Complete-chain quality and DAQ rank models differently. Test an intervention requiring linked diagnostic experiments and validated revisions, measuring both chain completion and DAQ.
- **How stable are results across interfaces?** The limited harness ablation shows substantial model-dependent changes. Extend the matched harness comparison to all evaluated models.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** in the reported benchmark design and headline results; **medium** in broader explanations of agent failure.

Material unavailable information includes executable assets and release location, actual prompt bodies despite their listed descriptions, per-task performance, trajectory-selection details and annotation agreement, and matched expert resource measurements. The supplied text contains no controlled experiment establishing that sustained clarification or research-chain completion causes higher DAQ.
