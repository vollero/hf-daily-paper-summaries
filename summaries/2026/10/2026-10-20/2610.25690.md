# ScaleNav: Scalable Internet Video Pretraining for Language-Conditioned Local Navigation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.25690) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25690)


## One-sentence takeaway
ScaleNav combines action-free Internet video pretraining with simulated action supervision to improve navigation to initially visible, language-specified indoor targets, with supporting results on two simulation benchmarks and a quadruped robot (§§3–4).

## Problem and motivation
The task is to reach a target described in text, visible in the robot’s initial RGB observation, and stop nearby. The authors argue that diverse real-robot action data is expensive, simulation leaves a transfer gap, and extracting motion supervision from Internet videos can introduce errors. Their proposed alternative learns goal-directed visual dynamics directly from action-free videos before aligning them with executable robot actions (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Navigation-specific video-to-action training:** Starting from Wan2.2-TI2V-5B, ScaleNav performs instruction-conditioned video pretraining, partially frozen action alignment on simulation data, then joint video-action fine-tuning. Internet video replay continues during the latter stages (§3.2).
- **Navigation conditioning and execution:** A shared Diffusion Transformer predicts future video latents and planar motion chunks. An initial-frame anchor preserves the instruction’s visual reference, while recent observations and actions supply history. Repeated zero-motion labels teach stopping (§§3.1–3.2). The joint world-action architecture follows DreamZero; the contribution is its navigation adaptation and training pipeline, rather than the architecture family itself (§2).
- **Data and evaluation construction:** The authors curate 18.2K video navigation segments and approximately 2.6K simulated action-labeled episodes from 650 scenes. They also construct HabitatGS-LN for instance-specific language grounding and adapt Short-Horizon OVON to a single-view setting (§§3.3, 4.1).

## Evidence and main results

Simulation success requires stopping within 200 executed actions and within 1.25 m footprint distance of a valid target. OVON accepts annotated category instances; HabitatGS-LN requires the instruction-specified instance (§4.1).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Best reported simulation performance among evaluated methods | Single-view OVON: **86.5% SR, 71.3% SPL**, versus fine-tuned Uni-NaVid’s 80.2%, 68.1%. Evaluation covers 524 episodes across 36 scenes. | §4.1, Table 1 |
| Strong instance-specific navigation results | HabitatGS-LN: **94.9% SR, 86.1% SPL**; LocateAnything + NavDP reaches 82.8% SR, while fine-tuned Uni-NaVid reaches 71.6% SPL. Evaluation covers 157 episodes across 45 held-out scenes. | §4.1, Table 1 |
| Added Internet video training improves results | Without curated Internet videos: OVON 81.1% SR / 65.5% SPL; HabitatGS-LN 90.4% / 80.8%, using the same simulated action data. | §4.1, Table 1 |
| Dedicated video pretraining outperforms tested alternatives | HabitatGS-LN: pseudo-action supervision gives 73.9% SR / 67.1% SPL; action-free video mixing without Stage 1 gives 90.4% / 83.1%; full model gives 94.9% / 86.1%. | §4.2 |
| Initial-frame context helps | Removing the anchor reduces HabitatGS-LN SR from 94.9% to 89.8%; skipping alignment gives 93.6%, and removing replay gives 94.3%. | §4.2 |
| Real-robot transfer is demonstrated within a limited setting | **83.3% overall SR** across 60 trials per method: four tasks in each of three indoor environments, five trials per task. Authors report the highest SR in every environment; comparator counts are absent from retrieved tables. | §4.3; Appendix E |
| Chunking amortizes inference cost | RTX 4090: **684 ms per nine-action policy call**, reported as **76 ms per executed action**; excludes rendering, communication, and motion execution. | Table 1; Appendix B |

These results support the authors’ claim that their curated video-training procedure benefits this local-navigation setting. They do not isolate video pretraining from replay in the main video/no-video comparison, establish statistical significance, or demonstrate broad navigation generality.

There is a numerical inconsistency: the authors report a **5.3 percentage-point** OVON SR gain, while Table 1’s rounded values imply **5.4 points**. The HabitatGS-LN gain is consistently 4.5 points (§4.1).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Quantitative evaluation covers indoor, initially visible object targets; outdoor and region-level navigation remain untested (§5).
- **Comparison boundary:** The single-view OVON selection changes the original protocol, so these scores are not directly comparable to published three-view results (Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference—attribution:** The no-Internet variant removes both Stage 1 and replay. Component ablations help, but no training-compute-matched comparison or video-data scaling sweep is reported (§§4.1–4.2).
- **Analyst inference—uncertainty:** No navigation confidence intervals or variation across training seeds are supplied. Real-robot evidence spans only three environments with manually assessed success (§4.3).
- **Analyst inference—deployment:** Amortized latency does not describe uninterrupted control: the real robot holds still between chunks while awaiting remote inference, and network time is excluded from profiling (Appendices B, E).
- **Incomplete retrieved evidence:** Tables 2–6 have captions but lack tabulated contents. Narrative descriptions recover some results, but full ablations, training settings, and real-robot comparison counts remain unavailable.

## Open research questions
- **Does performance improve with more Internet video data?** No scaling sweep is reported despite the scalability motivation. Next experiment: vary curated video volume while controlling simulation data and training compute.
- **Does transfer extend to outdoor scenes and region goals?** Both are explicitly unevaluated (§5). Next experiment: compare full and no-Internet variants on held-out outdoor and region-target tasks.
- **Is noisy pose supervision the cause of the pseudo-action variant’s degradation?** The authors suggest this explanation without isolating it (§4.2). Next experiment: compare unweighted pseudo-actions with confidence filtering and manually checked pose subsets.
- **How does chunk execution affect responsiveness?** Deployment pauses between chunks, and the denoising-step sweep measures latency only (Appendices B, E). Next experiment: vary executed chunk length and denoising steps while measuring success, wall-clock completion time, and stopping accuracy.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied methods and main simulation results are clear, but missing table bodies limit assessment. Material gaps include full stage-specific training configuration, total training compute, VLM identity, complete real-robot baseline results, navigation uncertainty estimates, dataset/checkpoint release status, and quantitative data-scaling evidence.
