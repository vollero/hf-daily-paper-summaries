# Text-to-Image Generative Modeling Without Language Pretraining

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.26863) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26863)


## One-sentence takeaway
On GPIC at 256×256 resolution, Halcyon learns competitive text-to-image generation without text-only model pretraining, outperforming selected pretrained-language baselines with substantially lower total pretraining compute (§§4.2–4.3).

## Problem and motivation
Modern text-to-image systems commonly depend on pretrained language models, but the authors argue that their contribution to image generation is insufficiently isolated and their training cost often omitted. The paper tests whether paired image–caption data alone can teach effective text conditioning, while accounting for language-model, image-tokenizer, and image-generator pretraining (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **From-scratch training recipe:** Halcyon uses an early-fusion, decoder-only Transformer with a joint text–image vocabulary and weighted next-token prediction. Its discrete image tokenizer, BPE text tokenizer, and generative model are trained from scratch (§§3.1–3.2). OpenWebText trains the BPE tokenizer only; it does not pretrain the generative model (§4.1).
- **Controlled evidence on language pretraining:** The authors vary text-only training allocation and scheduling, test additional language training followed by a full image-training epoch, and compare pretrained versus random Qwen3 initialization (§4.3).
- **Captioning through dropout replacement:** Replacing caption-dropped examples with image-to-text sequences preserves conditional and unconditional image-token counts while adding caption supervision (§4.4).
- **Compute-inclusive comparisons:** Selected autoregressive and diffusion baselines are trained on GPIC and compared using total component pretraining FLOPs (§4.2; Table 15).

The architecture builds on prior early-fusion methods; the principal contributions are the training recipe, controlled experiments, and compute accounting (§3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Better GPIC quality with lower total training compute | On GPIC Test-50K at 256×256, Halcyon-0.52B achieves **48.0 gFD** versus **63.3** for LlamaGen-XL/8, using **18.2× less total pretraining compute**. Lower gFD is better. | §1; §4.2; Table 15 |
| Larger Halcyon surpasses the selected diffusion baseline | Same GPIC setting: Halcyon-1.6B achieves **35.3 gFD** versus **41.0** for JiT-T2I-XXL/8++, with **275× less total pretraining compute**. | §4.2; Table 15 |
| Gains extend to compositional benchmarks | At 256×256, Halcyon-1.6B versus JiT-T2I-XXL/8++: **53.5 vs. 43.2 GenEval**, using rephrased prompts, and **77.9 vs. 76.9 DPG-Bench**, using original prompts. Guidance is selected on GPIC validation. | §§4.1–4.2; Table 5 |
| Text-only training does not help in tested schedules | For Halcyon-0.52B, allocating **10%, 20%, or 50%** of a fixed budget to language pretraining worsens GPIC gFD from **48.0** to **51.6, 52.8, and 65.1**. Additional language training followed by a full T2I epoch uses **1.5× compute** and yields **49.2**. | §4.3; Table 6 |
| Pretrained initialization does not beat random initialization | Qwen3-0.6B pretrained on **36T text tokens**, then trained on GPIC, obtains **47.8 gFD**, versus **46.6** for the same architecture randomly initialized; both use one GPIC epoch and CFG **6.0**. | §4.3; Table 7 |
| Dropout replacement adds captioning capability | At Halcyon-0.52B, replacing caption dropout with I2T reduces image-conditioned text NLL from **18.414 to 1.102** on GPIC Test-50K. Authors report comparable gFD, GenEval, and DPG-Bench across model sizes under the same budget. | §4.4; Table 9 |
| Sampling stability is better established than training stability | Across three sampling seeds per Halcyon checkpoint, GPIC gFD standard deviation is at most **0.14**. Across three training seeds, the tested **0.10B + I2T** configuration has gFD standard deviation **5.54**, evaluated with CFG **7.5**. | Appendices C.2–C.3 |

These results support the authors’ scoped conclusion that text-only pretraining is unnecessary for strong performance in the evaluated settings. They do not establish that language pretraining never helps. Cross-family comparisons change architecture and representation as well as language conditioning; the controlled initialization and scheduling experiments provide more direct evidence. Compute savings include the full cost of pretrained components, rather than only the incremental cost of reusing them (§5; Table 15).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Training uses GPIC at one resolution. Generalization to other datasets, higher resolutions, larger controlled models, and larger budgets remains untested (§5).
- **Author-stated causal gap:** The experiments do not isolate the benefit of jointly learning text representations and image generation. Controlled interventions focus on autoregressive models despite including diffusion baselines (§5).
- **Author-stated capability limits:** Small text and fine glyph reconstruction remain difficult; generated legible text is challenging. Caption alignment remains below GPIC ground-truth captions (§§4.1, 4.4; Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference:** Small gFD differences in the language-pretraining experiments lack configuration-specific training-seed uncertainty in the supplied text. The reported training-seed study covers only the smallest I2T model (Appendix C.2).
- **Analyst inference:** GenEval uses modified prompts, and all benchmark guidance scales are selected for GPIC gFD, which constrains interpretation outside that protocol (§4.1).
- **Author-stated ethical risks:** Models may inherit GPIC biases, and cheaper training may lower barriers to malicious visual generation (Ethics Statement).

## Open research questions
1. **Does the result generalize across datasets and resolutions?** The authors explicitly leave this open (§5). Repeat matched pretrained-versus-random comparisons on another paired corpus and at higher resolution.
2. **Does language pretraining help at larger model sizes or longer training budgets?** Controlled experiments use relatively small models (§5). Run matched initialization and scheduling studies at larger scales with repeated training seeds.
3. **Does the finding extend to diffusion or hybrid generators?** Controlled interventions are autoregressive (§5). Compare pretrained and randomly initialized text conditioning within a fixed diffusion or Transfusion-style architecture.
4. **Can captioning improve without sacrificing generation?** Dropout replacement preserves generation quality, but caption alignment remains below ground truth (§4.4). Sweep I2T allocation while holding image-token exposure fixed and measure both capabilities.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** Main findings are explicitly supported by prose and the available compute table, but many referenced tables contain only captions in the supplied retrieval.

Material missing information includes full architecture and optimization tables, complete benchmark and ablation results, per-category scores, most selected guidance scales, numerical inference throughput, and training-seed uncertainty for the central comparisons. Code and checkpoint availability is author-reported and was not independently verified.
