# Self-Evaluating Recursive Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.04902) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04902)


## One-sentence takeaway
SERA trains a shared recursive-agent policy to generate externally grounded evaluation rubrics, improving verified task success over RAO on two synthetic text benchmarks while reducing external supervision calls (§4.1).

## Problem and motivation
Recursive agents invent and delegate subtasks whose outcomes usually lack ground-truth labels. The authors argue that binary node rewards provide coarse execution feedback, require expensive verification throughout the tree, and do not measure whether the decomposition itself was useful. They propose separate training signals for decomposition, execution, and evaluation within one shared policy (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Learned subtask evaluation:** Before delegation, the parent commits weighted success criteria. After execution, another invocation of the same policy applies those frozen criteria, producing a continuous reward gated by predicted subtask success (§3.2).
- **Verified-outcome rubric training:** Cloned delegation states produce alternative rollouts of the same subtask. Program-verifier or external-judge labels train rubric generation to rank successful outcomes above failures; these external labels do not directly reward ordinary child execution (§3.4).
- **Decomposition credit:** Delegations receive the root outcome multiplied by their subtree’s fraction of all leaves. This rewards organizing substantial portions of successful trees, while treating leaves equally (§3.3).
- **Integrated training and search:** Separate execution, decomposition, and rubric updates alternate on shared weights. Learned rubrics also select candidate node resolutions during bottom-up inference search (§§3.5–3.6, 4.3).

The paper additionally introduces TextWorld-Sync, a shared-state coordination extension of TextWorldExpress (Appendix C).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| SERA improves over RAO on both benchmarks | Average root-task success: **74.31%** on TextCraft-Synth, **+5.38 percentage points**; **65.07%** on TextWorld-Sync, **+13.14 points**. Table caption specifies task-count-weighted averages over three seeds. | §4.1; Table 1 caption |
| Explicit rubric training improves rubric-reward execution | Adding rubric training to SERA without decomposition improves average success by **2.58 points** on TextCraft-Synth and **9.16 points** on TextWorld-Sync. | §4.1 |
| Decomposition reward has domain-dependent value | Full SERA versus SERA without decomposition: **+4.81 points** on TextWorld-Sync, but **−0.53 points** on TextCraft-Synth. | §4.1 |
| External supervision decreases | RAO-to-SERA program-verifier calls: **550.94K → 257.96K** on TextCraft-Synth. TextWorld-Sync external-judge calls: **147.80K → 35.79K**; input tokens: **633.80M → 125.32M**, accumulated over training. | §4.1; Table 2 discussion |
| Ranking alignment outperforms an added dispersion signal | On TextCraft-Synth with rubric execution rewards, ranking-trained evaluation achieves **74.84%** average success; adding the discriminativeness reward reduces it to **65.77%**. | §4.2; Table 3 discussion |
| Learned selection adds inference gains | On TextWorld-Sync, SERA with policy-guided selection and **N=2** candidates increases average success from **65.07% to 67.50%**; external Kimi-K3 selection achieves **67.29%**. | §4.3; Table 4 discussion |
| Fewer external calls do not consistently mean faster training | On identical **2 × 8 H200 GPUs**, SERA versus RAO takes **24 h 06 m versus 29 h 36 m** on TextCraft-Synth, but **24 h 14 m versus 13 h 08 m** on TextWorld-Sync. | Appendix B.3 |

These results support the effectiveness of the combined training scheme in the tested environments. Ablations support an interaction between rubric training and rubric-based execution rewards. They do not isolate shared-weight “learning to judge improves solving” as the sole causal mechanism, establish general-purpose agent gains, or demonstrate lower total compute cost.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Only one base model and two synthetic benchmarks are evaluated; larger-model and real-world transfer remain untested (§5).
- **Author-stated:** Self-evaluation can drift or be gamed. Robustness to sparse or noisy verification remains unresolved, and external grounding is still required (§5).
- **Author-stated:** Equal leaf weighting ignores differences in subtask importance (§§3.3, 5).
- **Analyst inference:** Equal optimizer-update budgets do not establish equal compute budgets: rubric training requires counterfactual rollouts, and measured runtimes differ substantially (Appendices B.2–B.3).
- **Evidence ambiguity:** Appendix B.3 calls SERA the most accurate TextCraft method, whereas §4.1 reports it second overall and below its no-decomposition variant. Its explanation of periodic rubric computation is also difficult to reconcile with ordinary rubric-scored execution in §3.2.
- **Reproducibility gap:** The supplied text contains table captions and narrative summaries but omits table bodies, including detailed hyperparameters, uncertainty values, and task-family listings. Release is described as planned.

## Open research questions
1. **How robust is rubric learning to unreliable labels?** The authors leave sparse/noisy verification unresolved. Vary label frequency and inject controlled label noise, measuring verified success and rubric ranking accuracy (§5).
2. **Does the method transfer beyond synthetic text tasks?** Only one model and two simulations are tested. Repeat the component ablations across model sizes and real tool-based tasks with verifiable outcomes (§5).
3. **Does leaf count measure useful decomposition?** Equal weighting and mixed benchmark effects leave this uncertain. Compare leaf coverage with importance-weighted credit and inspect unnecessary delegation (§§3.3, 4.1).
4. **What is the compute-adjusted benefit of search?** The reported selection experiment uses one branching setting without supplied inference-cost measurements. Sweep branching budgets and compare verified success at matched token or latency budgets (§4.3).

## Confidence and missing information
