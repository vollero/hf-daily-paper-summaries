# BlenderWorld: Benchmarking Visual Agents for 3D Creation from Real-World References

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.23986) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23986)


## One-sentence takeaway
BlenderWorld evaluates agents on realistic Blender deliverables, finding that the leading system scores 79.9/100 yet satisfies every rubric criterion on only 42.2% of tasks (§5.2).

## Problem and motivation
Realistic 3D creation requires agents to combine reference interpretation, spatial reasoning, tool use, and iterative verification while producing editable assets. The authors identify limited coverage of diverse production requests in existing benchmarks and argue that single-view assessment cannot reliably establish structural correctness, especially without canonical ground-truth assets (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Benchmark:** 223 reconstruction, modification, and reference-based design tasks across nine categories and 16 application scenarios, using text, images, videos, and optional structured specifications (§§3.1–3.3).
- **Evaluation protocol:** Eight task-conditioned rendered views, direct scene inspection, 2,651 weighted binary criteria, and eight visual-quality dimensions. Visual and scene-inspection judges receive criteria according to their required evidence (§§4.1–4.2).
- **Baseline study and diagnosis:** Thirteen complete agent systems evaluated on the same tasks, with tool-use analysis and selected failure traces exposing viewpoint shortcuts, contact errors, and specification drift (§5; Appendix E.8).

The contribution is a benchmark and evaluation framework rather than a newly trained 3D generation model.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Broad task and evidence coverage | 223 tasks: 78 without visual references and 145 with image/video references; 59 include specification files; 2,651 binary criteria. | §3.3; Appendix A |
| Leading system still falls short of complete acceptance | Across all 223 tasks, GPT-6-Astra scores 79.9 versus Opus-5’s 73.0 on the 0–100 fused metric; Perfect rates are 42.2% and 25.6%. Score equally weights Rubric and Dimension; failed evaluations count as zero. | §§4.2, 5.2 |

| Automatic judging agrees less with humans on close comparisons | GPT-5.6-terra Overall preference agreement is 82.99% on 147 human-majority artifact pairs, falling to 68.57% on 70 pairs with an average score gap below 10 points. | Appendix E.1 |
| Aggregate rankings largely withstand scoring changes | With artifacts and judgments fixed, varying rubric weight over {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1} changes only Grok-4.6/Opus-4.8 ordering. | Appendix E.6 |
| Selected traces explain concrete structural failures | Flat photographic quads cause side-view disappearance; projection checks omit ground support; a verifier checks combined leg-and-caster height instead of required timber length. | §5.3.3; Appendix E.8, Fig. 15 |

These results establish comparative performance of the tested **agent configurations** under this protocol. They do not isolate model capability from harness effects or show that additional inspection causes better results. The selected traces support specific failure mechanisms, without estimating their prevalence across the benchmark.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope:** Repeated runs, controlled harness/reasoning comparisons, broader platforms, deterministic geometric checks, and professional-artist rubric calibration remain future work (§7).
- **Analyst inference—comparison confounding:** GPT models use Codex while other systems use Claude Code, and each task is run once. Differences therefore cannot be attributed solely to the underlying models, and ranking variability is unmeasured (§5.1).
- **Analyst inference—judge uncertainty:** Human alignment is weaker on close pairs; preference agreement does not establish correctness of every binary scene judgment (Appendix E.1).
- **Analyst inference—feedback claims:** Aggregate inspection counts cannot establish how effectively agents use feedback, a qualification the authors explicitly acknowledge (§5.3.2).
- **Analyst inference—scope and sampling:** Model-assisted briefs favor references with identifiable structures; representativeness of unconstrained professional work is not established (§3.2). Binary criteria can combine conditions, so one failed criterion need not imply an easy repair (Appendix E.3).

## Open research questions
1. **How much do harnesses affect rankings?** Harness choice differs across systems (§5.1). Run supported models across both harnesses with matched tools, reasoning settings, and resource budgets.
2. **Can targeted verification improve structural correctness?** Inspection counts are observational, while traces reveal missed structural checks (§5.3.2; Appendix E.8). Compare ordinary visual review with explicit multi-view, contact, and dimension checks under equal budgets.
3. **How reliable are fine-grained acceptance decisions?** Close-pair agreement declines, and expert calibration is planned (Appendix E.1; §7). Audit criterion-level judgments against expert inspection and deterministic measurements.
4. **Are rankings stable across repeated executions?** Only one run per task is reported (§5.1). Repeat representative configurations and measure score, full-pass, and rank variability.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** Core methods and headline findings are explicit, but causal interpretations are limited.

Material gaps include exact generation time/token budgets, GPU models and matched compute costs, repeated-run uncertainty, criterion-level human validation, and confirmed release/licensing details.
