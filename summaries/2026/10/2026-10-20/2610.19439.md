# Progress-Aware Distillation for Mitigating Stagnation in Small Language Model Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.19439) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19439)


## One-sentence takeaway
ProD uses teacher–student stagnation gaps to adapt trajectory preference training, improving accuracy and reducing interaction costs over SFT across eight small language model agents on mathematical and factual reasoning benchmarks (Tables 3–4).

## Problem and motivation
SFT-distilled agents can repeatedly attempt failed reasoning and tool actions as interaction histories accumulate. The authors find this “progress stagnation” across model families, with stronger effects in smaller models and later turns (§2.2; Figure 2). Such loops waste tokens and impede successful completion.

The identified gap is that cloning successful teacher trajectories does not directly correct failures in the student’s own interactions, including failures on training queries (Appendix D).

## Approach and contributions
- **Stagnation diagnosis:** Quantify repeated thought–action pairs using maximum historical ROUGE-L overlap, embedding similarity, or an LLM judge. Teacher–student differences help account for benign task-specific repetition (§2.2; Table 1).
- **Adaptive training objective:** Starting from an SFT student, generate student rollouts and favor successful teacher trajectories over them using a length-normalized, SimPO-inspired objective. Its adaptive margin combines excess student stagnation with final-answer incorrectness; an SFT term preserves teacher-trajectory likelihood (§3; Equations 1–3).
- **Iterative correction:** Regenerate student trajectories and recompute margins between training rounds. Experiments assess accuracy, stagnation, retrieval evidence acquisition, and inference costs (§4; Appendices I and M).

The distinctive contribution is the stagnation-dependent margin applied to student-generated agent trajectories, rather than preference optimization or teacher imitation alone.

The evidence supports improved accuracy and lower interaction costs in the tested settings. Gold-evidence analysis strengthens the interpretation that ProD improves useful retrieval progress, rather than merely reducing repeated wording. It does not establish generalization to unrelated agent domains, identify stagnation’s root causes, or demonstrate statistically significant gains. Token reductions also do not directly establish latency or deployment-cost savings.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** OOD tests involve dataset shifts within mathematics and factual QA, rather than entirely new domains (Appendix Q).
- **Author-stated diagnostic limits:** Trajectory scores do not distinguish observation misunderstanding, planning failure, tool over-trust, or missing knowledge; accuracy remains the primary metric (Appendix Q).
- **Statistical uncertainty:** The checklist explicitly reports no error bars because of computational costs. Appendix L reports three-seed mean accuracy for its comparison, but does not resolve uncertainty for the main results.
- **Analyst inference—selection risk:** Checkpoints and hyperparameters are selected using in-domain evaluation results; a separate validation/test separation is not clearly described, creating possible optimism in ID estimates (Appendix B.1).
- **Analyst inference—component attribution:** The listed objective ablations remove both SFT and the adaptive margin together, or remove the margin alone. They do not isolate removal of SFT while retaining the adaptive margin (§4.5).
- **Observed tradeoff:** General benchmark averages are preserved or slightly improved, but some individual scores decline, including MMLU for Qwen3-4B and Gemma3-4B (Appendix N).

## Open research questions
1. **Does ProD transfer to different agent tasks and longer horizons?** Current OOD tests remain within the same task families and inference is capped at ten turns. Evaluate the same objective on a distinct tool environment with longer interaction budgets (§4.1; Appendix Q).
2. **Can localized supervision outperform trajectory-level penalties?** Current scores cannot identify why or where stagnation begins. Annotate failed observation updates and tool calls, then compare targeted penalties against the existing trajectory margin (Appendix Q).
3. **How much benefit comes from retaining SFT independently of the margin?** Existing ablations do not fully separate these factors. Run a matched factorial comparison of SFT retention and fixed versus adaptive margins across seeds (§4.5).
4. **When does extra training pay for itself?** Training overhead and inference-token savings are reported separately. Measure end-to-end latency and cumulative resource use across deployment volumes and iteration counts (Appendices H–I).

## Confidence and missing information
