# RoboValue: A Fine-Grained Sim-and-Real Benchmark for Unified Evaluation of Robotic Value Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.26663) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26663)


## One-sentence takeaway
RoboValue shows that strong robotic outcome discrimination and forward-progress scores can coexist with weak instruction grounding, history-dependent tracking, recovery assessment, and consistent subtask credit across valid executions.

## Problem and motivation
Robotic value models judge execution from observations and instructions, supplying feedback for policy learning, planning, and monitoring. Incorrect judgments could reward unresolved failures or misallocate credit to intermediate actions. The authors identify fragmented evaluation and heterogeneous model interfaces as obstacles to diagnosing these errors: outcome accuracy and forward-progress correlation alone leave important capabilities untested (§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Diagnostic dataset:** RoboValue-Dataset contains 3,500 expert training demonstrations and 2,792 held-out test trajectories across 15 simulation and 20 real-world dual-arm manipulation tasks. Test cases include matched failure/recovery branches, recurring visual states, and alternative valid subtask orders (§3).
- **Unified evaluation framework:** Model-specific adapters expose scalar values, pairwise comparisons, and supported textual subtask descriptions. Metrics cover task-state understanding, temporal progress, failure/recovery reasoning, and value consistency while retaining model-dependent value semantics (§4).
- **Leaderboard and diagnosis:** Separate zero-shot and one-shot evaluations cover standard conditions and independent embodiment/environment shifts. The contribution is a benchmark and comparative analysis; Full-Shot evaluation is planned rather than reported (§5–7).

## Evidence and main results

Unless stated otherwise, metric scores below are multiplied by 100 and average simulation and real-world task means with equal domain weight (§4.2; Table 3).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Different models lead the two tracks | Aggregated leaderboard overall scores: RoboMeter-4B leads zero-shot at **60.05**; Robo-Dopamine 2.0-8B Preview leads one-shot at **58.33**. These are separate rankings. | Table 2, p. 13 |
| Broad task grounding exceeds fine-grained grounding | In-domain zero-shot RynnValue-4B: **83.74 TGA-CT**, versus **56.01 TGA-CF**. RoboMeter-4B: **82.00**, versus **29.29**. | Table 3, p. 14 |
| Strong forward correlation can conceal reversal failures | In-domain zero-shot TOPReward-8B: **94.29 VOC**, **95.51 Memory-VOC**, but **13.50 Cycle-VOC**. | Table 3; §6.1, pp. 14–15 |
| Reversal tracking does not establish history-dependent tracking | In-domain zero-shot RoboMeter-4B: **89.43 Cycle-VOC**, but **2.36 Memory-VOC**; RynnValue-4B: **81.74**, but **−23.89**. | Table 3, p. 14 |
| One-shot adaptation improves ProcVLM grounding | ProcVLM-2B’s in-domain overall TGA-CF rises from **14.36% zero-shot** to **51.16% one-shot**; the latter has no category-level ties. | Table 5; §6.3.1, pp. 16–17 |
| Failure localization remains weak on average | Authors report mean FPL error of approximately **34% of trajectory duration**, versus approximately **32%** for uniformly sampled random onset predictions under the same protocol; lower is better. | §6.1, p. 15 |
| Failed-recovery outcomes are harder than corrective activity | In-domain one-shot Robo-Dopamine 2.0-8B Preview passes **75.6%** of failed-recovery attempt stages, **48.5%** of failed-outcome stages, and **38.4%** of complete failed-recovery branches. | Figure 5, p. 18 |

| Subtask credit varies across valid orders | On real-world tea-set executions, CSVC ×100 is **−40.98** for one-shot Robo-Dopamine 2.0-8B Preview and **2.30** for zero-shot RoboMeter. Illustrated curves associate gain changes with subtask position. | Figure 7; §6.3.4; Table 57 |

These results establish complementary weaknesses within the tested tasks and interfaces. They do not establish that any particular training component causes the differences, or that improving benchmark scores improves downstream policies. Timestamp dependence is an author hypothesis requiring a controlled ablation (§6.1); supervision explanations are likewise qualified by configuration and training-data differences (§7).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Truncated coverage:** A substantial middle portion of the source is missing, including evaluation implementation and sensitivity-analysis appendices. This analysis cannot provide complete coverage.
- **Author-stated attribution limits:** Training data, temporal inputs, score construction, and configurations differ across models, preventing clean attribution to model size, backbone, or supervision (§7).
- **Metric interpretation:** Memory-VOC can reward temporal shortcuts; reverse playback need not be physically executable; VS can favor a trend in the wrong direction; CSVC agreement does not establish correct progress valuation (§4.3.2–4.3.4).
- **Author-stated semantic caveat:** Semantically matched subtasks need not contribute identical value in different contexts, complicating interpretation of CSVC (§4.3.4).
- **Analyst inference—ranking sensitivity:** RoboReward’s discrete outputs exclude it from VS and CSVC, which count as zero in the leaderboard. Overall rankings therefore depend partly on interface compatibility and aggregation choices (Table 2).
- **Analyst inference—scope and judging:** Evidence is confined to collected dual-arm manipulation trajectories. SIA additionally depends on an LLM judge; judge validation and annotation agreement are not available in the supplied text (§3; §4.3.1).
- **Unreported downstream validation:** The supplied experiments diagnose evaluator behavior but do not demonstrate resulting policy-learning improvements; the conclusion identifies this as a next step (§8).

## Open research questions
1. **Do diagnostic scores predict downstream policy quality?** This link remains untested in the supplied experiments. A next experiment could train otherwise matched policies with different evaluators and compare benchmark profiles against actual task success and recovery.
2. **How much does timestamp information explain TOPReward’s reversal gap?** The authors explicitly leave causal attribution open. Remove or perturb timestamps while fixing visual sequences and inference settings, then repeat VOC and Cycle-VOC (§6.1).
3. **Can memory mechanisms improve accumulated-progress judgments?** Strong reversal tracking coexists with poor Memory-VOC. Compare matched evaluators using sampled history versus explicit completed-subtask memory, including history perturbations that expose time-based shortcuts (§7.1).
4. **Can cross-solution supervision improve useful credit consistency?** The authors propose aligning equivalent subtask gains but do not test it. Train matched models with and without this supervision, then evaluate held-out valid orders and downstream segment weighting while accounting for context-dependent contributions (§7.2).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied main text supports the central findings, but source truncation prevents full implementation and robustness review.

Material missing information includes complete adapters and inference settings, leaderboard normalization rules, sensitivity results, detailed test allocation, annotation/judge reliability, statistical uncertainty, and independently verified asset availability. Full-Shot results and downstream policy-improvement validation are not reported in the supplied text.
