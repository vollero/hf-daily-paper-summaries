# Beyond Maliciousness: Static and Runtime Attack-Surface Profiling of Agent Skills

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.19264) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19264)


## One-sentence takeaway
In a sampled cohort of 6,309 agent-skill listings, inferred capabilities and high exposure substantially exceed malicious-intent verdicts, supporting capability profiling alongside threat detection while leaving substantial validation and reproducibility gaps.

## Problem and motivation
Skills direct agents using the session’s existing privileges. A benign skill can therefore expose consequential capabilities without exhibiting malicious intent. The authors argue that prior threat-oriented studies do not adequately measure this distinction over the same population (§§I–II).

Author-written `allowed-tools` declarations provide pre-approval rather than enforceable capability limits. Their optional use, limited vocabulary, and unchecked accuracy make them insufficient descriptions of a bundle’s potential reach (§I; §IV-D).

## Approach and contributions
- **Two-phase profiling:** Whole-bundle model analysis, four deterministic heuristics, and dependency provenance checks are combined with instrumented sandbox execution and a separate trace judge. Threat verdicts, exposure scores, and capability footprints remain distinct (§III-B; Fig. 2).
- **Joint cohort measurement:** Listings are selected for execution without prior threat or risk filtering, allowing the authors to compare intent and exposure within the same popularity-banded sample. This is not a representative marketplace census (§§II-B, III-A).
- **Declaration-gap analysis:** Author pre-approvals are compared with inferred capabilities across five mutually expressible classes; credential and environment access are excluded because the declaration vocabulary cannot represent them (§IV-D).
- **Complementarity analysis:** Static and runtime findings are compared, with additional mappings to MITRE ATLAS and two OWASP catalogs. The authors explicitly do not claim to introduce skill detonation itself (§§II-B, II-E, IV-E).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Capability prevalence exceeds threat verdict prevalence | Among 6,309 listings, 4,431 (70.2%) have an elevated capability; 338 (5.4%) receive suspicious or malicious verdicts. | Fig. 1; §§IV-A–B |
| High exposure frequently lacks a threat verdict | Of 1,621 high/critical-exposure listings, 1,306 are labeled benign and 315 suspicious/malicious. All threat-verdict listings have at least medium exposure. | Table I; §IV-C2 |
| Shell and data-access capabilities are common | Across the full cohort: shell execution 50.1%, filesystem read 35.4%, filesystem write 31.4%, network egress 30.8%, credential access 13.1%; capabilities are inferred from bundles. | Fig. 6; §IV-D1 |
| Declarations are uncommon and often understate capabilities | Of 6,061 parseable listings, 770 (12.7%) declare tools. Within those 770, 457 (59%) under-declare, 304 (39%) over-declare, and 166 (22%) align; error categories overlap. Comparison covers five expressible classes. | §IV-D5; Table II |
| Declared dependencies are usually unpinned | Of 2,871 dependency-declaring listings, 2,830 (99%) reference at least one unpinned dependency; 75 reference a takeover-prone host. Resolution checks establish existence, not authenticity. | §§III-B5, IV-D4 |
| Static and runtime risk findings overlap only partially | Using presence of a phase-specific risk finding: 978 listings are flagged by both phases, 1,653 by static analysis only, and 708 by detonation only. This differs from the exposure-level risk definition. | Table III; §IV-E1 |
| Validation provides limited reassurance | Full-bundle review of 140 randomly sampled benign-labeled listings found no missed threats, yielding an approximately 2.1% one-sided 95% upper bound on the benign miss rate. Verdict agreement across 89 re-scans was 84%. | §§III-B1, III-B6 |

These results establish differences among the scanner’s measured capability, exposure, and intent outputs within this cohort. They do not establish realized harm, marketplace-wide prevalence, scanner precision, or a causal effect of marketplace review policies. Runtime flags also lack clean separation between executor and skill behavior (§§III-B6, IV-E).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Sampling and dependence:** Popularity extremes are excluded, marketplaces use different popularity measures, and publishers contribute uneven numbers of listings. The authors caution against marketplace-wide estimates and independence-based uncertainty intervals (§III-A; §III-B6).
- **Incomplete validation:** Flagged-listing adjudication remains unfinished and covers one marketplace; no precision estimate or external ground truth is available. The scanner’s developers authored the study ( §III-B1; Conflict of Interest).
- **Runtime coverage and attribution:** Almost all listings receive one run without a matched control. Cohort-level prevalence filtering cannot reliably attribute activity to individual skills; shell and environment access occur universally in the smaller control cohort (§III-B6).
- **Model and label instability:** Verdicts are thresholded model scores; static models differ across listings, and free-text capability labels are canonicalized afterward. Rare-label counts are only indicative (§III-B).
- **Reproducibility:** Bundles, raw reports, prompts, rubric, and implementation are withheld. Conditional aggregate/index access cannot reconstruct the full results (Data Availability).
- **Analyst inference:** Separately designed scoring axes do not independently validate the exposure measure. The joint distribution supports the authors’ descriptive distinction, while its accuracy depends on the proprietary scoring process.

## Open research questions
1. **How much additional behavior would repeated execution reveal?** Single sessions miss paths and uninvoked scripts. Run matched-control, multi-session evaluations with varied task inputs and measure added coverage and phase overlap (§III-B6; §VII).
2. **How accurate are capability and exposure labels against independent judgments?** Existing review does not establish their accuracy or scanner precision. Have external reviewers assess a stratified bundle sample using a published rubric (§III-B1; Conflict of Interest).
3. **Would independently generated profiles improve decisions?** Profiles and attestations are proposed but not implemented or evaluated. Compare user and reviewer decisions with profiles, existing declarations, and permission prompts (§V; §VII).
4. **Does marketplace composition explain exposure differences?** Current comparisons mix executable bundles with inert prose and differently sampled catalogs. Repeat comparisons within matched exposed-listing strata and across successive crawls (§IV-C6; §VII).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the descriptive findings and explicitly documents substantial caveats.

Material unavailable information includes the production rubric and prompts, full execution tasks and sandbox configuration, raw bundles and traces, independent ground truth, completed flagged-verdict adjudication and precision, and complete results needed for independent reproduction.
