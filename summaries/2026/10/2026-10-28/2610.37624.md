# Rare, Relevant, and Still Wrong: How Memory Fails LLM Group Decisions

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.37624) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37624)


## One-sentence takeaway
On controlled group-decision tasks, rarity and relevance select misleading evidence under different ownership patterns, while attribution tags and poor disclosure introduce additional failures beyond memory selection (§§5.2–5.5).

## Problem and motivation
An LLM team can collectively possess the evidence needed for a correct decision yet fail to communicate, preserve, or properly weigh it. Existing hidden-profile evaluations confound rarity with usefulness: private facts support the correct answer, while shared facts favor a competing option. Consequently, successful rarest-first memory selection does not establish that rarity is a reliable selection principle (§1).

The authors also distinguish criterion relevance from decision usefulness: a true, on-topic fact can favor the wrong option when separated from counterevidence.

## Approach and contributions
- **CrossProfile diagnostic benchmark:** 60 templates spanning 12 decision domains, expanded into 480 tasks by independently making support, decoy, and irrelevant facts shared or private. Fact text and answer keys remain fixed across ownership variants (§3.2).
- **Controlled memory comparisons:** Rarest-first selection (R1), relevance-first selection (R1-rel), first mention (B4), random selection, and model summaries (B3), with token budgets and oracle-relevance, fixed-item-count, and random-reference controls (§§3–4).
- **Failure-stage separation:** Holder-tag interventions test attribution effects independently of line order; natural HiddenBench discussions and prompt ablations examine evidence loss before compression (§§5.4–5.5).

These are primarily diagnostic contributions; the paper does not demonstrate a new memory policy that reliably preserves the correct evidence balance.

## Evidence and main results

Here, Qwen and Llama denote the two local instruct models. CrossProfile results use 60 templates, eight ownership variants, three seeds, and budget ratio \(r=.6\), unless specified. The **classic cell** has private support, shared decoys, and private irrelevant facts.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Rarity offers no aggregate advantage over random selection | CrossProfile accuracy: R1 versus Random is **64.0% vs 64.5%** on Qwen and **63.0% vs 63.4%** on Llama. | Table 2 |
| Decoy ownership strongly changes rarity’s advantage | Interaction in R1-minus-Random accuracy between shared versus private decoys: **+40.7 points [34.6, 47.1]** on Qwen and **+34.2 [27.8, 41.0]** on Llama; intervals are 95% template-bootstrap intervals. | §5.2; Table 11 |
| Relevance helps on average but fails in the classic cell | Mean R1-rel accuracy is **72.6% / 74.6%** for Qwen/Llama. In the classic cell, oracle relevance trails R1 by **23.3 / 31.1 points**, despite correctly identifying both support and decoys as relevant. | Table 2; §5.3; Table 11 |
| Retained evidence balance predicts decisions | Pooling copy policies, accuracy rises from **13.5% / 10.3%** at a keyed evidence lead of −2 to **96.2% / 96.1%** at +4 or more, for Qwen/Llama; policy coefficients are no longer distinguishable from zero after adjustment. | §5.3 |
| Attribution affects decisions independently of line order | Removing tags from all-facts classic-cell memories improves accuracy by **14.4 points [7.8, 21.1]**, **13.3 [6.7, 20.0]**, and **24.4 [17.2, 32.2]** on Qwen, Llama, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash. Randomly reassigning tags also helps the local models. | §5.4; Tables 11, 18 |
| Natural discussions fail before compression | On 65 HiddenBench tasks, full-transcript versus empty-memory accuracy is **22.1% vs 33.8%**, **33.8% vs 36.9%**, and **25.6% vs 35.9%** for Qwen, Llama, and Flash. | Table 3 |
| Evidence-first prompting has model-dependent benefits | In a fresh-seed paired ablation over 65 HiddenBench tasks, evidence-first wording raises Llama full-transcript accuracy by **25.1 points [15.9, 34.4]**; Qwen’s **+5.6 [−1.0, 12.3]** is inconclusive. | Appendix A.4, Table 14 |

The controlled interventions support the authors’ claim that neither rarity nor relevance reliably tracks useful evidence, and that holder attribution can change decisions. The evidence-balance analysis is an association within the constructed scoring system, rather than proof of a general reasoning mechanism. Natural-discussion results identify an upstream problem, but do not establish that every failure comes from omitted facts.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Constructed correctness:** Accuracy measures agreement with an additive key. The decision prompt requests equally weighted criteria but does not explicitly state the scoring rule (§3.2).
- **Restricted scope:** CrossProfile fixes group size, option count, and fact-role counts; the authors explicitly note that group-size effects are not varied (§2; §3.2).
- **Presentation coupling:** Ownership changes first appearance, tag content, and token cost. Random-reference, item-count, and tag controls address these separately, but removing tags leaves a residual ownership effect (§§3.2, 5.4; Appendix A.3).
- **Disclosure measurement:** Natural-discussion disclosure uses a string-matching heuristic for facts stated in full; Qwen and Llama disclosure estimates use one seed. **Analyst inference:** faithful paraphrases may therefore be missed (Table 3).
- **Exploratory evidence:** Controls, hosted-model runs, and prompt ablations are exploratory; original HiddenBench Llama results did not meet the authors’ lower-bound headroom criterion (§4). DeepSeek-V4-Pro covers only a template subset and excludes R1-rel (Appendix A.6).
- **Reproducibility:** Prompts and implementation details are supplied, but artifact release is promised upon publication rather than established in the text (Appendix A.7).

## Open research questions
- **Can selection preserve evidence balance without knowing the answer?** The paper diagnoses balance dependence but supplies no validated solution. A next experiment could compare option-wise evidence summaries against R1-rel at matched budgets, withholding evaluator roles.
- **Can models distinguish repeated testimony from independent evidence?** Assigned holder tags mislead here. A next experiment could cross actual source independence with explicit provenance descriptions.
- **What reliably improves disclosure across models?** Evidence-first prompting helps Llama but is inconclusive for Qwen. A next experiment could vary disclosure structure and discussion length while auditing semantic fact coverage.
- **Do the findings survive less artificial decision rules?** CrossProfile uses fixed additive keys. A next experiment could introduce unequal criterion weights and interacting facts, with independently verified answers.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported diagnostic findings within these settings; broader generalization remains uncertain.

Material information unavailable in the supplied text includes the complete template collection, executable verifier and analysis code, raw outputs, semantic validation of disclosure measurements, and evaluation across other group sizes or non-additive decision rules.
