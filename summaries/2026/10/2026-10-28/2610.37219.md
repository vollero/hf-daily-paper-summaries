# Vision-Language-Action or World-Action? Just Action Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.37219) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37219)


## One-sentence takeaway
JAM replaces visual-generation supervision with training-only prediction of frozen future representations, improving matched robot policies in simulation while preserving their deployment architecture and reducing estimated online training cost (§§4.2–4.5).

## Problem and motivation
Comparisons between vision-language-action and world-action models confound backbone initialization, future-observation supervision, and inference machinery. The authors ask whether robot policies can benefit from recorded futures without learning to generate images or videos, and whether that supervision also works with non-generative backbones (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Common training recipe:** JAM combines an action flow-matching loss with a cosine-distance foresight loss. A small transformer head maps current-observation tokens to frozen encodings of recorded futures; future observations never enter the action path. The head and anchor branch are discarded after training (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Controlled objective comparisons:** Matched FastWAM and ImageWAM experiments retain policy architecture, data, optimizer, training budget, and deployment path while replacing visual generation with foresight. Ablations examine target offsets, anchor initialization, and VAE-direct targets (§§4.1–4.2).
- **Cross-family applicability and efficiency:** The recipe supports generative, self-supervised, and vision-language backbones. Fixed targets can be cached, reducing online computation (§§3.3, 4.3, 4.5).

The contribution is this particular recipe and its controlled evaluation; future-representation supervision itself has substantial prior work acknowledged in §2.

LIBERO evaluates 40 tasks with 50 rollouts each. Local LIBERO-Plus comparisons share 1,506 perturbed tasks with one rollout each; RoboTwin evaluates 50 tasks with 100 trials per task and setting (§4.1; Appendices D.2–D.3).

These results support replacing visual generation in the tested policy-training settings. They do not establish that backbone pretraining families are equivalent, that foresight improves every perturbation category, or that the policies transfer safely to physical robots.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** All experiments use simulation. The ethics statement identifies distribution-shift failures and deployment without physical validation as risks.
- **Author-stated confounding:** Cross-backbone comparisons vary architecture and action interfaces, preventing isolation of pretraining effects (Table 3; §5). Random-backbone controls still retain pretrained action experts, VAEs, and text conditioners (Appendix D.1).
- **Cost-accounting boundary:** Estimates omit attention-score terms, text encoding, and optimizer updates. Cached costs exclude one-time extraction and require fixed anchors, preprocessing, temporal indices, and conditioning (Appendix B.2).
- **Comparison boundary:** Published benchmark rows retain their original protocols; local matched comparisons provide stronger evidence than overall leaderboard ordering (§4.4).
- **Analyst inference:** Robustness gains are selective rather than uniform: both generative architectures lose ground in one perturbation category, and action-only controls win some categories (Appendices C.5, D.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Attention maps and reconstruction probes support descriptive representation analyses, but do not establish the causal mechanism behind improved control (Figures 4–5; Appendices D.5–D.6).

## Open research questions
1. **What determines the best future-target representation?** Wan favors VAE-direct targets while Ovis-U1 favors pretrained features; the proposed temporal-information explanation remains tentative (§4.2). Next experiment: match temporal horizons and conditioning while crossing VAE, random, and pretrained anchors.
2. **Do the gains survive physical deployment?** Evaluation is entirely simulated. Next experiment: compare matched JAM and generative policies on physical manipulation tasks under controlled camera, lighting, and state perturbations.
3. **How much does pretraining family independently matter?** Architecture and action-interface differences confound the comparison (§4.3). Next experiment: compare differently pretrained backbones with matched architecture, capacity, action interface, and training budget.
4. **What is the end-to-end value of caching?** Reported savings exclude target extraction and depend on fixed cache settings (Appendix B.2). Next experiment: measure total training time, storage, and cache-generation cost across dataset sizes and training durations.
