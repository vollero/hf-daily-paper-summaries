# RSI Arena: Towards Recursive Self-Improvement with Human Feedback

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.33624) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33624)


## One-sentence takeaway
Ten agents autonomously post-trained useful assistants, and most improved their relative human ratings after further development with feedback, but the experiment neither isolates feedback’s effect nor demonstrates a closed recursive improvement loop (§§6–7).

## Problem and motivation
The authors ask whether agents can develop general-purpose assistants end to end and then improve them using users’ feedback. They identify a gap between short, automatically scored research tasks and sustained model development judged by actual users: optimizing a named benchmark does not establish that people find the resulting assistant useful (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Two-stage experimental setting:** Ten agents independently selected data, implemented training, and nominated checkpoints from the same NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 Lightning 30B-A3B Base. Stage 1 allowed six days, 1,000 GPU-hours and $300 API credit per agent; Stage 2 supplied each agent’s human comparison records plus three days, 500 GPU-hours and $150 (§§2, 6.1).
- **Complementary evaluation:** Twenty capability and safety benchmarks accompany blind human comparisons. Bradley–Terry ratings are reported with and without adjustment for answer length and formatting; NVIDIA Instruct, with reasoning disabled, anchors comparisons between separate arenas (§3).
- **Auditable empirical study and assets:** Recorded development trajectories expose training choices, engineering failures and feedback use. The authors report releasing submitted checkpoints and documenting trajectories for subsequent analysis (§§1, 7). The contribution is the experimental setting and findings rather than a new optimization algorithm.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Autonomous post-training was feasible | All ten agents produced working assistants; Astra’s Stage 1 model closed a median **76%** of the base-to-Instruct score gap over the **12 capability benchmarks** with reference gaps exceeding 10 points. | §§3.1, 5.1; Table 3 |
| Best human rating depended on style adjustment | From **795 accepted non-search votes**, Stage 1 adjusted ratings were Astra **1,095**, Instruct **1,094**; unadjusted ratings were **1,161** and **1,182**, respectively. Instruct used reasoning off. | §§3.3, 5.2; Figure 6 |
| Most relative preference ratings improved | In separate arenas linked through unchanged Instruct, **8/10** models improved their style-controlled relative rating; median gain **28 rating points**. DeepSeek’s gap changed from **−207 to −35**. | §6.4; Figure 11 |
| Unadjusted preference still favored Instruct | Stage 2 unadjusted ratings were Instruct **1,134**, DeepSeek **1,053**; Instruct led in **99.5% of bootstrap resamples**. Ratings used **1,096 accepted non-search votes**. | §6.4; Figure 11 |
| Benchmark improvements were modest overall | Across agents, median change was **+2 percentage points of base-to-Instruct gap closure**; Astra rose **76%→87%**. Correlation between benchmark-progress changes and relative adjusted-rating changes was **Spearman ρ=−0.19** across ten models. | §6.5; Figure 12 |
| Presentation affected observed preferences | Longer answers won **72% of 999 decisive Stage 1 votes** between answers of different lengths; this is observational. | §5.3 |
| Preference gains did not guarantee safer behavior | MiniMax gained **68 relative adjusted-rating points**, while its StrongREJECT harmful-compliance score increased **2.6→24.5**, where lower is safer. | §§6.4–6.5 |
| Compute use did not reliably rank outcomes | Across ten Stage 1 agents, GPU-hours correlated with adjusted human rating at **Spearman ρ=−0.21** and benchmark progress at **ρ=−0.01**. | §4.4 |

These results support feasibility under the reported platform and budgets. They do not establish that feedback caused the improvements, that adjusted-rating proximity proves equivalent quality, or that additional compute has no value. “Progress” measures normalized benchmark gap closure, not overall accuracy or a percentage of general intelligence.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** One base model and one run per agent limit reliability claims. The research agents remained unchanged while training smaller, separate models, so the recursive loop was not closed (§7).
- **Author-stated:** Stage 2 added resources and development time alongside feedback and used a separate arena; feedback’s causal contribution is unresolved (§7).
- **Author-stated:** Votes capture partial preferences, including presentation. Safety was absent from the task objective, and safety outcomes varied (§§5–7).
- **Analyst inference:** Recruitment at COLM and online leaves generalization to broader user populations uncertain; participant demographics are unavailable (§3.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Stage comparisons have additional measurement differences: style coefficients were fitted separately, questions differed, and Stage 2 benchmark generation used a larger initial token allowance (§§3.3, 6.3–6.5).
- **Reproducibility caveat:** Appendix K reports inaccurate model-card claims and a Stage 1 Grok repository containing an earlier nomination. Appendix I also reports shared-source benchmark-item overlaps despite the authors’ conclusion that no agent trained on a benchmark test set.

## Open research questions
- **What improvement comes specifically from human feedback?** Added resources confound Stage 2 (§7). Randomize matched continuation runs to genuine feedback, no feedback and shuffled votes.
- **Can improvements persist recursively?** Improved models never became the research agents (§7). Let successor models conduct subsequent development rounds, with independent capability and safety checks.
- **How much feedback is sufficient?** The authors leave feedback quantity and type unresolved (§7). Compare feedback budgets and targeted error annotations against preference votes alone.
- **Can explicit safety objectives preserve preference gains?** Safety was unspecified and some outcomes regressed (§§6–7). Compare matched development runs with and without safety constraints, measuring harmful compliance and benign refusals separately.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied narrative supports the central findings and caveats, but many referenced tables contain captions without their numerical bodies. Missing information includes complete benchmark scores and settings, exact rating intervals, full per-agent Stage 2 details, voter demographics, and complete executable training and evaluation artifacts. Checkpoint and trajectory availability is reported by the authors, not independently verified here.
