# T-DIG: Text-Conditioned Diversity for Image Generation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.29003) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29003)


## One-sentence takeaway
T-DIG trains an LLM to write prompts for frozen image models that increase variation along user-specified axes, with human-supported gains but imperfect preservation of prompt constraints (§§3, 5, Appendix A.2).

## Problem and motivation
Image generators typically offer several outputs, but generic diversity may not vary what a user actually cares about—such as clothing, viewpoint, or artistic style. The authors identify a gap between methods that maximize general visual differences or target fixed attributes and a system that accepts arbitrary, free-form requests for variation (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Conditional diversity task:** Given a base prompt and a textual axis, generate a set that varies along that axis while preserving the prompt. The authors claim this formulation extends beyond predefined diversity categories (§1).
- **Interactive framework:** Users inspect initial samples, choose or write an axis, adjust diversity strength, and generate or edit sets over multiple rounds. Strength scales the policy’s LoRA adapter (§2).
- **Policy training:** GRPO trains separate generation and editing policies to produce prompt sets for frozen renderers. The reward combines normalized TPIPS-Vendi diversity with baseline-relative alignment and preference penalties and a prompt-length penalty; editing adds a DreamSim drift penalty (§3.2).
- **Dataset and assets:** Training contains 76,089 prompt–axis pairs from 19,765 prompts; axes are VLM-generated using human examples. The interactive test set contains 405 user-written pairs from 58 participants. The authors report releasing code, data, and trained adapters (§§3.1, 4.4; Reproducibility Statement).

These are author-reported results. Automatic metrics and independent human ratings support improved requested-axis variation in the tested settings. They do not establish universal constraint preservation, uniformly better image quality, or superiority over alternative methods that also accept an axis: the listed diversity baselines do not receive one (§4.2).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Renderer capabilities limit control, particularly camera angles and framing; linear LoRA scaling produces poorly calibrated strength; optimizing TPIPS may inherit its biases (§8).
- **Observed failures:** Participants report unrequested changes, narrow variation, style/content confusion, and accumulating artifacts during repeated editing (Appendix A.2, Table 3). Continued training also produces reward hacking (§5, RQ2).
- **Analyst inference—comparison attribution:** T-DIG uses a shared latent across a set, whereas baselines use independent latents. Without a reported latent-control ablation, the separate effects of this choice and prompt adaptation remain unclear (§4.2).
- **Analyst inference—evaluation scope:** Main results emphasize four-image sets; the supplied text does not provide separate quantitative results for editing or repeated interaction rounds. The reward ablation covers one renderer (§§4.1, 5).
- **Safety boundary:** T-DIG adds no safety filters beyond those of the underlying image model; the authors acknowledge possible stereotyped depictions when varying sensitive attributes (Ethics Statement).

## Open research questions
- **Can control preserve every untouched constraint?** Participant reports show off-axis changes despite alignment penalties. A next experiment could score individual prompt constraints and compare targeted preservation rewards (Appendix A.2).
- **Can diversity strength become perceptually predictable?** Users found linear adapter scaling uneven. A next experiment could compare renderer-specific, human-calibrated mappings against linear scaling (§8).
- **How robust are gains beyond TPIPS optimization?** The authors acknowledge metric bias and reward hacking. A next experiment could evaluate unseen axes with human judgments and independent aspect-specific measures (§§5, 8).
- **Does editing remain useful over long sessions?** Participants report artifact accumulation. A next experiment could compare repeated editing with periodic regeneration, measuring preservation and quality after each round (Appendix A.2).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The method and narrative results are detailed, but the supplied retrieval omits numerical bodies of Tables 1–2 and the actual policy/filter prompt contents behind figure captions. Confidence intervals, full significance tests, final post-N/A rating counts, editing drift hyperparameters, and total training compute are unavailable.

Dataset accounting is also incompletely reconciled: Appendix A.1 gives 23,020 clustered prompts before the final training pool, while §3.1 describes roughly 20,000; the conclusion rounds the 405-request test set to 400. Asset availability is reported by the authors and was not independently verified.
