# MoveBench: A Rubric-Based Benchmark for Evaluating Large Language Models in Sports and Health Coaching

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.26185) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26185)


## One-sentence takeaway
MoveBench evaluates coaching knowledge and generated recommendations with domain-specific rubrics, finding greater differentiation on open-ended tasks than single-select questions, while judge disagreement and incomplete coverage limit ranking and safety conclusions (§§4–6).

## Problem and motivation
The authors identify a gap between answering sports-health questions correctly and producing safe, personalized, executable coaching advice. They argue that existing benchmarks inadequately assess exercise prescription, training progression, user constraints, and physiological safety—requirements whose failure could cause physical harm (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Coaching benchmark:** 809 questions with 5,012 rubric criteria covering personalized plan generation (PG), sport-data analysis (DA), and sports-health QA. Question formats comprise single-select, multi-select, and open-ended responses (§4).
- **Construction pipeline:** Gemini-3.0-pro generates questions using platform-derived user-profile distributions; DA incorporates real activity logs. Automated screening precedes expert approval, revision, or rejection (§§3.2–3.3).
- **Scoring framework:** Objective answers use exact matching. Open-ended answers receive criterion scores from Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude 4.6 Sonnet, and GPT-5.2, weighted 0.35/0.35/0.30. At least two safety-redline votes zero the item’s earned score (§3.4).
- **Evaluation and validation:** The authors compare 15 student models and examine judge alignment with an adjudicated expert reference, repeatability, and weight choices (§§3.5, 4).

The contribution is the domain-specific benchmark and evaluation pipeline; the text does not establish the authors’ broader “first comprehensive” novelty claim through an exhaustive comparison.

These results support evaluating generated coaching responses separately from objective answer selection. They establish performance under this rubric and scoring system, not effective interventions or real-world safety. Close rankings are uncertain because request coverage is incomplete, judging varies, and final-ensemble alignment remains unvalidated (§§4.3, 6).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Judge alignment with expert references is moderate, objective questions have limited discriminative power, and repeated judgments vary (§§4.3, 5).
- **Unresolved records:** One expert comparison lacks a classified criterion; Claude’s judge comparison leaves 20 criteria unclassified. Causes are unknown, and differing denominators prevent straightforward agreement comparisons (§§4.3.1–4.3.2).
- **Narrow validation:** Reference validation uses responses from one student model on a small open-ended subset. The authors explicitly call for final-ensemble agreement and independent validation (§§3.5.2, 4.3.4).
- **Analyst inference:** Platform-based profile sampling supports relevance to that platform but does not establish representativeness across other populations; subgroup performance is not supplied (§3.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Aggregate scores can conceal safety failures. Although the protocol calls for separate override rates, the supplied results do not report them (§3.4.3).
- **Reproducibility gaps:** The retrieved text contains table captions and explanatory prose but omits Tables 1–4’s full contents. Per-model request coverage, full student inference settings, and complete generation/judging prompts are unavailable.

## Open research questions
- **Does the ensemble improve expert alignment?** Individual-judge results do not establish the final committee’s benefit (§4.3.4). Compare individual judges and alternative ensembles on an independently adjudicated set spanning multiple student models.
- **How reliably does the redline rule detect unsafe advice?** The majority-vote mechanism is specified, but safety-detection performance is not reported (§3.4.3). Evaluate expert-labeled safe, unsafe, and borderline responses, measuring missed hazards and false alarms.
- **Are close model rankings stable?** Three judge-run summaries and incomplete coverage cannot establish ranking stability (§§4.3.3, 6). Repeat evaluation with fixed student responses and matched coverage, then separately vary response generation.
- **Do rubric scores predict coaching outcomes?** No user-outcome study is reported (§6). Test whether expert-supervised plan execution outcomes track benchmark scores.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly specifies the benchmark and aggregation rules, but supports weaker conclusions about exact rankings and safety.

Material missing information includes full reliability tables, per-model coverage, safety-override rates, subgroup results, complete prompts and inference settings, independent ensemble validation, and real-world coaching outcomes.
