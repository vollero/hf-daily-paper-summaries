# What Effort Level Should I Choose? On the Economics of Reasoning Effort in LLM Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.34491) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34491)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across the evaluated models and tasks, higher reasoning effort often improves aggregate accuracy but increases spending and does not preserve every observed lower-effort success, while externally verified low-to-high retries offer a useful, conditional alternative.

## Problem and motivation
Users choose reasoning effort before knowing whether extra computation will help; even success does not reveal whether that computation was necessary. The authors frame effort as a **credence good** and identify a gap in aggregate evaluations: they obscure task-level success reversals, unnecessary spending, and changes in agent behavior (§§1, 4).

This matters especially for agents, where effort can increase both computation per call and trajectory length, repeatedly charging for growing interaction histories.

## Approach and contributions
- **Task-level effort audit:** Compare eight proprietary and open-weight models across four benchmarks, holding tasks, prompts, and harnesses fixed within each configuration. Measure outcomes, complete execution token costs, and success-set overlap (§2).
- **Behavior and evaluation analysis:** Annotate trajectories using Act·ONOMY and construct SWE-Bench Pro-Corrected by removing repository history and restricting network access, exposing effort-dependent reference-solution retrieval (§3.3).
- **Allocation-policy comparison:** Evaluate self-routing, random routing, an outcome-informed oracle, and naïve laddering: start at the lowest reasoning-enabled setting and retry at the highest evaluated setting after externally verified failure (§3.4; Appendix H).
- **Broader cost accounting and robustness:** Add modeled user waiting costs and analyze repeated DeepSWE rollouts, including same-effort retry controls (Appendices F, H.1, J).

The main contribution is an empirical audit and allocation-policy evaluation, rather than a newly trained reasoning model.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Higher effort can lose observed successes. | On Terminal-Bench’s 89 tasks, GPT-5.6-sol high→xhigh gains 5 tasks and loses 8, reducing solved tasks from 76 to 73; one execution per task per setting. | §3.1; Figure 1 |
| Reversals persist with repeated attempts. | On DeepSWE’s 113 tasks with four rollouts per setting, Claude Sonnet 5 high→xhigh gains 7 tasks and loses 12; pass-at-least-once coverage falls from 90 to 85 tasks. | Appendix J.1 |
| Higher effort costs more on tasks already solved at every setting. | On original SWE-Bench Pro’s always-solved subset, GPT-5.6-sol low→xhigh raises reported mean API cost from $0.27 to $1.24, or 4.54×; mean calls increase from 14.7 to 28.4. | §3.2; Figure 2 |
| The cost finding survives environment correction. | Across all eight models on corrected SWE-Bench Pro, highest evaluated effort costs 1.83–25.59× the lowest reasoning-enabled setting on model-specific always-solved subsets containing 10–20 of 44 tasks. | Appendix G; Figure 11 |
| High effort increasingly retrieves exposed fixes. | GPT-5.6-sol reads reference-fix commits on 15.9% of original SWE-Bench Pro tasks at low versus 75.0% at xhigh. At xhigh, solve rate drops from 77.3% original to 59.1% corrected on the same 44 instances. | §3.3; Figure 3; Table 5 |
| More verification need not change outcomes. | On annotated corrected SWE-Bench Pro trajectories, all eight models increase mean verification counts both for tasks passed at both effort endpoints and tasks failed at both endpoints. | §3.3; Figure 4 |
| Laddering improves the recorded success–cost trade-off in many settings. | Against fixed highest evaluated effort, reconstructed laddering matches or improves success across the main model–benchmark configurations; median API savings across models are 25.3% on AIME, 41.5% on GPQA, and 15.5% on corrected SWE-Bench Pro, charging retries. | §§3.4; Appendix H |
| Retrying high effort remains more successful than laddering in the retry control. | Across five DeepSWE models, low→high beats low→low in estimated success and costs less than high→high, but high→high achieves higher success. | Appendix H.1; Table 6 |
| Waiting-cost advantages depend on explicit assumptions. | For GPT-5.6-terra on Terminal-Bench, laddering saves 4.8% in API cost versus fixed xhigh and 19.5% under the paper’s full-cost model. | §3.4; Equation 1; Appendix F |

These results establish observed task-level reversals and substantial conditional cost differences. They do **not** establish that higher effort systematically lowers any particular task’s success probability. Laddering’s preserved coverage follows from combining recorded initial and retry successes; it does not demonstrate that effort escalation outperforms an equally funded high-effort retry policy.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated sampling limitation:** Main agentic evaluations use one execution per task per effort. Reversals can reflect execution variability; repeated DeepSWE observations do not by themselves establish causal decreases in task-specific success probability (§3.1; Appendix J).
- **Restricted coverage:** SWE-Bench Pro uses a 44-instance pilot. Proprietary `max` settings are excluded for cost, so “highest effort” means highest **evaluated** effort (Appendices A–C).
- **Conditional cost interpretation — analyst inference:** Always-solved subsets are selected retrospectively and differ across models and environments. They demonstrate unnecessary spending on those observed subsets, rather than providing a deployable rule for recognizing easy tasks (§3.2; Appendix G).
- **Environment confounding — analyst inference:** Correction simultaneously removes git history and restricts networking. Its performance effect cannot be attributed exclusively to gold-fix access, although direct retrieval traces support leakage concerns (§3.3).
- **Feedback dependency:** Laddering is reconstructed from saved executions, assumes reliable external verdicts, and pays for fresh retries; many deployments lack such verification (Appendix H).
- **Modeled latency:** Waiting costs assume users remain blocked during decoding, use tabulated throughput and an hourly wage, and omit prompt processing, queues, and tool latency (Appendix F).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Terminal-Bench routing percentages in §3.4 differ from overall Table 4 values for some models. Figures specify matched policy cohorts, but the supplied text does not fully reconcile those denominators.

## Open research questions
1. **Can agents allocate effort during execution without external correctness labels?** Task-description self-routing offers no consistent advantage here. A next experiment could compare trajectory-conditioned escalation against fixed effort and laddering under matched budgets (§3.4; §5).
2. **How much benefit comes from escalation rather than retrying?** DeepSWE controls show high→high remains more successful. Test low→low, low→high, and high→high on the main benchmarks with repeated runs and equal dollar or latency budgets (Appendix H.1).
3. **Which verification actions actually improve correctness?** Additional verification appears in both successful and failed trajectories. Ablate specific verification behaviors and measure external correctness and complete execution cost (§3.3).
4. **Do cost rankings survive realistic user workflows?** Waiting costs are modeled rather than measured. Record end-to-end latency and user blocking time across synchronous and background-agent workflows, then recompute policy rankings (Appendix F).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the central empirical findings, but stronger conclusions about per-task reliability and deployment economics remain conditional.

Material gaps include exact pricing and throughput tables used, full laddering results for every configuration, reconciliation of routing cohorts with overall benchmark scores, independent validation of behavioral annotations, and confirmation of the linked repository’s released contents.
