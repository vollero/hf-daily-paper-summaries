# MLE-World: Predicting Machine Learning Experiment Outcomes with a Learned World Model

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.37175) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37175)


## One-sentence takeaway
MLE-World predicts ML programs’ execution feedback to guide agent search and selectively skip real runs, with promising efficiency gains demonstrated mainly on tasks included in its finetuning corpus (§§6–7).

## Problem and motivation
ML engineering agents repeatedly train and evaluate candidate programs, making execution the bottleneck in exploring alternatives. The authors identify a gap in approaches that prune or rank candidates: they do not supply predicted error traces and training logs that can guide debugging and revision before execution (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Execution-feedback model:** Qwen3.5-4B is supervised finetuned on 497,056 execution records, including failures. It receives code, package versions, a data overview, and a runtime limit, and predicts exception class, terminal output, validation metric, and submission existence (§4; §6.1).
- **Search using predicted feedback:** Five policies—Best-of-N, Sequential revision, Pairwise judging, Fusion, and UCB-style selection—use predictions to compare or refine candidates. The contribution is their integration with predicted execution feedback; the underlying search families have precedents (§5.1; Appendix J).
- **Confidence-guided execution:** Confidence comes from the maximum probability over exception-class tokens. Low-confidence selections receive real execution; new best predicted metrics require verification, and predicted timeouts are skipped. UCB uses outcome uncertainty as an exploration proxy, explicitly without a formal confidence-bound guarantee (§§4–5).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Better terminal-output prediction than prompted baselines | On 1,700 held-out programs from training tasks, judged agreement is 77.9% versus 41.1% for DeepSeek. Agreement is semantic rather than exact text matching; successful headline metrics allow a 15% gap under the judge specification. | §6.1; Table 1 caption; Appendix I |
| Lower prediction inference cost | GPU inference time, measured as wall clock × GPUs, is reported as 13–1,061× lower than prompted models. This compares prediction models, not prediction against real training. | §6.1; Figure 3 |
| Higher observed agent quality | Ungated UCB reaches mean leaderboard percentile 52.1 versus 45.4 for Base AIDE; main search evaluation uses 17 tasks, three seeds, and a 12-hour budget per trial. | §6.2; Table 2; Figure 1 |
| Predicted feedback helps Fusion locally | On 150 shared states, Fusion reaches mean percentile 20.6 with predicted feedback versus 14.0 with code alone; invalid outputs score zero. Appendix P reports a paired gain of 6.55 points. | §6.2; Table 3; Appendix P |
| Exploration bonus improves an isolated control | Changing only Improve-stage β from 0 to 1 yields mean percentile 45.4 versus 38.6 in eight-hour runs on 17 tasks × three matched seeds. Search lengths differ despite matched time budgets. | §6.2; Appendix K.1 |
| Gating saves real execution | Authors report 30–40% less real execution across five policies while broadly maintaining observed quality. | §6.3; Table 4 |
| Confidence beats random skipping on execution cost | In separate eight-hour BoN cohorts, confidence gating uses 4.49 versus 5.54 execution hours per trial, a reported 19% reduction; percentile difference is −0.15 ± 2.32 points SE. Each cohort has 51 trials with different seeds. | Appendix N |
| Exploratory transfer beyond training tasks | On 300 programs across three excluded tasks, terminal-output agreement is reported as 53.3% versus 42.1% for DeepSeek and 21.1% for Coder; failures dominate the sample. | §7; Appendix R |

These results support useful execution prediction and higher observed search performance in the tested setting. They do not establish universal superiority, equivalence between predicted and real feedback, or total compute savings including corpus generation and training. Several full numerical tables are absent from the supplied rendering, limiting assessment of uncertainty around headline comparisons.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope:** Main evaluation predicts new programs on tasks already seen during finetuning. Five Lite tasks are excluded because agents rarely progress or execution data are absent (§7; Appendix A).
- **Unequal task knowledge:** Prompted baselines and Base AIDE lack the finetuning corpus’s task knowledge. Corpus retrieval controls are tested, but Base AIDE with corpus access is not (§7).
- **Prediction failures:** Timeouts are hardest to predict; incorrect feedback can misdirect search. Unseen-task evidence mainly concerns failures rather than successful training scores or runtime prediction (§7; Appendix R).
- **Comparison qualifications:** Archived execution cohorts differ in revisions, serving, capacity, and concurrency; associated comparisons are descriptive. Fixed-state methods also differ in model-call costs (Appendices O–P).
- **Protocol ambiguity:** §6.3 describes UCB failure threshold 0.83, while Appendix M says the twelve-hour extension uses 0.56 and calls that comparison descriptive.
- **Analyst inference:** Confidence correlating with accuracy does not establish calibrated probabilities or reliable gating under distribution shift. Near-duplicate filtering also cannot eliminate semantic overlap (Figure 7; Appendix E).

## Open research questions
1. **Does prediction improve complete searches on unseen tasks?** Appendix R evaluates prediction only, mainly on failures. Run prospectively selected unseen-task searches with frozen models and gate thresholds.
2. **How much gain comes from learned simulation versus task knowledge?** Corpus-enabled Base AIDE remains untested. Compare it with MLE-World under matched information and total resource budgets.
3. **Can confidence remain reliable as search changes its candidate distribution?** Appendix M acknowledges that holdout confidence distributions differ from search. Measure calibration and gating errors throughout trajectories and across software environments.
4. **What causes Fusion’s longer-run advantage?** Local diagnostics and execution records do not isolate repeated synthesis causally. Run matched full-search interventions controlling synthesis, fallback rules, and model-call budgets (Appendix P).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the method and selected results, but omits the bodies of many tables and figures, including full main-result uncertainties, per-policy costs, detailed training configuration, and transfer breakdowns. Release availability is unconfirmed. Total amortized cost, corpus-enabled agent comparisons, and unseen-task end-to-end search results are unavailable.
