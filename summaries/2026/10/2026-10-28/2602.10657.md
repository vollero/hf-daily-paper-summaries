# The Lexical Imprint of Pre-training Data

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2602.10657) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10657)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors find that word-frequency alignment tracks base-model benchmark performance and supports target-aware data selection and drafter routing, while explicitly failing to capture several broader capabilities (§§4–7).

## Problem and motivation

Zero-shot benchmark scores may partly reflect how closely benchmark vocabulary distributions match pre-training data. Earlier lexical analyses used existing checkpoints, entangling corpus identity with model scale, training recipe, and history; the authors instead vary corpora under controlled from-scratch training (§1).

The practical question is how much benchmark performance reflects lexical familiarity alongside the capabilities a benchmark intends to measure (§§2, 7.1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Corpus-level measurement:** Use word-level unigram cross-entropy, \(H(Q,P)=-\sum_w Q(w)\log_2 P(w)\), between target word frequencies \(Q\) and corpus frequencies \(P\). For a fixed target, ranking by UCE equals ranking by marginal KL divergence. Computation uses normalized, punctuation-stripped, case-preserving whitespace words and zero-count smoothing (§3; Appendix A).
- **Controlled empirical analysis:** Compare corpus alignment and downstream scores across from-scratch training scales and random corpus subsets (§4).
- **Document-level intervention:** Introduce UCE-filter, which ranks documents by the analytic change in target UCE caused by removing each document. The authors position it as a controlled probe, rather than a standalone selection advance (§5.1). Appendix B acknowledges earlier removal-based unigram selection.
- **Prompt-level application:** Route each prompt to the specialist drafter whose corpus has the lowest prompt UCE, using precomputed word distributions (§6).

The evidence supports a useful lexical predictor and targeted interventions within the tested settings. It does **not** identify a universal fraction of capability explained by lexical alignment. Corpus comparisons remain associational, and document selection changes other document properties alongside UCE. The mathematical decomposition establishes marginal mismatch, not a causal law connecting UCE to accuracy.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ scope limits:** UCE ignores syntax, composition, and factual co-occurrence. It does not order performance on BLiMP, MathQA, or Global-PIQA (§7.3).
- **Known-target adaptation:** Selection uses benchmark-derived \(Q\), ordinarily pooling train and test splits. The authors interpret gains as an upper bound under a known target, not unseen-target generalization (§5.2).
- **Language and training boundaries:** Whitespace segmentation limits applicability to other scripts and morphologies; persistence through SFT or RLHF remains untested (§7.3).
- **Contamination remains partly unresolved:** The audit samples corpora and uses exact 13-gram matches. Authors acknowledge possible contributions on SciQ and MMLU; training-end removal covers only DCLM at 1.34B (§7.2). **Analyst inference:** these checks weaken the measured overlap explanation but cannot establish absence of every contamination channel.
- **Selection approximation:** One-shot document ranking ignores interactions among removed documents (§5.1).
- **Limited extrapolation:** The production experiment is a single case study; routing uses one verifier and a fixed specialist setup (§§5.3, 6).
- **Incomplete source:** The supplied source is truncated, and several tables contain captions without numerical bodies, limiting independent assessment.

## Open research questions

1. **Does alignment predict performance after meaning-preserving paraphrasing?** The authors leave lexical-realization dependence open (§7.3). Next experiment: paraphrase benchmark items, validate preserved answers, and compare changes in UCE and scores.
2. **Can selection transfer to genuinely unseen targets?** Current gains use known benchmark distributions (§5.2). Next experiment: select using separate domain text and evaluate on held-out tasks never used to construct \(Q\).
3. **Does post-training preserve the lexical relationship?** SFT and RLHF are outside the controlled study (§7.3). Next experiment: apply matched post-training recipes to corpus-controlled checkpoints and remeasure alignment–performance relationships.
4. **Can richer statistics address UCE’s failures?** UCE misses conditional structure (§7.3); a higher-order filtering ablation is referenced but its results are unavailable here. Next experiment: compare unigram and conditional metrics on grammar and arithmetic tasks under matched data budgets.
