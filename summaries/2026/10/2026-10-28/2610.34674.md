# Benchmarking Obstacle-Conditioned Safety in Household Robot Navigation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.34674) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34674)


## One-sentence takeaway
OSMA-Bench shows that evaluated household-navigation policies often fail to translate obstacle semantics into appropriately cautious motion, while a modular VLM intervention improves motion ordering but leaves clearance failures and task-success tradeoffs (§§4.2–4.4; Appendix F).

## Problem and motivation
Collision avoidance alone does not establish appropriate household navigation: robots should adjust clearance, speed, and smoothness to obstacle safety implications without being uniformly conservative. The authors identify a gap between evaluations of safety judgments or human-aware navigation and controlled tests of whether diverse obstacle semantics influence executed motion (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **OSMA-Bench:** A RoboCasa-based simulation benchmark with 625 scenarios combining 18 obstacles, seven routes, and five kitchen layouts. Matched comparisons fix spatial settings while changing obstacle identity across Living, Fragile, and Robust caution tiers (§3.1; Table 1).
- **Semantic Sensitivity Index (SSI):** A diagnostic measure of whether tier-average motion follows the expected caution ordering. It averages sign-aligned Kendall correlations across matched cells and seven features: minimum clearance plus mean/maximum proximity-normalized speed, acceleration, and jerk. Primary comparisons use common collision-free successful tasks within each policy group (§3.2.2).
- **Diagnosis and intervention:** The authors evaluate VLA policies and VLM policies instantiated through VoxPoser and PIVOT. Their three-step VoxPoser intervention infers clearance, then speed and its activation region conditioned on clearance, then execution code. It addresses both parameter ordering and whether requested speeds actually affect motion (§§4.1–4.4).

## Evidence and main results

Here, TSR measures goal completion; CFSR measures goal completion without obstacle contact; CFSR/TSR measures collision-free execution conditional on success.

|---|---|---|

Only 6 of 35 matched cells satisfy the VLA CFSR-I criterion. | §4.2; Figure 3; footnote 3 |
| VLM frameworks differ descriptively | PIVOT: CFSR/TSR 77.8–89.5%, SSI −0.140–0.132. VoxPoser: CFSR/TSR 94.9–99.5%, SSI 0.169–0.493. SSI subsets are shared within each group, rather than across groups. | §§3.2.2, 4.2; Figure 3 |
| Isolating parameter inference largely restores requested-value ordering, but does not ensure executed adaptation | For Qwen3.5-9B, isolated value SSI is 0.98 versus baseline 0.45. RQ2 uses one seed and a 569-task common collision-free subset for motion SSI. | §4.3; Table 2; footnote 4 |
| Three-step inference improves motion SSI with reduced task success | Qwen3.5-9B motion SSI rises from 0.38 to 0.58; inferred speed takes effect in 59.0% of tasks versus 0.0% at baseline. TSR falls from 99.5% to 96.5%, and CFSR from 98.6% to 95.7%. | §4.3; Table 2 |

| The strongest intervention depends on model size | On Qwen3.5-4B’s 217-task common subset, obstacle-specific prompting achieves SSI 0.59 versus 0.44 for three-step decomposition. Across all 625 tasks, their CFSRs are 72.6% and 84.3%, respectively, versus baseline 93.0%. | Appendix F.5; Table 18 |
| VLA rollout selection improves collision-free success without recovering semantic ordering | For π₀.₅-OpenTau, one seed, four candidates, and the 35B verifier, CFSR rises from 37.92% to 44.64%; paired-subset SSI falls from −0.059 to −0.204. | §4.4; Tables 3, 24 |

These results support the authors’ claim of a semantic-to-motion grounding gap in the evaluated systems. They also show that requested parameters, executed behavior, collision avoidance, and goal completion can diverge. They do not establish universal policy rankings, absolute safety, or a general causal explanation for all VLA failures.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Evaluation covers simulated navigation with static obstacles, compact open-weight models, and selected kitchen layouts. Physical robots, manipulation, frontier models, classical planners, and RL policies remain outside the evaluated coverage (Appendices A, B.1, C.3).
- **Author-stated metric limits:** SSI measures relative ordering of selected features, not universal safety requirements, approach direction, or legibility. Ordering alone does not measure adaptation magnitude; positive and negative feature correlations can cancel (§3.2.2; Appendices A, C.6).
- **Geometry confounds:** Obstacle footprints affect passage width. Table fixtures additionally dominate clearance for several Fragile objects, weakening semantic attribution of clearance differences (Appendices A, F.6).
- **Selection and replication:** Common-success subsets can be small and differ across groups or experiments. RQ2 and RQ3 use single seeds; style changes also alter outcomes (§§4.3–4.4; Table 4).
- **Analyst inference—intervention attribution:** Three-step inference also expands the permitted velocity-region radius. The supplied results therefore do not isolate decomposition from this parameter-range change (Appendix C.3).
- **Author-stated causal limits:** Rollout selection changes random-number handling, so its baseline comparison does not isolate selection. Backbone reconstruction adds decoder layers, and classifier accuracy does not demonstrate the original action head’s recognition or risk understanding (Appendices G.1–G.3).

## Open research questions
- **Can clearance adaptation survive geometry controls?** Fixture effects remain substantial. Test matched-size obstacles and fixture-aware planning costs while reporting clearance ordering and absolute margins (Appendix F.6).
- **Can caution improve without stalling?** Three-step failures concentrate in very slow High-tier executions. Test minimum-speed constraints or progress-aware control, measuring SSI alongside TSR, CFSR, and traversal time (Appendix F.7).
- **Does decomposition generalize across models and appearances?** The smaller-model ranking differs, and style changes affect outcomes. Repeat matched interventions across models, styles, and seeds with identical parameter bounds (Appendices B.1, F.5).
- **Can more diverse VLA candidates improve semantic adaptation?** Existing candidates mostly vary locally. Compare broader candidate generation under fixed candidate sets, separating generation quality from verifier selection quality (Appendix G.1.1).
