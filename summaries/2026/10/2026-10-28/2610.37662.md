# Can Discrete Tokens Serve as Semantically Lossless Representations of Visual Signals?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.37662) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37662)


## One-sentence takeaway

FVQ matches the average multimodal understanding performance of continuous QwenViT features in a controlled comparison, supporting task-level semantic fidelity without proving universally lossless visual representation (Table 2).

## Problem and motivation

Discrete visual tokens offer a shared interface for vision and language, but quantization error is commonly assumed to imply semantic loss. The authors question whether feature-reconstruction error predicts understanding and identify a scaling bottleneck: additional RVQ and PQ codebooks deliver diminishing gains despite increasing nominal capacity (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Reconstruction-error analysis:** Compare VQ with selected groups of RVQ codes to show that similar reconstruction errors can accompany different understanding scores, and larger errors can accompany better understanding (§2.1).
- **Factorized Vector Quantization:** Apply fixed, dense tight-frame projections to frozen vision features, quantize complementary views with independent parallel codebooks, and reconstruct through tight-frame synthesis. The projection–synthesis pair preserves features exactly **before quantization** (§3; Appendix A).
- **Controlled and industrial-scale evaluation:** Scale codebook count under shared training conditions, then train a larger model using Qwen3-8B and a frozen FVQ tokenizer. Discrete IDs receive newly initialized embeddings and a learnable merger (§4.1).

The controlled results support competitive benchmark understanding from discrete indices and undermine reconstruction MSE as a sufficient semantic-quality measure. They do not establish preservation of every image detail, universal question-answering equivalence, or pixel-level losslessness. The industrial-scale claims cannot be independently checked from the supplied table contents.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Understanding serves as a proxy for semantic fidelity (§1). Appendix C.3 explicitly describes its manually selected examples as illustrative rather than statistical evidence.
- **Analyst inference:** Matching an aggregate benchmark score cannot establish universal semantic losslessness; Table 2 also contains individual-task deficits.
- **Analyst inference:** The supplied experiments do not separately ablate dense projections, the tight-frame constraint, and codebook symmetry, leaving their individual contributions unresolved.
- **Analyst inference:** “Effective semantic capacity” is inferred from downstream scores; the text does not directly measure information contributed by each codebook (§2.2).
- **Comparison risk:** Table 6 mixes reported baseline results and includes a different language backbone for LLaVA-OneVision. Industrial training data composition is described broadly, limiting attribution and reproducibility (§4.1; Table 6).
- Numerical bodies of Tables 3–5 and 7–8 are missing, restricting assessment of flagship comparisons, encoder generalization, and implementation details.

## Open research questions

- **Which FVQ component drives improvement?** Component-specific evidence is absent. Next experiment: ablate tight-frame constraints, projection density, and symmetry at matched capacity and training cost.
- **Does scaling continue beyond the tested budget?** Table 2 stops at 16 codebooks. Next experiment: increase codebook count while measuring task scores, code utilization, memory, and latency.
- **How broadly does semantic fidelity hold?** Evaluation concerns image understanding rather than universal information preservation. Next experiment: test controlled fine-detail perturbations and out-of-distribution questions against the same continuous features.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** Tables 1–2 provide clear controlled evidence, but broader claims have incomplete supporting data.

Material missing information includes the numerical contents of Tables 3–5 and 7–8; run-to-run uncertainty; detailed data mixtures and overlap checks; concrete projection dimensions and several training hyperparameters; compute and inference costs; and verifiable code or checkpoint release information.
