# CodeCap-Bench: Can Code Serve as a Good Video Caption?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.37341) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37341)


## One-sentence takeaway
CodeCap-Bench shows that executable captions can preserve structured 2D video information better than the tested natural-language pipelines, while temporal fidelity remains difficult and agentic refinement provides inconsistent benefits (§4).

## Problem and motivation
Natural-language captions can become lengthy or ambiguous when specifying exact layouts, trajectories, and coordinated state changes. The authors investigate executable code as a complementary representation whose visual interpretation can be rendered and checked. They identify a gap in systematic evaluation across diverse structured 2D videos, beyond individual reconstruction domains (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Executable caption task:** Generate programs that reproduce a reference video’s elements, layout, and temporal evolution, without reusing reference frames or external visual assets (§3.1).
- **Benchmark and annotations:** Introduce 323 structured 2D videos across five categories, with human-reviewed static/dynamic focal points and object visibility intervals and bounding boxes. Mean video duration is 19.0 seconds (§§3.3–3.4).
- **Evaluation framework:** Compare reference and rendered videos using V-JEPA feature similarity, object-level spatio-temporal IoU, and focal-point retention scored by GPT-6-sol (§3.2).
- **Empirical analysis:** Evaluate seven MLLMs, agentic versus single-pass generation, thinking effort, cost, judge validity, and comparisons with natural-language captions (§4). The contribution is primarily a task formulation, benchmark, and evaluation study rather than a newly trained model.

These results support code captions’ utility under the tested conditions. They do not isolate representation quality from downstream processing: QA compares rendered video with text, and reconstruction compares deterministic code rendering with a particular video generator. Retention is defined on a 0–5 focal-point scale (§3.2), but the conversion to Table 1’s reported scale is not explicitly described.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-defined scope:** Filtering excludes complex 3D viewpoints, detailed textures, and dense pixel-level dynamics; findings therefore concern content selected for programmatic representation (§3.3).
- **Author-reported weakness:** Dynamic information remains less faithfully reproduced across all evaluated configurations (§4.2).
- **Analyst inference—comparison confounds:** Agentic and non-agentic settings differ in frame access as well as tools and feedback. Their comparison does not isolate the effect of iterative refinement (§4.1).
- **Analyst inference—limited judge validation:** Human agreement is tested on a subset, while cross-judge ranking consistency covers only two generation models. Object localization also depends on GPT-6-sol and SAM 3.1, without separate accuracy validation reported (§§3.2, 4.3.4).
- **Analyst inference—uncertainty and reproducibility:** No repeated-run variability or confidence intervals are reported. Appendix C names prompt sections but supplies no actual prompt bodies in the retrieved text.

## Open research questions
- **What causes agentic fidelity gains or losses?** Current comparisons change both observation and refinement. Next experiment: hold frames and compute budgets fixed while separately enabling tools and rendering feedback (§4.1).
- **Can temporal supervision close the static–dynamic gap?** The gap persists throughout Table 1. Next experiment: add explicit trajectory and event-timing feedback, then evaluate dynamic retention and object IoU.
- **How much of code’s advantage comes from the downstream pipeline?** QA modalities and reconstruction mechanisms differ (§4.3.6). Next experiment: match caption budgets and test multiple QA readers and text-to-video generators.
- **How robust are automated scores across model families?** Cross-judge validation is narrow (§4.3.4). Next experiment: expand blinded human assessment and independent judging across all evaluated models, including object alignment.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the central benchmark design and reported comparisons, but broader causal conclusions remain limited.

Material missing information includes full prompts, caption-length budgets, exact retention-score rescaling, repeated-run uncertainty, human inter-annotator agreement, complete sampling/preprocessing details, and confirmed downloadable dataset/code contents and licensing.
