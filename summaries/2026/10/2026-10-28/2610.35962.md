# h1: A Taste of the Bitter Lesson in Automated Theorem Proving

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.35962) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35962)


## One-sentence takeaway
The authors show that a persistent Lean proof agent with efficient tools and external validation can match or exceed several specialized workflows at comparable inference cost, while leaving proof strategy to the model.

## Problem and motivation
Formal theorem proving increasingly wraps language models in prescribed planning, decomposition, and multi-agent workflows. The authors ask whether those structures improve performance beyond what an efficient simple loop achieves with sufficient test-time compute. Existing comparisons often confound workflow changes with stronger models, larger budgets, or weak baselines (§1).

The practical motivation is mechanically verifying increasingly difficult mathematical arguments. The methodological gap is evaluating harnesses with matched models and explicit inference-cost accounting, rather than treating every system’s “attempt” as equivalent (§§1–2.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **h1 harness:** A model edits a persistent Lean file, receives compiler feedback, searches Mathlib, and tests fragments. An external validator checks submissions against the original statement. Failed submissions trigger reflection and context compaction before work continues until acceptance or a budget limit; the harness prescribes no proof decomposition (§2.2; Fig. 2).
- **Cost-aware comparisons:** The study compares solve–cost curves across competition mathematics, graduate-level benchmarks, and research conjectures, holding models fixed where possible (§§2.1, 3.1–3.2).
- **Depth versus repetition:** Repeated-run experiments test when extending one attempt is more efficient than launching additional attempts (§3.3; Appendix E.6).
- **Harness-design evidence:** Ablations examine tools, prompts, reflection intervals, and reasoning effort. These extend existing simple-loop ideas rather than introduce reflection or tool use themselves (§4; Appendices A, F).

These results establish that a simple, carefully engineered harness **can** achieve the reported coverage. They do not establish universal superiority over specialized workflows or that persistence always beats repetition. State-of-the-art claims use an **August 27, 2026 comparison cutoff**. Dollar figures are token-based API-equivalent estimates, not measured inference FLOPs or necessarily actual expenditure (Appendices C.2–C.3).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ limitations:** Proprietary training exposure is unknown; h1 handles a single file and Lean tools, leaving large multi-file formalization and richer environments untested (§5).
- **Statistical scope:** Many configurations have one run. These support capability claims more directly than stable expected performance rankings; selected ablations include uncertainty estimates (Appendix C.6).
- **Comparison uncertainty:** Cost conventions vary across systems. DeepSeek cache reuse is reconstructed; APN failed-group costs are unavailable, its full-system coordinates omit AlphaProof cost, and exact correspondence of reconstructed APN tasks is unconfirmed (Appendices C.3–C.5, E.6).
- **Subset qualification:** The headline APN cost comparison excludes Erdős 125, where every h1 replica encounters Lean timeouts. Across all six instances, terminal pass@1 success is **68.75% for h1 versus 71.67% for full APN**, without matched budgets (Table 15).
- **Configuration and statement changes:** DeepSeek’s FATE-X and FC100-solved runs accidentally omit reflection. Full PutnamBench completion combines the original campaign with corrected statements and additional attempts (Appendices E.3, C.4).
- **Analyst inference:** Improvements from newer models do not isolate a harness advantage, and kernel verification establishes the formal proposition rather than its correspondence to the intended mathematical question (§3.2; Appendix D.2).

## Open research questions
1. **Can h1 scale to multi-file formalization?** Its current scope excludes dependency management and codebase validation (§5). Compare freely coordinating agents with prescribed decomposition on the same multi-file tasks and budgets.
2. **When should an agent restart rather than persist?** Repetition wins at some larger budgets, and the trade-off varies by problem (§3.3; Fig. 14). Test an adaptive restart policy against fixed depth–breadth allocations, charging all failures.
3. **How robust are harness rankings across repeated runs?** Many reported configurations use one run (Appendix C.6). Repeat matched-model comparisons on fixed benchmark revisions with shared validation and cost accounting.
4. **Can improved Lean execution remove the Erdős 125 failure?** Timeouts prevent h1 from completing this instance (Appendix E.1). Rerun identical settings with a more robust interface to separate execution limits from proof-search limits.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported setup and qualified results; **medium** in general claims of workflow superiority.

Material unavailable information includes proprietary training exposure, repeated-run uncertainty for many comparisons, complete APN failure costs and confirmed initial task files, underlying inference FLOPs, and an explicit public release location for h1 code and full traces.
