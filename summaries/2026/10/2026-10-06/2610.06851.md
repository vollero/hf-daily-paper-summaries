# Base Models Can Reason By Taking a Cue From Training Data

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.06851) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06851)


## One-sentence takeaway
In the tested settings, short response prefills recover substantial base-model reasoning gains associated with RL, while controlled training-data edits show that learned associations can create and redirect these cue effects (§§2–3).

## Problem and motivation
The authors ask how little intervention is needed to elicit reasoning already available in a base language model, and how training data makes particular openings effective. This matters for separating capabilities a model possesses from behaviors it ordinarily generates: observing reasoning after RL does not establish that RL created the capability (§1).

The identified gap is an explanation linking minimal response openings, RL policy changes, and training-data associations.

## Approach and contributions
- **Cue discovery and elicitation:** Rank likely short openings with beam search, then select a fixed cue using agreement among sampled answers without reference labels. Prefill that cue across evaluation problems without updating model weights (§2.1).
- **Analysis of RL gains:** Compare base and RL models’ opening probabilities and conditional continuations; test cue-forced GRPO and a targeted weight edit that increases cue likelihood (§2.3; §D.5).
- **Causal data interventions:** Retrain matched checkpoints on original, renamed, and redirected training mixtures to create arbitrary reasoning cues and weaken existing ones. Extend the intervention to a prompt instruction (§3.1; App. F).
- **Behavioral and representational analysis:** Compare cue-induced hidden states with training-document states and examine cue position, refusal, and harmful responses (§§3.2–4). These similarity measurements are descriptive rather than causal attribution (§G.1).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Olmo cue exceeds its RL baseline on MATH-500 | Olmo-3-7B, RL-Zero prompt: pass@1 **42.2 ± 2.2% → 77.9 ± 1.5%** with `.\n\n Okay`; uncued RL counterpart **75.0 ± 1.5%**. Evaluation uses 32 responses/problem and a 31,744-token budget. | Tables 6–7; §§2.1–2.2 |
| Qwen cue approximately matches RL on MATH-500 | Qwen3-14B, boxed prompt: pass@1 **72.2 ± 1.3% → 86.6 ± 0.8%** with `␣Alright,`; uncued RL counterpart **86.8 ± 0.9%**, under the same evaluation protocol. | Tables 6–7 |
| Transfer to coding is mixed | HumanEval pass@1: Olmo-3-7B under RL-Zero improves **50.2% → 69.8%**; Qwen3-14B under boxed prompting decreases **86.3% → 82.6%**. Both use 32 responses/problem and the default 31,744-token budget. | Table 6; §2.1 |
| RL favors the discovered openings | In the MATH-500 analysis, cue probability rises **0.14 → 0.65** for Olmo and **0.04 → 0.58** for Qwen; next-token KL divergence peaks at the first two output positions given identical histories. | §2.3; Fig. 3 caption |
| Cue gains extend beyond longer generation | Olmo-3-7B’s cue beats no cue from a **1,000-token** truncation cap onward and reaches RL-level accuracy at roughly half the cap. Responses were generated first, then truncated and rescored rather than regenerated under each cap. | §2.4; Fig. 4; §E.1 |
| Data edits create and redirect a cue | After Olmo’s 10B-token mid-training, `.\n\n Chicken` MATH-500 pass@1 rises **2.4% → 37.2%** from base to rename mix; `Okay` scores **38.3%** on the rename mix and **0.2%** on the redirect mix. | An arbitrary instruction acquires a reasoning effect | With no response prefill, phrase editing raises MATH-500 pass@1 for “Think duck duck goose” **6.4% → 16.5%**; “Think step by step” changes **12.2% → 14.9%**. Olmo after 10B mid-training tokens, 32 responses/problem, 7,168-token budget. | §F.4; Table 24 caption |
| Formatting strongly changes safety behavior | On XSTest’s unsafe prompts, Olmo’s WildGuard-rated harmful-response rate is **42.4%** with `␣Okay,` versus **0.2%** with `.\n\n Okay,`. The evidence supports strong cue effects and a causal role for edited training associations in the studied models. It does **not** establish that all RL gains reduce to opening selection, that every model has an effective cue, or that similarity to reasoning traces guarantees correct reasoning. The supplied benchmark tables show heterogeneous gains.

Reported pass@1 error bars measure sampling variation across rollout indices, not variation across independently trained models (§B.1). Some figure references in the supplied prose conflict with caption numbering; section references and explicit captions provide the clearest anchors.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** The RL analysis concerns direct base-to-RL training without an intervening supervised stage; complex post-training pipelines may add capabilities beyond those elicited here (§6).
- **Author-stated model dependence:** No tested effective cue was found for Llama-3.1-8B. Data counterfactuals focus on Olmo and SmolLM3 mixtures containing synthetic reasoning traces (§6).
- **Search limitation:** Beam search can miss low-probability effective openings, and low answer entropy does not guarantee accuracy (§A.1). The GEPA comparison uses one run per model (§A.3).
- **Measurement limitation:** Hidden-state neighbor shares depend on a balanced reference bank rather than the actual training mixture and do not measure causal source contributions (§G.1). Phrase markers measure wording, not correctness (§G.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Safety conclusions remain conditional on XSTest and WildGuard grading, which includes the prefilled cue and lacks a partial-compliance category (§H.1). Olmo’s selective-refusal benefit does not transfer to the tested Qwen models (§H.3).
- **Coverage limitation:** The source is truncated, and many tables contain captions without numerical cells. Full experimental coverage and reproducibility cannot be assessed.

## Open research questions
- **What does multi-stage post-training add beyond cue elicitation?** §6 leaves this unresolved. A next experiment could compare matched base, supervised, and RL checkpoints with cue search and opening-swap controls.
- **How much do cue effects depend on synthetic-trace formatting?** The causal experiments use trace-containing mixtures (§6). A next experiment could vary trace share and opener diversity while holding training tokens and solution content fixed.
- **Do sustained representation shifts cause better reasoning?** Neighbor similarity is descriptive, and some reasoning-related shifts accompany poor accuracy (§§3.2, G.1). A next experiment could intervene on the identified states and measure correctness under matched token budgets.
- **When do reasoning cues improve safety?** The selective-refusal benefit is specific to Olmo among tested models (§H.3). A next experiment could repeat matched formatting interventions across models and prompt sets with independent human grading.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the central findings, but the source was truncated and this analysis does not imply complete coverage.

Material missing information includes the omitted middle content, numerical cells for many appendix tables, complete default sampling parameters, full RL training details, variation across independent training seeds, and verified contents of the linked code and model assets.
