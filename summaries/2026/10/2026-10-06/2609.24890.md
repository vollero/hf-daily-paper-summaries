# OSWorld-Pro: Process-based Evaluation for Computer Use Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.24890) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24890)


## One-sentence takeaway
OSWorld-Pro evaluates desktop agents through human-annotated, sequential subgoals, exposing failures and wasted actions that final-outcome scores miss, although its automated judge is substantially weaker at assessing feasibility and progress than completion.

## Problem and motivation
Final-deliverable evaluation can assign the same failure score to agents that stop immediately and agents that nearly finish a long workflow. It also provides little information about which actions fail or how efficiently agents proceed. The authors propose process evaluation to distinguish intermediate progress, diagnose action-specific weaknesses, and inspect how outcomes are reached (§1; Fig. 1).

The identified gap is evaluation of open-ended computer tasks with **dependent milestones**, rather than only final artifacts or loosely connected task chains (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Benchmark and annotations:** OSWorld-Pro contains 305 tasks, 2,814 progressive subgoals, and 67,264 human-annotated labels. Tasks cover uncommon applications, coordination across applications, and varied Linux desktop environments (§2).
- **Process-labeling framework:** LLM-generated subgoals undergo human correction and dependency checks. Annotators and independent reviewers label targeted subgoals, feasibility, observable progress, and completion at each trajectory step (§3; Appendix D).
- **Automated trajectory judge:** A single multimodal request includes the entire trajectory’s screenshots, reasoning, and actions. The authors compare GPT-5.6 judges against human annotations and select GPT-5.6-Sol Max for agent benchmarking (§4; Appendix F).
- **Diagnostic evaluation:** Beyond completing all feasible subgoals, the benchmark measures action-level progression and steps spent on successful, failed, or infeasible subgoals (§§5–6). Using these signals for training or harness improvement remains future work (§7).

The results support finer diagnosis than a final success flag and show substantial completion agreement between the selected judge and human labels. They do **not** establish equally reliable progress or feasibility judgments, causal explanations for model differences, or improvements from training on process signals. Reported OSWorld comparisons use different tasks, evaluation criteria, and turn limits, so they do not isolate a single source of increased difficulty (§5; Appendix G).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-recognized judgment ambiguity:** Feasibility can be confused with an undiscovered solution; progress can be ambiguous when an action advances functionality but introduces a bug. These fields show weaker judge agreement (§4.2).
- **Author-recognized infrastructure restriction:** Whole-trajectory requests can require approximately 500 MB of screenshots. Only GPT-5.6 models worked with the authors’ final setup; other tested providers encountered payload, image-count, or context errors (§4.1; Appendix F).
- **Analyst inference—restricted workflow validity:** Annotation and judge rules prohibit moving back to earlier subgoals. This may limit evaluation of legitimate recovery, reordering, or revisiting milestones (Appendices D.6.1 and F).
- **Analyst inference—uncertain rankings and diagnostics:** Agent benchmarking uses one run per model without reported confidence intervals. The judge’s weaker progress estimates also affect confidence in action-level diagnostics (Table 1; Appendix G).
- **Analyst inference—confounded explanations:** Model-size analysis uses API pricing as a proxy for undisclosed sizes, while open-weight comparisons mix architectures and training recipes. Suggestions of purposeful GUI training are hypotheses, not demonstrated causes (§§5–6).
- **Reproducibility gap:** Prompts, labeling rules, and broad inference settings are supplied, but a benchmark download, license, exact harness versions, and complete model-specific configurations are not established.

## Open research questions
1. **Can feasibility and progress judging become more reliable?** These remain the weakest judge fields (§4.2). Compare whole-trajectory judging with targeted environment checks against independently labeled ambiguous cases.
2. **Does milestone ordering distort evaluation of recovery?** The current rubric restricts backward targeting (Appendices D–F). Human-label trajectories that revisit completed steps and compare strict-order scoring with a dependency-graph alternative.
3. **Do process signals improve agents?** Reinforcement learning and harness optimization are proposed but untested (§7). Compare matched agents trained with outcome-only versus process rewards on held-out tasks.
4. **How stable are model rankings?** Single-run benchmarking leaves uncertainty unquantified (Appendix G). Repeat agent runs and audit a human-scored subset to measure ranking sensitivity to both execution variability and judge error.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** Benchmark construction and reported results are well documented, but important validity questions remain.

Unavailable information includes public asset access and licensing, annotation-trajectory generating models, judge-development versus held-out evaluation splits, exact harness versions, repeated-run agent uncertainty, and human audits of benchmark model rankings. There is also an internal reporting inconsistency: the scaling discussion assigns 54.9 steps to GPT-5.6-Sol, whereas Table 2 reports 53.3 for Sol and 54.9 for Terra.
