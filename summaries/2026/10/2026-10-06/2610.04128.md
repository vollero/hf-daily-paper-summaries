# What Gradients Add to Text Leakage in Split Language Models, Counted per Token and per Document

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.04128) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04128)


## One-sentence takeaway
On held-out 32-token WikiText-2 passages, adding gradients to a matched activation-based GPT-2 attacker increases token recovery by 3.17 percentage points but raises exact-document recovery 2.75-fold, showing why privacy evaluations need both metrics (§§4.2–4.3).

## Problem and motivation
Split learning keeps raw text on the client but exposes token activations and, during training, returned gradients. Prior attacks establish that both signals can reveal text; the authors identify a narrower unanswered question: how much gradients add when activations already leak heavily, and how the answer changes with the scoring unit (§1).

A second question concerns partial offloading: whether the starting layer of a server-trained segment affects leakage and model quality beyond the segment’s length (§5).

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled gradient comparison:** Both attackers fit a ridge map from activations to token embeddings using public calibration documents, then reconstruct text with beam search. The joint attacker replaces half its activation-derived candidates with gradient-derived candidates while retaining the same candidate budget (§3.1).
- **Multiple leakage measures:** Token, exact-document, and annotated-span recovery expose different residual risks under secret mixup and replica-median (§§3, 4.5).
- **Placement study:** A factorial experiment varies server-segment start, length, and communication defence on GPT-2 and Qwen3-0.6B, measuring excess held-out loss and a combined reconstruction/property-leakage index (§5).
- **Reproduction assets:** The authors report releasing frozen code, exact inputs, protocols, predictions, logs, and analyses under dataset tag `v1.0` (Appendix A).

The main novelty is controlled measurement and evaluation design; combining activations and gradients is acknowledged as prior work, and the joint attacker does not reproduce BiSR’s optimisation (§2).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Gradients improve token recovery over an already strong baseline | GPT-2, split depth 6, public client weights, candidate budget 32: 94.20% activation-only versus 97.38% joint; paired gain **3.17 points**, 95% crossed-bootstrap interval **[2.72, 3.64]**, over 10 victim seeds and 256 evaluation documents, averaging two attack seeds. | §§3, 4.2 |
| Exact-document improvement is substantially larger | Same setting: **13.71% → 37.77%** exact recovery of 32-token documents, a **2.75-fold** increase; descriptive result. | §4.3; Table 3; Figure 4 |
| Corrupting observed gradients retains much of their contribution | Same main setting: token recovery **96.83%** with Gaussian noise at gradient-RMS scale 1.0 and **96.38%** with 4-bit rounding, versus **97.38%** with true gradients and **94.20%** activation-only. Training is unchanged. | §4.4; Figure 5 |
| Secret mixup separates token leakage from exact-document leakage | Across tested defended conditions: **83.01–90.67%** token recovery, but at most **1.48%** exact-document recovery; held-out perplexity rises **31.8%** at depth 3 and **26.9%** at depth 6 versus undefended final models. | §4.5; Figure 6 |
| Sensitive-span protection is uneven | Secret mixup: exact name recovery **47.85–66.15%**, number recovery **93.94–96.50%**, identifier recovery **90–100%**, across the same 24 attack/depth/weight-copy conditions; identifiers comprise only two annotated spans. | §4.5; Table 4 |
| Segment start explains outcomes beyond segment length | GPT-2 passes **3/4** utility/leakage tests; Qwen3 passes **4/4**, each with partial R² ≥ **0.90** and permutation p = **0.0001**. Tests include start–length interactions and use eight victim seeds under two defences. | §§5.1–5.2; Table 5 |
| Explanatory effects do not consistently predict new seeds | Adding start meets the specified **10%** held-out RMSE-reduction criterion for both outcomes and models under 8-bit protection, but neither model meets it under 4-bit noisy protection. | §§5.1–5.2 |

These results support the authors’ claim that exact-document recovery alone can obscure substantial residual leakage. They establish measured attack capability within the tested settings, rather than general privacy guarantees. Gradient perturbation and document/span results are descriptive; the authors did not test equivalence under perturbation (§4).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope:** Two small models and short WikiText-2 windows do not establish behaviour for larger models, longer documents, or other text domains (§7).
- **Authors’ stated attack limits:** Defended models receive the existing attacks rather than defence-specific attacks. Partial-offloading leakage uses a ridge decoder and one attack seed, not the published optimisation-based inversion (§7).
- **Analyst inference—placement confounding:** For starts above one, changing segment start also changes the observation boundary, so the experiment does not isolate trained-layer placement from observation depth (§5).
- **Analyst inference—statistical scope:** The main bootstrap holds the training set fixed; inference evaluations reuse training-setting measurements rather than independent attack runs (§3).
- **Metric caveats:** First-token spacing errors strongly constrain exact-document recovery; the leakage index can be positive from finite-sample noise; identifier evidence is sparse (§§4.3, 4.5, 5).
- **Execution scope:** Qwen3 required substantial FP64 computation after an FP32 failure, limiting evidence for ordinary FP32 execution (§5.2).

## Open research questions
- **Does the gradient increment persist on longer or different text?** The evaluation uses short WikiText-2 windows (§7). Repeat the matched-budget comparison across document lengths and domains.
- **How much stronger are defence-aware attacks?** Current defended results use existing attacks (§7). Compare reconstruction explicitly modelling mixup or quantisation against the present attacks at matched budgets.
- **Is placement or observation depth driving the effect?** Both change together for some starts (§5). Vary trained-segment placement while holding the observation boundary fixed.
- **Why do noisy-defence effects generalise poorly across seeds?** Significant explanatory effects do not yield consistent held-out prediction (§§5.1–5.2). Test frozen placement predictors on additional seeds and separate reconstruction from property-leakage outcomes.
