# Agentic discovery of blood biomarker from distilled private health records

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.04749) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04749)


## One-sentence takeaway
A released graph-attention scorer distilled from private clinical evidence improves agent-generated CBC biomarker prioritization, but its external benefits depend strongly on the cohort and do not establish clinical readiness.

## Problem and motivation
Routine complete blood counts (CBCs) offer inexpensive, interpretable biomarker inputs, but their arithmetic expression space is too large to enumerate manually. Frontier language-model agents can propose expressions from literature yet often cannot access protected electronic health records to evaluate them. The authors address the missing portable artifact that could rank new hypotheses using private clinical evidence without querying patient records (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Private-evidence distillation:** Inside Clalit, approximately two million synthetic expressions per disease receive cohort-level, direction-agnostic AUC labels. Expressions combine 14 CBC features with addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division, nominally restricted to depth three without numeric constants (§§4.2–4.3).
- **Portable expression scorer:** Disease-specific graph-attention networks predict AUC from expression structure using combined regression and listwise-ranking losses. Only weights and non-patient metadata are exported; an MCP/Python interface scores expressions without patient inputs (§§4.4–4.5).
- **Agent integration and evaluation:** Predicted scores guide a propose–score–refine loop and rerank independently generated tool candidates. External AUCs are computed after selection and withheld from the discovery agent. The authors report releasing checkpoints, scoring code, and discovery/explanation skills ( §§4.6, 4.10; Code availability).

## Evidence and main results

AUC percentage points are abbreviated **AUC pp**.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong expression-ranking fidelity | Held-out synthetic-expression split across 13 diseases: median Spearman correlation ≈0.93, range 0.80–0.99; maximum AUC MAE 0.0186; NDCG@5000 0.984–0.999. | §2.1; Table 2 |
| Guided search improves over starting ratios | Post-hoc primary-cohort AUC gain: median +4.18 AUC pp across 13 diseases; +4.08 across 12 after excluding unstable FMF. Primary cohort is MIMIC-IV except FMF on EHRShot. | §2.5; Tables 6–7 |
| Transfer is uneven | Discovered expressions matched/exceeded starting point AUC in 12/12 MIMIC-IV cells, but exceeded it in 7/12 EHRShot cells; only 6/12 and 1/12, respectively, passed the authors’ CI-lower-bound criterion. NHANES RA improved 0.522→0.640; psoriasis declined 0.592→0.570. | §2.2; Table 3; Figure 3 |
| Reranking beats first-emitted candidates overall | Across 81 primary disease–cohort–tool comparisons: 55/81 wins, 67.9% [57.1%, 77.1%]; median paired gain +1.50 AUC pp; Wilcoxon p=4.0×10⁻⁵. | §2.3; §4.10 |
| Reranking advantage varies by cohort | MIMIC-IV: 86.1% wins across 36 comparisons, median +3.67 AUC pp. EHRShot: 48.7% across 39, median 0.00, p=0.92. Verified-diagnosis NHANES: 83.3% across six, median +3.83, p=0.063. | §2.3 |
| Random feedback largely removes search gains | Excluding FMF, mean within-arm gain falls from +5.95 to +0.13 AUC pp; median from +4.08 to −0.51. Starting expressions differ between arms in eight diseases. | §2.6; Table 8; §4.8 |
| Stronger comparisons temper discovery claims | Iterative discoveries fall below the strongest pooled independent-tool candidate in all ten runs with available comparators. Separately, scorer-ranked candidates exceed the best single feature in all 13 diseases on the development split, mean +2.83 AUC pp, with selection on that same split. | §§3, 4.7; §2.4; Table 5 |
| Screening enrichment and biological agreement are incomplete | At the top 1% screening threshold, 15/27 external cells exceed random screening and nine show no enrichment. Literature audit labels 41/64 feature contributions expected, 21 surprising, and two unclear. | §2.2; Table 4; §2.7; Table 9 |

The evidence supports learned ranking and hypothesis prioritization, especially on MIMIC-IV. It does not establish uniform transfer, superiority to the best available tool-generated candidate, deployable screening performance, or causal biological discovery. Held-out expression fidelity measures reproduction of Clalit-derived labels, rather than external clinical validity.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated limitations:** Five selected expressions exceed the training depth bound, so their predicted scores are unvalidated extrapolations. Early stopping was also applied inconsistently (§4.6).
- **Comparison limitations:** First-emitted candidates are weaker comparators than tools’ best candidates. Development results select maxima on the reporting split from unequal candidate pools; the random-control arms use different starting expressions (§§4.8, 4.10–4.11).
- **Population and label limitations:** EHRShot has few positive cases; FMF estimates rely on three positives. NHANES coverage is restricted, with coarse self-report phenotypes separated into sensitivity analyses. Prior tool exposure to evaluation cohorts was not tested (§§2.3, 4.2).
- **Interpretation limitations:** Age, medication, and acute illness may confound CBC signals. The attribution logistic link is fitted in-sample on MIMIC-IV, and literature concordance does not establish causation (§4.12).
- **Analyst inference—privacy evidence:** The architecture establishes that inference requires no patient-row access. The stronger authors’ claim that released weights cannot reveal sensitive information rests on aggregate-label reasoning; no formal privacy guarantee or empirical attack audit is reported (§4.13).
- **Analyst inference—attribution consistency:** Supplementary descriptions call Shapley results “out-of-sample,” while Methods disclose the in-sample logistic fit. The supplied text does not fully reconcile that terminology (§4.12; Supplementary Data 1).

## Open research questions
1. **Does reranking help larger ambulatory populations?** EHRShot shows no detectable advantage and has limited positive counts. Next experiment: evaluate frozen scorers and candidates in a larger ambulatory cohort with harmonized diagnoses.
2. **How much gain survives strictly controlled search?** Depth enforcement, stopping, and starting seeds vary. Next experiment: repeat both scoring arms with identical fixed seeds, enforced grammar, matched budgets, and multiple runs.
3. **Do expressions outperform simple markers prospectively?** Single-feature comparisons use selection on the reporting split. Next experiment: freeze expressions and feature baselines before evaluation on an untouched cohort.
4. **What privacy assurance does aggregate-label release provide?** The paper offers a design argument without a formal guarantee or attack evaluation. Next experiment: specify an adversary and test sensitive-information inference from repeated scorer queries and released weights.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied methods and narrative support the main conclusions, but most numbered tables contain captions without their numerical bodies.

Material unavailable information includes complete per-disease results and confidence intervals, detailed training optimizer/batch/epoch settings, repeated-run variability, full external preprocessing and arithmetic edge-case handling, and empirical privacy-audit results. The reported software release was not independently inspected.
