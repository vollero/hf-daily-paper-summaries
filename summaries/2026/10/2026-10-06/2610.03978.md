# Learning Latent Protein Languages for Autoregressive Generation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.03978) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03978)


## One-sentence takeaway
Learned protein token languages improve fitted downstream autoregressive scaling and reduce low-complexity sequence generation, while supporting diverse backbone generation and confidence-based structure selection without establishing superior biological quality (§§4.2, 5).

## Problem and motivation
The authors investigate whether target representations contribute to autoregressive models’ weaknesses in protein sequence and backbone generation. Amino acid tokens specify residue identity without explicit contextual semantics, while backbone coordinates need discretization in their framework. Their proposed gap is a decodable protein representation that supports both biological information and next-token prediction (§§1, 3).

## Approach and contributions
- **PLL sequence language:** Quantize frozen ESM-2 representations into a contextual, residue-aligned alphabet. A two-stage tokenizer first reconstructs encoder representations, then freezes the codes and learns amino acid recovery (§3.1).
- **SLL structure language:** Adapt GCP-VQVAE Lite with residue-identity, confidence, and ESM-2 representation supervision, plus revised reconstruction losses. Codes remain decodable to backbone coordinates (§3.2; Appendix A.3).
- **Autoregressive evaluation:** Train separate PLLM and SLLM transformers; compare PLLM with amino acid modeling under matched downstream sequence training, and evaluate SLLM backbone generation (§§3.3, 4.2).
- **Structure candidate selection:** Condition SLLM on E1 sequence representations and select sampled trajectories using internal token entropy followed by token-space consensus, before coordinate decoding (§3.3; Appendix A.6).

## Evidence and main results

All results below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| PLL preserves residue identities | Fixed PLL codes achieve **99.81% micro residue recovery** on UniRef50 validation; every standard amino acid exceeds **99.17%**. | §4.1.1 |
| PLLM has steeper fitted scaling | Under matched UniRef50 sequences, token budget, and optimization, fitted validation-loss exponents are **0.038 PLLM vs 0.020 amino acid PLM**, approximately **1.9×**. SLLM’s **0.049** uses different structure data/exposure. | §4.2.1; Fig. 5; Appendix A.7 |
| PLLM reduces unconditional composition collapse | Across twelve temperatures, entropy below **1.5 bits** occurs in **2,383/12,000 PLLM samples (19.9%)**, versus **5,154/12,000 PLM samples (43.0%)**: approximately **54% relative reduction**. Both models are **1.3B**. | §4.2.2; Appendix C.3.1 |
| Better composition does not imply better folding confidence | At **T=0.5**, with **50 entropy-filtered sequences per length**, averaged equally over lengths **100, 200, 300, 500**, PLM has higher ESMFold mean pLDDT (**65.26 vs 59.86**); PLLM has higher cluster ratio (**0.9700 vs 0.9250**). | §4.2.2; Table 4 caption; Appendix A.8 |
| SLL improves supervised representation utility | Full-run PST category averages for SLL versus original Lite are **71.32 vs 61.84 functional-site AUROC (%)**, and **29.63 vs 20.84 physicochemical Spearman ρ (%)**, using the main probe columns. | Table 18 |
| SLL improves target predictability | Changing only the target tokenizer in a matched sequence-to-structure experiment lowers best validation perplexity by **34%**; comparable training loss is reached **49% faster by epoch**. | §4.1.2; Fig. 4; Appendix C.2.5 |
| Backbone diversity comes with a quality tradeoff | Across ProteinBench target lengths **50, 100, 300, 500**, SLLM reportedly has the highest cluster ratio and lowest PDB Max TM among generators with mean **scTM >0.5**; La-Proteina leads self-consistency quality. Numerical table cells are unavailable. | §4.2.3; Table 5 caption |
| Confidence selection improves held-out prediction | On **574 CAMEO-2024 targets**, the CASP-tuned selector at **k=256** raises average TM-score from **0.725 single-sample to 0.754**; oracle best-of-256 reaches **0.825**. Sampling uses **T=1.0, top-p=0.90**. | Appendix C.3.4; Fig. 17 |
| Candidate generation is fast in the reported setup | Approximately **1,400 tokens/s** on **two RTX 6000 Ada GPUs**; long-protein candidate-token generation is reportedly about **1,000× faster** than MSA-based AlphaFold2 on **one A100**. | Appendix C.3.4 |

The controlled comparisons support the complete PLL representation and SLL tokenizer recipe. They do not isolate which PLL component causes the gains, establish biological function, or demonstrate quality-equivalent speed against AlphaFold2. Oracle selection uses native structures and is only an evaluation ceiling.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated limits:** Backbone self-consistency quality trails specialized generators; joint PLL–SLL generation, unified multimodal pretraining, and multichain relative poses remain untested (§5).
- **Attribution and resources:** PLL lacks comparisons against continuous-target generation or similarly resourced ESM-2 fine-tuning/distillation. Scaling compute excludes encoder pretraining, tokenizer learning, and preprocessing; fit confidence intervals and additive-loss sensitivity are absent (§§3, 5; Appendix A.7).
- **Proxy evaluation:** Composition entropy ignores residue order and function. pLDDT is predicted confidence, not experimental validation. Filtered folding results describe a different population from unconditional drift diagnostics (Appendix A.8).
- **Comparison limits:** Backbone baselines have unmatched training data, sizes, and modalities; published starred rows use pairwise TM rather than the nearest-neighbor TM used elsewhere. Sequence evaluations lack uncertainty across independent repeats (§4.2.3; Appendices A.8–A.9).
- **Analyst inference:** The heterogeneous hardware and incompletely specified AlphaFold2 timing scope prevent interpreting the speed claim as a controlled end-to-end comparison. Missing table cells and inconsistent sequence-table references hinder auditing; Appendix C.2.1’s inverse-folding weight also differs from the final objective in Appendix A.3.

## Open research questions
- **Which PLL ingredients drive improvement?** The complete representation comparison leaves semantics, quantization, and vocabulary effects entangled. Next experiment: factorial ablations with matched total resource accounting (§3).
- **Do reduced drift and increased diversity improve biological usefulness?** Current evidence relies on computational proxies. Next experiment: experimentally test matched generated sets for folding and function (Appendix A.8).
- **Can selection recover more candidate-pool quality?** Held-out confidence selection remains below the oracle ceiling. Next experiment: compare alternative selectors on a new held-out set at equal sampling budgets (Appendix C.3.4).
- **Can independently learned languages support joint generation?** Compatibility is untested. Next experiment: jointly train PLL–SLL generation and evaluate sequence–backbone consistency (§5).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** Main findings and several controls are explicit, but most tables contain captions without cells and figures lack readable plotted values. Missing material includes complete architecture/hyperparameter tables, most generation baseline scores, statistical uncertainty, detailed AlphaFold2 timing conditions, and verified checkpoint availability. No experimental biological validation is reported.
