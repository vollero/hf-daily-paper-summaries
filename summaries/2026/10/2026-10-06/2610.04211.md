# CurveCodec 2: Skeleton-agnostic animation compression with a learned entropy model

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.04211) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04211)


## One-sentence takeaway
The authors report that hierarchy-aware quantization and sparse keys, combined with learned residual entropy coding, reduce skeletal-animation storage to 0.37× ACL’s bytes at 0.01 cm under an ACL-relative worst-case contract, while requiring whole-clip decoding (§6.2; §7).

## Problem and motivation
Skeletal-animation libraries must fit memory and streaming budgets while avoiding visible errors on individual joints. The authors identify a gap between ACL’s fast, independently coded samples and learned motion representations that depend on training skeletons or miss production precision. Their previous CurveCodec also lacked worst-case error matching, actual entropy-coded byte accounting, and CPU decoding (§1).

The central question is which motion structure saves bits under verified error constraints, and where learning contributes enough to justify its cost.

## Approach and contributions
- **Hierarchy-aware codec:** Represent rotations as log-map curves and translations in centimetres; choose quantization steps and retained keys by decoding candidates and checking object-space errors throughout the affected subtree. Reconstruct omitted samples with fixed Catmull–Rom interpolation (§§4.1–4.3).
- **Learned lossless back end:** Predict quantized integers from each curve’s past, then use an MLP or causal transformer to model residual probabilities for rANS coding. Integer probability inference is bit-exact; learning changes stream size rather than reconstruction error (§4.4).
- **Verified delivery contracts:** Check either ACL-relative per-joint worst-case limits or clip-mean limits with additional guards. Re-encode failures with margins or store ACL’s stream; reported sizes include fallback (§3.3).
- **Empirical diagnosis:** Measure ACL’s bit budget and test prediction, sample omission, interpolation, and alternative learned roles. The authors find temporal prediction and hierarchy-aware sparsity provide the largest gains, with learning most useful for residual distributions (§5; §6.5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Substantial storage reduction at default precision | On 4,472 test clips, at 0.01 cm: 6.1 versus ACL’s 16.4 bits per joint-sample; 0.37× bytes under either contract, including fallback. Primary ACL comparison disables keyframe stripping. | §6.2; Table 2; Fig. 7 |
| Gains survive default ACL stripping | Relative to ACL with stripping enabled: 0.380× bytes at 0.01 cm and 0.25–0.26× at 0.1 cm on the test side. | §6.2 |
| Contracts produce different error distributions | At 0.01 cm, mean-gate maximum shell error is 0.094 versus ACL’s 0.213 cm; max-gate mean error is reported as 1.4× ACL’s. | §6.2; Table 2 |
| Learned entropy modeling contributes incremental savings | On a 311-clip subset spanning all datasets, removing the entropy model increases bytes by 3–6% under the max gate and 7–10% under the mean gate across tested precisions. Ablations do not hold distortion strictly equal. | §6.5; Table 4 |
| Tested interpolation alternatives offer little benefit | Under the iso-mean contract, the retrieval oracle achieves cubic-error/oracle-error ratios of 0.85–0.90 on encoder-selected gaps; four learned in-betweeners change codec size by −2.5% to +5.7%. | §5.3; Fig. 6 |
| Transfer to an unseen species is demonstrated | On held-out dog motion, the shared entropy model saves 5.7–6.9% over the codec without a network, achieving 91–95% of the savings of a dog-trained model. | §6.4 |
| Storage savings carry a decoding cost | Per million joint-samples on one pinned Threadripper core, pooled across precisions: ACL decodes in 12.5 ms, max-gate codec in 0.48 s, and transformer product in 2.1 s. | §§6.1, 6.6 |

These author-reported results support a storage and transport codec with verified sampled-time errors on a diverse corpus. They do not establish an equivalent runtime replacement for ACL, universal superiority of entropy models over other learned roles, or utility for motion generation and recognition. Stream sizes include headers and fallback but exclude shared model weights (§§3.1, 7).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ limitations:** Decoding is sequential and requires whole clips. Re-encoding into ACL adds another lossy stage, so the storage comparison does not establish equal-error runtime deployment (§§6.6, 7).
- **Error-bound scope:** Contracts depend on ACL’s measured errors, permit stated tolerances, and apply only at sample times. The shell metric approximates maximum displacement rather than evaluating the exact shell maximum (§§3.1, 3.3, 7).
- **Loose-precision failures:** The benchmarked max-gate encoder cannot always undo problematic folding late in its search. The mean gate permits maxima up to 24.7 cm on a few clips at 1 cm precision; the authors discourage it at loose precision (§§6.3, 6.7).
- **Limited negative evidence:** Alternative learned roles were tested with limited architectures and training runs; interpolation experiments used the mean gate, with extension to the max gate explicitly identified as inference (§5.3; §7).
- **Analyst validity assessment:** Frame-rate comparisons are confounded with dataset identity, as the authors acknowledge. Development clips also appear in the test side, although the reported exclusion check changes ratios by less than one point (§§6.1, 6.4).
- The full-corpus storage estimate is extrapolated; the full corpus was not encoded (Fig. 8).

## Open research questions
1. **Can an ACL-independent bound retain the corpus-scale savings?** Standalone precision constraints were tested only on a subset (§7). Next experiment: encode the full test side under an absolute bound, including checks between sample times.
2. **Can partial decoding offer a useful storage–latency tradeoff?** Whole-clip access is an inherent limitation of this design (§7). Next experiment: add independently decodable blocks and measure overhead, seek latency, and error.
3. **Can better interpolation change which keys are retained?** Negative results cover specific estimators, mainly under the mean gate (§5.3). Next experiment: compare multiple architectures and training runs with keys reoptimized under both contracts.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported codec design and headline results; **medium** in broad conclusions about where learning can help.
