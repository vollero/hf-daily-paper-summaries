# SearchJev: A Fast and Calibrated System-1 Model for Search Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.05107) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.05107)


## One-sentence takeaway
SearchJev directly scores schema-defined search decisions, reporting substantially faster inference and better in-distribution calibration than same-size generative baselines, while its strongest dual-system agent raises BrowseComp-Plus accuracy from 45% to 54% in a small evaluation (Figure 1; §§5.1–5.2).

## Problem and motivation
Search agents repeatedly judge relevance, evidence sufficiency, and next actions. The authors argue that generating these short judgments autoregressively adds latency and provides unreliable confidence, while task-specific classifiers fragment the decision pipeline. They identify a need for one efficient, calibrated model that handles heterogeneous decisions and signals when additional reasoning is needed (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Unified decision model:** SearchJev conditions a Qwen3.5 backbone on the search state, decision question, and legal options. It normalizes first-output-position logits over single-token option labels, using the existing LM head without generating a response (§3.1).
- **SLCD training and calibration:** LoRA training combines soft-target cross-entropy and Brier losses, downweights less reliable supervision, and applies held-out temperature calibration separately by output type (§§3.2, 4.4).
- **Dual-system agent:** SearchJev handles short judgments; a confidence threshold delegates uncertain cases to System 2, which also plans, generates queries, and writes answers. In the evaluated agent, verification only reports answer support afterward; it does not revise answers (Appendices B, D.3).
- **SearchDecision-Bench:** The authors standardize heterogeneous resources into state-schema examples covering routing, rewriting, relevance, sufficiency, navigation, and verification, with categorical, ordinal, and binary outputs (§2).

The contribution is this search-specific integration and benchmark; the paper explicitly builds on existing typed-output ideas, proper scoring losses, temperature scaling, and LoRA (§1; Appendix A).

## Evidence and main results

All results below are author-reported; the supplied text contains table captions and narrative summaries but omits the table bodies.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Better in-distribution decision quality | Both SearchJev sizes outperform same-size, untuned Qwen3.5 AR (JSON) across all six families; relevance uses NDCG@10 and the others accuracy. Jev remains stronger on navigation. | §§4.2–4.3, 5.1; Table 3 |
| Lower mean in-distribution calibration error | Mean ECE across six families decreases **74.4% at 0.8B** and **40.7% at 4B** relative to same-size Qwen3.5 AR (JSON). Jev has the lowest mean ECE. | §5.1; Table 3 |
| Faster decision inference | Median per-decision latency at batch size one is **5.2–5.3× faster** than same-size Qwen3.5 AR (JSON), and **7.2–9.6× faster** than the Jev API. | §5.1; Table 3 caption |
| Improved agent answers and efficiency | On **100 randomly sampled BrowseComp-Plus questions**, one run each, LLM-judged accuracy is **45%** for System-2-only, **46%** with SearchJev-0.8B, and **54%** with SearchJev-4B. With Qwen3.5-27B and BM25 fixed, SearchJev agents achieve **3.7–4.7× faster median active time** and **3.3–4.8× fewer System-2 output tokens**. | Figure 1; §§4.3–4.4, 5.2; Table 4 |
| Mixed generalization and calibration | On four held-out families, both sizes improve routing, rewriting, and relevance over same-size AR baselines; routing accuracy gains are **11.9–12.1 percentage points**. Verification improves at 0.8B but declines at 4B, and ECE improvements vary by family. | §5.3; Table 5 |
| Weakness on external verification | SearchJev-4B achieves **26.5 F1 for the hallucinated class** on held-out RAGTruth versus **71.2–81.0** reported for peers. Most peer comparisons use different test samples. | Appendices F.1–F.3; Table 9 |
| Delegation was not observed in reported agent runs | Neither SearchJev agent records explicit System-2 short-decision calls; the numerical confidence threshold cannot be recovered from aggregate logs. | Appendices D.3, E.2; Table 8 |

The evidence supports faster decisions and promising end-to-end performance in the tested configuration. It does **not** isolate the effects of direct readout, task-specific training, SLCD, or calibration. Nor does it establish that confidence-gated escalation caused the agent gains: the reported runs show no explicit delegated judgments. Appendix G’s utility bound concerns binary decisions and population calibration relative to rules using the same score; the authors explicitly caution against substituting empirical table ECE into it.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Evaluation covers Chinese and English text, finite option sets, and one end-to-end benchmark. Other languages, modalities, and domains remain untested (Appendix H).
- **Author-stated supervision risk:** Original, derived, and LLM-assisted labels may carry biases despite soft targets and weighting; LLMs participate in labeling 33.7% of training rows (Appendices C.1, H).
- **Analyst inference—confounded comparison:** SearchJev receives task-specific training, whereas the main AR baselines do not. No component ablations are supplied, limiting causal attribution (§4.2).
- **Analyst inference—limited agent evidence:** One run on 100 questions, with no reported uncertainty intervals or repeated runs, leaves the robustness of accuracy gains unresolved (§4.4).
- **Reproducibility and comparison limits:** The gate threshold is unavailable; Jev is evaluated through an API; peer scores are mostly self-reported and often use different samples. Appendix F.2 also reports training-state overlap in bev-decision and inconsistent sample-count descriptions.
- **Observed transfer risk:** OOD calibration is uneven, and external verification performance is weak (§5.3; Appendix F.3).

## Open research questions
1. **Which components produce the gains?** The supplied experiments do not disentangle training, readout, soft labels, Brier loss, and temperature scaling. A matched-training factorial ablation could measure accuracy, ECE, and latency.
2. **Does calibrated delegation improve the agent trade-off?** The gate threshold is missing and no explicit escalation occurs in reported runs. Sweep the threshold while logging delegation rates, decision errors, answer accuracy, and active time.
3. **How can confidence transfer reliably?** OOD calibration varies by family and RAGTruth verification is weak. Compare validation-only calibration with target-domain recalibration on fully held-out datasets.
4. **Do end-to-end gains persist across configurations?** Only one reasoner, retriever, and small question sample are tested. Repeat runs on a larger sample and vary reasoners and retrieval systems with paired uncertainty estimates.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The mechanism and narrative findings are clear, but important evidence is unavailable: table bodies and absolute decision scores, ECE and latency values; hardware and serving details; several training hyperparameters; the numerical gate threshold; component ablations; repeated-run statistics; and verified artifact contents. These omissions limit independent assessment of effect sizes, reproducibility, and causal explanations.
