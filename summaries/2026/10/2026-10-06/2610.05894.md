# Noise Out, Bias In: Targeted Bias Injection in Diffusion Language Models via Closed-Loop Activation Steering

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.05894) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.05894)


## One-sentence takeaway
The authors show that answer-probability feedback during denoising can amplify targeted demographic preferences in frozen diffusion language models beyond fixed steering on selected benchmarks, with substantial validity costs and uneven transfer (Table 1; §4.4).

## Problem and motivation
Checkpoint audits can miss bias introduced through compromised inference-time activation hooks. The authors identify repeated predictions before token commitment as an attack channel: an adversary can observe a target answer’s probability and adjust steering before the answer becomes final. They argue that prior diffusion-model steering did not exploit this answer-probability feedback (§§1–2, E.2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Decode-time feedback steering:** Fit a fixed target-versus-comparator direction from held-out completion activations, then use a proportional–integral controller to adjust its strength during denoising. The intervention changes residual activations while leaving weights, prompts, and the sampler unchanged (§3).
- **Controlled attribution of gains:** Compare against existing steering methods and ablations sharing the same direction and injection sites, including constant and per-example constant strengths (§§4.1–4.3).
- **Conditional theory and empirical boundaries:** A local linear model explains example-specific strength requirements and PI convergence; evaluations identify both successful settings and cases where constant steering wins (§3; Appendix D; §4.4).
- **Measurement and suppression diagnostics:** Test answer-order, parser, and sensor sensitivity, plus sign-inverted control as a limited suppression experiment (§4.5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong primary attack, with output failures | LLaDA-8B-Instruct, 400 ambiguous Black-target BBQ items × three rotations: PI raises the target–comparator gap from 1.8 to 16.7 pp; tuned constant steering reaches 4.6 pp. PI invalidity is 7.8%, versus 0% unsteered. | Table 1; §4.2 |
| Feedback exceeds matched constant strengths | Same primary setting: constant strength matched over token-updating steps gives a 3.5 pp gap and 20.8% invalidity; a hindsight per-example constant gives 8.4 pp and 13.4%, versus PI’s 16.7 pp and 7.8%. | Table 1; §4.3; A.4 |
| Primary advantage persists outside calibration items | On the remaining 300 BBQ items × three rotations, PI reaches a 16.2 pp gap, +14.1 pp over unsteered, with 7.2% invalidity; it exceeds every baseline by at least 12.1 pp. | A.5 |
| SocialStigmaQA answer control transfers | On 518 race items × three rotations, PI increases biased-answer selection from 17.6% to 58.1%, with strict-parser Δgap of +83.8 pp and 17.4% invalidity. Semantic parsing yields +99.9 pp Δgap and 1.4% invalidity. | Table 2; §4.4; C.8 |
| Demographic transfer includes failures | On position-balanced LLaDA-8B BBQ evaluations, Old yields Δgap +37.2 pp; Asian, White, and Latino PI runs have 93.8%, 96.1%, and 59.6% strict-invalid outputs and are classified as failures. | §4.4; C.2 |
| Cross-model superiority is not established | On the primary BBQ items and rotations, Dream PI yields Δgap +4.2 pp with zero invalidity, but its baseline lead is unresolved. LLaDA-MoE block-8 PI trails tuned CAA by 2.3 pp, with both producing zero strict-invalid outputs. | §4.4; C.3 |
| Suppression evidence is limited | On Black-target BBQ, sign-inverted PI changes the gap from 1.8 to 0.2 pp with 1.8% invalidity; the suppression gap’s interval contains zero. | §4.5; C.5 |

These author-reported results support a feedback advantage in the primary LLaDA setting against the tested constant-strength controls. They do not establish universal superiority, reliable free-form bias injection, or deployable mitigation. SocialStigmaQA demonstrates transfer of answer control: similar shifts without race descriptions prevent attributing the effect specifically to racial stigma (C.8).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ stated limits:** Primary gains were calibrated on part of the evaluation pool; several target-specific operating points were selected on evaluation data. Free-form generation is untested, and theory assumes a local linear response before commitment (§6).
- **Measurement sensitivity:** Answer position strongly changes measured gaps. Strict parsing can both accept malformed letter-prefixed responses and reject grammatical prose; SocialStigmaQA’s semantic parser was added after output inspection (§4.5; A.6; C.8).
- **Limited mitigation evidence:** Suppression was tested only where initial bias was near zero; the authors explicitly reject a deployable mitigation claim (§6; Ethics Statement).
- **Reproducibility gap:** Although code is claimed public, independent reproduction requires outputs and fitted directions outside the manuscript checkout; comprehensive artifact packaging is planned (Reproducibility Statement).
- **Analyst inference:** Recorded trajectories omit answer-token commitment times, limiting direct attribution of which feedback updates changed the final answer (C.7). Parser validity also provides incomplete evidence of output coherence (A.6).

## Open research questions

- **Can feedback steer free-form demographic content?** The method measures a designated answer letter, and free-form generation is untested. A next experiment could compare feedback and matched constants using a validated multi-token target sensor (§6).
- **Can susceptibility be predicted across models?** Dream saturates at its bound, while constant steering wins on LLaDA-MoE. Measure response sensitivity and commitment times on held-out prompts, then test whether these predict feedback gains (C.3, C.7).
- **Does SocialStigmaQA transfer survive unseen templates?** Calibration and evaluation share templates, and identity-free prompts shift similarly. Evaluate on disjoint templates with matched identity-present and identity-free questions (C.8).
- **Can serving-layer defenses mitigate substantial injected bias?** The suppression experiment begins near zero bias. Test suppression and endpoint detection against strong injected preferences, reporting accuracy, invalidity, and false alarms (§6; C.5).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the main comparisons and caveats, but omits numerical bodies of Tables 3 and 4, plotted confidence-interval endpoints, and some theorem statements. It also lacks verified release contents, complete immutable experiment artifacts, commitment-time logs, and free-form or substantial-bias mitigation evaluations.
