# Foundations of Proactive Agents: Principles, Technical Layers, and Proactivity-Gym

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.37267) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37267)


## One-sentence takeaway
Proactivity-Gym and a scenario-based human study support evaluating proactive LLM agents separately on task capability, temporal allocation, and trust, because correct assistance can still be poorly scheduled or violate users’ delegation preferences (§§5.2–5.3).

## Problem and motivation
Proactive agents must decide whether, what, when, and how to help without an explicit request. Correct work can consume resources needed elsewhere, interrupt users, or exceed their preferred autonomy. The authors argue that existing evaluations emphasize task or intervention success while insufficiently separating compute allocation and evolving trust (§§1, 3.2; Appendix A).

## Approach and contributions
- **Joint design framework:** Three objectives—Task Capability (TC), Temporal Allocation (TA), and Trust (TR)—combined in a weighted design objective. This synthesizes prior foundations; the paper does not demonstrate a new optimization algorithm (§3).
- **Technical design space:** Five dimensions—task scope, anticipation horizon, activation trigger, processing timing, and intervention depth—connected to user/environment modeling and LLM/harness requirements (§4).
- **Evaluation testbed and findings:** Proactivity-Gym supplies stateful tools, simulated time, conditional tasks, and persona-conditioned feedback across 10 scenarios lasting 7–10 simulated days. Evaluations cover 23 model–harness configurations, complemented by a 30-participant study (§§5, 5.3). Code and data release are planned, rather than established as available (reproducibility statement).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strongest reported model still has substantial gaps | In main non-reasoning evaluations, Claude Opus 5 has highest model-average TC: **65.1/100**, versus **51.7% TA** and **52.4% TR-D**. Each configuration has 90 scenario runs. | §5.2, Fig. 6(a); Appendix E.1 |
| Task capability tracks judged trust more than allocation or depth alignment | Run-level Pearson correlations: TC–TA **0.31**, TC–TR-D **0.34**, TC–TR-J **0.70**. | §5.2, Fig. 6(c) |
| Additional reasoning mainly improves TC and TA | GPT 5.6 Sol with Codex, none → extra-high reasoning: TC **49.96 → 58.93/100**; TA **10% → 17.2%**; TR-D **51.2% → 53.9%**; TR-J **3.99 → 4.06/5**. | Appendix F.2, Fig. 10 |
| Delegated execution frequently goes unattempted | Among tasks expecting execution, **77.21%** end without a valid execution attempt across evaluated agents; **45.96%** for Claude Opus 5. | Appendix F.5 |
| Timing affects stated acceptance despite imperfect output | Across the 30-participant scenario survey, acceptance is **26.7%** for correct assistance competing with ongoing work versus **97.8%** for sleep-time assistance requiring correction. | Trust loss exceeds subsequent recovery in scripted histories | With correct task outcomes and five-point trust ratings, aligned → misaligned transitions average **−1.86 points**, versus **+1.27** for the reverse. These results support separating the three evaluation dimensions. They do not establish that jointly optimizing 3T improves a deployed agent: no such intervention is evaluated. The authors interpret the judge correlations and human results as evidence that LLM judges underweight intervention misalignment; the human study uses scripted scenarios rather than identical benchmark trajectories.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Resource constraints and user models are simplified; richer scheduling, evolving preferences, broader actions, and longer real-world studies remain future work (§6; Appendix H).
- **Metric boundary:** TA requires both correct immediate prioritization and explicit deferral. Low TA can therefore reflect missing explicit deferral rather than choosing the wrong priority (Appendix E.3).
- **Trust boundary:** TR-D measures depth agreement; TR-J estimates perceived interaction quality, not human psychological trust (Appendix E.4).
- **Analyst inference:** Generalization is uncertain because the gym uses hand-authored scenarios and deterministic approval decisions, while participants are frequent LLM users judging hypothetical histories (Appendices D.1–D.2, G).
- **Reproducibility:** Many detailed tables and prompt bodies are absent from the supplied rendering; code/data availability is not confirmed.

## Open research questions
- **Does joint 3T optimization improve assistance?** The framework is proposed but not tested as a training or harness intervention. Compare a 3T-aware agent against the same backbone and harness without that intervention on held-out scenarios (Appendix H).
- **Can agents manage realistic resource schedules?** Current TA evaluates a constrained task pair. Add variable durations, changing budgets, and preemption; measure deferred-task completion and deadline success (Appendix E.3).
- **Can judged trust predict human trust?** TR-J is explicitly indirect and diverges from depth alignment. Have humans and judges rate identical trajectories, including both under- and over-intervention (Appendices E.4, F.5).
- **Do trust recovery patterns persist during real use?** Reported changes come from scripted week-long histories. Run a longitudinal field study with repeated trust and delegation measurements (Appendices G.1, H).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the framework and reported headline findings, but lacks full numerical table contents, most judge prompt bodies, exact confidence intervals for several headline comparisons, complete harness/runtime settings, and confirmed released code/data. Real-world effectiveness and a tested joint 3T optimization method remain unestablished.
