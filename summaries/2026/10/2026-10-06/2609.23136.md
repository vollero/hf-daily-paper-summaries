# Intent Interpretation at RIC Timescales: Jev Decision Models versus Large Language Models in 6G Open RAN

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.23136) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23136)


## One-sentence takeaway
Jev-1.13.0 met the measured one-second RIC deadline more reliably than hosted LLMs, but the study did not establish a general radio SLA penalty from slower interpretation (§V-A–D).

## Problem and motivation
Natural-language network intents must become typed A1 policies while traffic, mobility, and handovers continue. Interpretation delay can postpone enforcement and consume RIC capacity. The authors identify a gap in evaluations that jointly compare interpreter families, relate latency to control-loop deadlines, and measure subsequent radio outcomes (§I–II).

## Approach and contributions

- **RANIntent v1:** A synthetic, label-first benchmark pairing intents with telemetry tables of different sizes and qualities; a blind model verifier checks texts against canonical labels (§IV-B).
- **Closed-loop evaluation:** Recorded policies and latencies drive a multi-cell ns-3/5G-LENA simulation. Latency-only, accuracy-only, and combined arms separate timing effects from semantic errors (§IV-D–E).
- **Control-path validation:** Live interpretations traverse A1 and E2 to a software gNB, with timestamps separating interpretation, transfer, and acknowledgement (§IV-F).
- **Interpreter comparison:** Existing decision models and hosted LLMs share one policy schema; a same-weight generative reference helps examine whether typed readout itself improves performance (§IV-C).

The contribution is primarily the benchmark and integrated evaluation, rather than a newly trained interpreter.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Deadline reliability varies substantially | Across 4,800 calls per interpreter over 16 telemetry conditions, one-second feasibility was 99.8% for Jev, 97.5% for DeepSeek, 17.9% for GLM, and 0% for Qwen3.8; Jev’s decision-latency p99 was 0.472 s. | §V-D; Fig. 7 |
| Interpretation dominates the measured control path | On the software stack, with 30 clean intents per interpreter, median interpretation ranged from 0.286–2.35 s, versus 15.5–19.1 ms for A1 and 2.4–3.1 ms for E2 acknowledgement. | §IV-F; §V-F; Fig. 8 |
| Delay can affect SLA, but interpreter superiority was unresolved | At 0.3 intents/s and 30 km/h, primary two-second affected-class SLA violation moved 3.96 percentage points in the requested direction under oracle enforcement versus no update; delaying true-policy enforcement from 0.1 to 5 s raised violation by 3.93 points. Both resolved, but no H1 interpreter pair satisfied the required increase in both SLA metrics. | §V-A; Tables VI–VII |
| A general effect across radio conditions could not be tested | Only 3 of 9 rate-by-speed points passed eligibility controls; H2 required at least 5. | §V-B; Tables VI, VIII |
| Slow service can saturate interpretation capacity | With four slots at 2 intents/s, AnyJev-L0 and Qwen3.8 reached utilization 1.17 and 1.09, with p95 queue waits of 34.5 and 20.3 s. Jev utilization remained at most 0.149 across tested rates. | §IV-E; §V-C; Fig. 6 |
| Larger telemetry exposes grounding weaknesses | On fresh 57-cell tables, target-cluster accuracy was 0.907 for Jev, 0.960 for DeepSeek, and 0.967 for GLM; paired hosted-LLM differences against Jev were unresolved. Jev’s decline from 3 to 57 cells was 0.093 and resolved. | §V-D; Table XII; §VI-B |
| Direct interpreter control showed no resolved SLA gain | At the base point, per-second direct control yielded no resolved decrease among 28 SLA contrasts against policy-plus-xApp or oracle-plus-xApp; five contrasts increased. Tests were exploratory without multiplicity adjustment. | §V-E; Table XIV |

The evidence supports deployment-specific deadline and queueing differences. It also establishes that sufficiently delayed enforcement can worsen SLA in the calibrated simulation. It does **not** establish radio equivalence between interpreters, a universal advantage for decision-model interfaces, or a general radio penalty for slower hosted LLMs. “Unresolved” means the comparison did not meet the paper’s statistical criteria.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:** Simulation intervals describe temporal uncertainty within one shared radio realization per design point, not variation across independent realizations. Hosted-LLM base-point contrasts were sensitive to scheduler event ordering. The corpus is synthetic, hosted latency includes Sydney WAN and provider conditions, and simulation weights differ from real-stack PRB-quota actuation (§VI-C).

The real stack uses one cell without handover; gNB acknowledgement does not establish immediate radio actuation, and KPM changes include convergence and TCP effects (§IV-F). Only scheduler weights are actuated in simulation; other contract fields receive interpretation-layer scoring (§IV-D).

**Protocol qualification:** Eligibility depended on an amended C-1 rule. The authors report that the original five-percentage-point margin would leave neither H1 nor H2 testable (§IV-E; §V-B).

**Analyst inference:** Radio conclusions provide limited evidence about telemetry-grounding failures because radio and real-stack intents come from the named-scope, fresh-telemetry pool (§IV-B). The results also do not isolate output-interface effects from every serving-stack difference (§IV-C).

## Open research questions

- **Do interpreter SLA differences persist across independent radio realizations?** Current intervals condition on individual realizations and base-point ordering sensitivity. Next experiment: repeat paired arms across independent mobility, channel, and traffic seeds (§IV-H; §VI-C).
- **Can telemetry selection preserve grounding while meeting deadlines?** Accuracy declines with table size, but selection is proposed rather than evaluated. Next experiment: compare full tables, intent-specific filtering, and aggregation using correctness and deadline metrics (§V-D; §VI-A).
- **How do state-dependent grounding errors propagate into radio outcomes?** Such cases are benchmarked but excluded from the radio intent pool. Next experiment: replay state-dependent intents with stale and contradictory telemetry in the combined arm (§IV-B).
- **Does interpreter placement remain beneficial under richer actuation?** Simulation controls weights, while the small real stack controls PRB quotas. Next experiment: compare both mechanisms under matched workloads and measure enforcement effects beyond acknowledgement (§IV-D, F; §VI-C).
