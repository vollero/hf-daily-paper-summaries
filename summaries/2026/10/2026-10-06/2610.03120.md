# In-Distribution Forcing for Long Video Generation at Test Time

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.03120) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03120)


## One-sentence takeaway
ID-Forcing modifies cache construction and conditioning at inference time to reduce long-video drift, improving color stability and sustained motion on two evaluated backbones without additional training (§§3–4).

## Problem and motivation
Autoregressive video diffusion models generate successive chunks using cached key–value (KV) states, but colors, textures, and motion can deteriorate beyond their training horizon. The authors identify a gap in methods that change which cached entries are read or their temporal positions: these methods leave unchanged the context under which entries were originally constructed—the **KV-provenance problem** (§§1–2; Fig. 2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Provenance diagnosis:** The authors argue that identical chunks can produce different KV representations depending on their caching context; unseen provenance can propagate drift even when the subsequent conditioning window is unchanged (§3.1; Fig. 2).
- **Self-caching:** Selected entries are cached without attending to preceding entries. Remaining entries are reconstructed autoregressively above a self-cached anchor, following context configurations encountered during training (§3.1).
- **Joint conditioning control:** The method permanently retains the first chunk, bounds the conditioning window below the training horizon, and re-rotates recent keys into trained temporal positions. The first-chunk rationale depends on its distinctive causal-VAE encoding (§3.2; Algorithm 1).
- **Empirical validation:** Experiments compare the combined training-free method with existing cache-conditioning approaches and cumulatively ablate its components (§4; Tables 1–4).

## Evidence and main results

All quantitative generation comparisons below use the first 128 MovieGen prompts refined with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, at 480 × 832 resolution (§4.1). Drift scores are calibrated so higher is better (Appendix B).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Stronger Self-Forcing drift scores | At 240 seconds, ID-Forcing scores **70.34 color / 92.19 motion**, versus **20.98 / 56.25** for Self-Forcing. The strongest competing test-time scores are **62.52 / 88.28**, both from Deep Forcing. | Table 1 |
| LongLive improvement is metric-dependent | At 240 seconds, ID-Forcing scores **71.76 color / 96.09 motion**, versus LongLive’s **55.64 / 85.16**. MemRoPE has a higher motion score, **96.88**, but lower color score, **64.23**. | Table 1 |
| Highest dynamic degree in the main evaluation | ID-Forcing leads Dynamic Degree across both backbones at both 120 and 240 seconds; it does not lead every quality metric. | Table 1 |
| Self-caching adds benefit after conditioning fixes | In the cumulative 120-second Self-Forcing ablation, adding self-caching after sink retention and re-rotation raises color score **60.66 → 72.37**, motion score **87.50 → 89.06**, and aesthetic quality **54.14 → 58.23**. | Table 4 |
| Human preferences favor ID-Forcing | With 20 participants and 20 prompts using balanced baseline assignments, overall preference is **100%** versus Self-Forcing, **95%** versus Deep Forcing, **93%** versus ∞-RoPE, and **86%** versus MemRoPE. Comparisons use Self-Forcing-based methods. | §4.2; Table 2; Appendix C |

The evidence supports reduced drift in the tested settings, reinforced by component ablations and human preferences. It does not establish universally superior scores or arbitrary-duration stability. The authors’ claim of gains over all baselines on both drift metrics is too broad: MemRoPE leads LongLive motion scores in Table 1. Appendix A’s claim of universally highest Dynamic Degree also has an exception: MemRoPE exceeds ID-Forcing on 30-second Self-Forcing videos (Table 3).

## Limitations and validity risks
The supplied text contains no dedicated author limitations discussion.

- **Analyst inference—generalization:** Both backbones share the evaluated Wan architecture; results do not establish transfer to other architectures or VAE designs (§4.1).
- **Analyst inference—distributional claim:** Matching cache configurations and temporal positions does not by itself demonstrate equality of generated-content distributions. “Exactly in-distribution” remains stronger than the empirical drift evidence (§3).
- **Analyst inference—metric coverage:** Color drift compares endpoint hue histograms; motion drift compares early and late Dynamic Degree. These can miss intermediate failures, legitimate color changes, or consistently low motion (Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference—ablation scope:** Cumulative component additions support incremental benefit, but do not isolate every component interaction or window-length choice (Table 4).
- **Reporting gaps:** No uncertainty estimates, measured runtime overhead, hardware details, or explicit code-release information are supplied.

## Open research questions
- **Does the first-chunk rule generalize across encoders?** Its justification relies on causal-VAE asymmetry (§3.2). Test the same cache policies with different first-frame encoding schemes.
- **How do cache lengths affect quality and cost?** The experiments specify fixed backbone-dependent settings (§4.1). Sweep conditioning and self-caching lengths while measuring drift, latency, and memory.
- **Does stability persist beyond the evaluated horizon?** Results stop at 240 seconds (Tables 1–3). Evaluate substantially longer rollouts with intermediate checkpoints and human review.
- **Can endpoint stability coexist with narrative failure?** Current drift metrics emphasize endpoints (Appendix B). Test prompts requiring planned scene changes and score event completion alongside drift.

## Confidence and missing information
