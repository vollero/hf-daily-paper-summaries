# Skeleton-Grounded Latent Action Model for Robot World Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-23.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.31474) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31474)


## One-sentence takeaway

SkeLAM uses skeleton supervision to separate motion-focused latent actions from residual visual dynamics, improving action-conditioned video prediction on DROID while showing mixed AgiBot results and limited evidence for policy evaluation.

## Problem and motivation

Latent action models learn proxy actions by reconstructing visual transitions, which can mix robot motion with object responses and unrelated scene changes. The authors argue that this makes mapping robot controls into the latent space harder: similar motions can produce different visual outcomes depending on contact and scene state. Their proposed gap is a dedicated motion representation for action adaptation that still preserves complementary information for learning scene dynamics (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Skeleton-grounded decomposition:** A Transformer VAE encodes RGB transitions into kinematic and residual components. A pretrained, frozen skeleton encoder–decoder supervises the RGB kinematic component through skeleton reconstruction and latent alignment (§3.1).
- **Cross-modal swap reconstruction:** The RGB decoder reconstructs a transition using its residual latent paired with the corresponding skeleton-derived kinematic latent. This encourages interchangeable motion conditioning beyond latent-space alignment (§3.1).
- **Action-conditioned generation:** Separate projections feed both components into a Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B world model. Whole-branch dropout and a residual-removal curriculum prepare it for deployment without future-derived residual information. An action adapter predicts kinematic latents, followed by action-conditioned world-model fine-tuning (§3.2; Appendix B.2).

At deployment, generation uses the current RGB observation and robot actions; skeletons supervise representation learning rather than serving as required inference inputs (§3.2).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Better DROID prediction than latent-action baselines | On 400 clips with one conditioning frame and 12 future frames, SkeLAM achieves ADE **41.15 px** versus DreamDojo **51.11** and CD-LAM **53.90**; LPIPS **0.1310** versus **0.1473** and **0.1586**. Authors report reductions of **19.5%/23.7%** in ADE and **11.1%/17.4%** in LPIPS. | §4.1–4.2; Table 1 |
| Gains do not cover every motion metric | DROID direction macro-F1 is **0.5465**, below Ctrl-World **0.5888** and OSCAR **0.5810**. | Table 1 |
| Skeleton supervision and swap matter in reported ablations | Under the same stated training budget, removing skeleton supervision raises DROID ADE from **41.15** to **64.59 px**; removing swap raises it to **66.74 px**. | Table 2; Appendix D |
| Residual training has a modest motion benefit | Removing the residual from world-model training raises DROID ADE from **41.15** to **43.37 px**, while LPIPS slightly improves from **0.1310** to **0.1305**. | Table 2 |
| Kinematic latents concentrate linearly decodable motion | Frozen linear probes give EEF displacement MAE **0.00599** for the 32D kinematic latent versus **0.01620** for DreamDojo and **0.01615** for CD-LAM; the 64D concatenation reaches **0.00597**. | Table 3; Appendix C.2 |
| AgiBot results are mixed | On 400 held-out clips, SkeLAM improves SSIM over DreamDojo (**0.8479** versus **0.8330**) but worsens LPIPS (**0.1049** versus **0.0889**) and ADE (**21.10** versus **19.55 px**). Valid arm trajectories differ: **755/800** versus **754/800**. | Tables 7–8 |
| Policy agreement improves, but successful outcomes remain difficult | Across seven policies and 105 paired RoboArena episodes, SkeLAM versus Cosmos-Predict2.5 achieves balanced accuracy **63.02% versus 48.44%**, success recall **33.33% versus 0.00%**, and policy success-rate MAE **4.76 versus 7.62 percentage points**. SkeLAM’s policy Spearman correlation is **0.5347**. | Table 4; §4.5; Appendix C.3 |
| Human-to-robot transfer is qualitative | Two UniSkill prompts produce visibly different generated robot motions with fixed weights; the protocol assesses motion correspondence rather than task completion. | §4.4; Figure 5; Appendix C.3 |

The evidence supports motion specialization and improved DROID prediction under the reported protocol. It does not establish complete disentanglement, consistent gains across datasets, reliable human-to-robot task execution, or closed-loop policy evaluation. Motion comparisons also use method-dependent valid trajectory subsets, rather than an identical scored subset (Appendices C.1–C.3).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Human transfer comprises two qualitative examples and does not establish task completion or robustness across arbitrary viewpoints and embodiments (Appendix C.3).
- **Author-stated policy limits:** Recorded actions are replayed without policy feedback. Only nine real successes occur among 105 episodes, and seven policy-level rate pairs limit ranking evidence (Appendix C.3). Closed-loop evaluation is future work (§5).
- **Author-stated representation limit:** Cross-covariance regularization and interventions support specialization without establishing independent or fully disentangled components (Appendices B.1, C.2).
- **Analyst inference—action realism:** DROID actions are computed from consecutive executed states, including destination gripper openness. This leaves performance with prospective commanded controls unresolved (Appendix A).
- **Analyst inference—measurement and uncertainty:** Invalid tracks are excluded, direction F1 further excludes stationary cases, and visual metrics include the conditioning frame. These choices constrain interpretation. No repeated-run uncertainty or confidence intervals are supplied (Appendices B.2, C.1).
- **Analyst inference—component attribution:** The reported ablations do not independently isolate branch dropout or the residual-removal curriculum. Removing skeleton supervision also removes swap, so that ablation measures a bundled change (Appendix D).

## Open research questions

- **Can the model support closed-loop evaluation over longer horizons?** Current policy tests replay recorded actions. A next experiment would feed generated observations back to policies and compare decisions and outcomes against matched real executions (§5).
- **Why do DROID gains fail to transfer uniformly to AgiBot?** AgiBot motion and perceptual results trail DreamDojo. A next experiment could stratify results by embodiment, camera, and motion type while scoring a common valid subset (Tables 7–8).
- **How much do dropout and the residual curriculum contribute independently?** Neither is separately isolated. A factorial ablation could vary both while holding initialization and optimization budgets fixed (Appendix B.2; Table 2).
- **Does action adaptation work equally well with commanded controls?** The DROID interface uses executed state deltas. A matched experiment could compare command-conditioned and state-delta-conditioned predictions (Appendix A).

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text provides detailed methods, protocols, and results, but broader claims exceed the demonstrated settings.

Material missing information includes code/checkpoint availability, full baseline adaptation and compute-matching procedures, repeated-run uncertainty, probe data splits and magnitude-class definitions, human-annotation agreement, detailed AgiBot action-interface specifications, and commanded-action or closed-loop validation.
