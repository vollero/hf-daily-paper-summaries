# Agent Mosaic: Recognizing Confidentiality Leaks in Agentic LLM Systems

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-23.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.30418) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30418)


## One-sentence takeaway
In a synthetic confidentiality benchmark, agents disclose prohibited conclusions assembled from permitted documents even under benign queries, while classification headers and aggregation rules reduce—but do not eliminate—disclosure (§§5.2–5.4).

## Problem and motivation
Document access controls can permit every retrieved source while prohibiting a sensitive conclusion inferred from their combination. The authors study this “mosaic effect”: whether agents reveal designated higher-sensitivity relationships during otherwise authorized use. Their identified gap is an evaluation that explicitly separates permission to read constituent facts from permission to disclose their composite (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled benchmark:** Thirty synthetic examples across cybersecurity, medical, and military domains, each containing two permitted documents, a forbidden composite, an optional distractor, and benign, explicit, and adversarial queries. The experiment crosses intent, distractor presence, classification headers, and aggregation rules (§3.1).
- **Simulation and live evaluation:** Three generators—GPT-5.6-Sol, Claude Sonnet 5, and Qwen3.5-27B—produce 10,800 simulated answers; GPT-led Pi and Hermes harnesses produce 1,440 live answers with autonomous document selection (§3; Abstract).
- **Separate disclosure and refusal measurement:** A human-informed MiMo judge labels both outcomes independently, exposing refusals that themselves reveal protected conclusions (§4).

The contribution is an evaluation framework and empirical findings, rather than a new enforcement mechanism. Code and reproducibility assets are promised upon acceptance; availability is not established in the supplied text (§1).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Benign requests cause disclosure | GPT simulation: **7.08%**, 95% CI **3.75–10.67%**; live Pi: **12.50%**; live Hermes: **8.75%**. Rates pool safeguard and distractor settings under benign queries. | Abstract; §5.2, Fig. 1 |
| Both safeguards reduce pooled disclosure but leave substantial residual risk | Across three simulated generators, all intents, and both distractor settings, enabling headers and rules reduces disclosure by **36.26 percentage points**, leaving **32.37%**, 95% CI **28.33–36.19%**. | Abstract; §5.3, Figs. 3–4 |
| Residual disclosure differs sharply by generator | With headers shown, rules on, and no distractor, explicit-query disclosure is **0.00% GPT / 58.00% Claude / 64.00% Qwen**; adversarial-query disclosure is **0.67% / 79.33% / 75.33%**, respectively; **150 answers per cell**. | §5.2; Table 14 caption |
| Refusal can reveal the composite | With both safeguards enabled, **404 of Claude’s 410 disclosure-positive answers** also contain refusal, across all intents and both distractor settings; this arm contains **900 answers per generator**. | §5.4, Fig. 5 |
| Live differences depend on domain | Military disclosure rises from approximately **17%** in simulated GPT to approximately **one-quarter** in each live harness; both military live-minus-simulation confidence intervals are above zero. | §5.5, Figs. 7–8 |

These findings establish observed final-answer confidentiality failures in the evaluated settings. They do not estimate real deployment prevalence, demonstrate task utility, or isolate why live harnesses differ from simulation. Pooled safeguard results include explicit and adversarial requests and therefore are not benign-use rates.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ scope limitations:** The small synthetic benchmark has only author-reviewed realism, fixed queries, one adversarial suffix, and at most one distractor. Intermediate reasoning and tool-output disclosure are excluded (§3.1; Limitations).
- **Authors’ measurement limitations:** Judge selection and assessment reuse the same human-labeled development answers. Labels use volunteer majority votes rather than adjudication; confidence intervals exclude judge error and annotation bias (§4; Limitations).
- **Authors’ comparison limitations:** Native prompts, tools, and document selection vary together across harnesses. Served model aliases are not independently verified immutable weights, and there is no reasoning-off arm. Correctness and utility require separate evaluation (Limitations).
- **Analyst inference:** Generalization of the scoring protocol remains uncertain because there is no independent judge-validation set. Moreover, the supplied text omits numerical table bodies, preventing inspection of reported judge agreement and many detailed contrasts.

## Open research questions
- **Can deterministic controls preserve confidentiality while retaining utility?** The paper tests in-context safeguards, while enforcement is proposed as future work. Next experiment: compare retrieval and answer-release checks against prompting, measuring disclosure, false positives, and task correctness (§7).
- **Which harness components drive domain differences?** Prompts, tools, and context selection are confounded. Next experiment: vary these components separately with matched documents, model settings, and exposure logs (§§5.5–6).
- **How robust are the findings to broader inputs?** Fixed queries and limited distractors leave adaptive attacks and retrieval variation unresolved. Next experiment: expand independently reviewed examples and vary paraphrases, distractor volume, and workflow length (Limitations; §7).
- **Do local safeguards survive multi-agent handoffs?** Distributed access and shared memory are not evaluated. Next experiment: vary communication and clearance boundaries while tracking end-to-end disclosure and utility (§7).
