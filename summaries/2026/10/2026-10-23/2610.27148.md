# Who Takes the Lead? Model Ability and Task-Conditioned Collaboration with Humans

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-23.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.27148) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27148)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across four models in a controlled shared maze, stronger solo navigation was associated with more selective use of a better-positioned human teammate, without establishing that capability causes better collaboration (§4.3).

## Problem and motivation
Human-involvement rates are ambiguous: frequent involvement can reflect useful delegation or weak independent ability, while infrequent involvement can reflect competent self-reliance or missed opportunities. The authors address this ambiguity by measuring solo capability and experimentally controlling the human teammate’s positional advantage (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled shared-control environment:** Two avatars pursue one target; the model chooses self-movement, human movement with directional guidance, or human movement without guidance. Exact-distance start sampling varies relative route length while preserving the objective and protocol (§3).
- **Capability-conditioned evaluation:** Solo navigation establishes an independent capability baseline; co-play measures initial allocation and subsequent behavior across agent-favored, tied, and human-favored conditions (§§4.1–4.2).
- **Empirical findings:** The three near-ceiling solo models showed selective human involvement; the weaker model did not. Human involvement overwhelmingly retained model guidance rather than transferring directional authority (§4.3).

These are environment, evaluation, and behavioral contributions; the paper does not introduce a trained collaboration policy.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

The evidence supports a descriptive association between solo capability and allocation selectivity in this setting. It does not establish a causal capability effect or a causal benefit from increased human involvement. Whole-game advantage also depends on earlier actions. Numerical confidence intervals cannot be checked because the supplied text contains table captions but omits table cells.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ limitations:** Only four models, four human participants—all authors—and one maze environment and protocol; comparisons are descriptive rather than population-level estimates (§§4.2, 6).
- **Authors’ causal qualification:** Model capability is measured, not experimentally manipulated; later route advantage is affected by preceding actions (§4.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Positional advantage and asymmetric visibility favor using the human as a guided actuator. Rare deference therefore provides limited evidence about willingness to yield authority when humans possess superior knowledge (§3.2; Appendix A).
- **Analyst inference:** Because prompts never identify the teammate as human, the results establish adaptation to teammate position more directly than adaptation to human identity (Appendix A).
- **Evidence gap:** Missing table cells prevent inspection of detailed uncertainty, efficiency, inference settings, and most diagnostics.

## Open research questions
- **Does capability itself improve allocation selectivity?** Four model identities cannot isolate that effect. A next experiment could vary navigation support within one model and measure resulting solo capability and co-play selectivity (§§4.1, 4.3).
- **Does selection generalize beyond route length?** Only positional advantage is manipulated. Give humans private task information and test whether models appropriately request guidance or defer (§6).
- **Would a broader participant population produce the same pattern?** Operators are four authors. Replicate with independent participants of varied navigation experience and record guidance overrides (§4.2).
- **How much does asymmetric visibility explain rare deference?** Humans have local views while models see the full map. Compare local, equal, and human-superior observability while holding start distances fixed (§3.2).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The narrative clearly supports the central descriptive finding, but detailed verification is constrained by missing table contents.

Material unavailable information includes numerical confidence intervals, complete allocation and efficiency results, exact inference settings and compute costs, precise human-view geometry, participant ordering procedures, and a code/data release location.
