# SplatLab: Autonomous Scientific Discovery for 3DGS Research

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-23.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.30468) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30468)


## One-sentence takeaway
SplatLab combines verified 3DGS baselines, execution-guided evolutionary search, and claim-to-log audits to generate research manuscripts, with promising reported results but unresolved compute fairness, generalization, and evaluation limitations.

## Problem and motivation
The authors argue that autonomous research systems lack reliable baseline reproduction, sustained experimental refinement, and traceability from manuscript claims to measurements. In 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), implementation details make reproduction expensive, so an apparent improvement can reflect baseline errors rather than a useful mechanism (§1).

Their proposed gap is a complete research workflow anchored to verified implementations in an active vision subfield, covering literature review through experiments and manuscript revision.

## Approach and contributions
SplatLab orchestrates 36 agents through six operational stages, with loops back to implementation and experimentation (§3; Fig. 2).

- **Verified implementation substrate:** Candidates modify previously verified SPLATIFY/NERFIFY plugins through constrained code regions. The reproduction backbone is inherited; SplatLab’s contribution is its integration into the research loop.
- **Execution-guided search:** Initial LLM-based idea ranking feeds an eight-island tournament scored on measured PSNR. Elo updates depend on evaluation depth and benchmark/loss context; textual reflections guide subsequent ideas (§3.1, Stages 2–4).
- **Falsification coupled to selection:** Stress tests cover exposure, trajectories, sparse views, and hyperparameter perturbations; failures penalize candidate ratings (§3.1, Stage 4).
- **Audited manuscript production:** Numerical claims link to experiment identifiers, commits, and configurations. Audits also check compute matching and portability; reviewers can request new training runs (§3.1, Stages 5–6).
- **Domain-specific modification scaffolds:** A 3DGS taxonomy and hook mapper structure the search space. The authors claim this combination improves search efficiency and portability, rather than introducing evolutionary search or verbal feedback themselves.

## Evidence and main results

All entries below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Mixed research outcomes | Across 16 runs and 198 experiment branches: five verified positives, five marginal results, five non-PSNR contributions, and one failure. | §4, “Run outcomes,” p. 7 |
| Standard-benchmark improvement | TileFocalGS versus FastGS on Mip-NeRF 360: PSNR 29.69 versus 29.12 dB; SSIM 0.869 versus 0.858; LPIPS 0.194 versus 0.217. | Table 1, p. 7 |
| Largest highlighted gain | On the new Nebula scene, Nebula3DGS-v2 versus vanilla 3DGS: PSNR 36.36 versus 32.58 dB, a +3.78 dB gain. | Table 1, p. 7 |
| One gain survives repeated seeds | Five-seed GNS-AA evaluation: 26.18±0.05 versus GNS 25.95±0.07 dB; reported delta +0.23±0.09 dB. | §4, “Multi-seed Robustness,” p. 8 |
| Audit and falsifier ablations favor the full system | In the canonical pool, unbacked-number manuscripts decrease from 16/16 without the audit gate to 0/16 with it; winners failing held-out attacks decrease from 5/16 without falsifier penalties to 0/16 with them. | Table 6, p. 9 |
| Writing scores approach published references | Over five shared prompts, Gemini-judged writing scores are 90/100 for SplatLab, 93 for published papers, and 79 for PaperOrchestra. This evaluates writing, not end-to-end discovery. | Table 4, p. 9 |
| Human review is less favorable than automated review | For five selected SplatLab papers versus five accepted papers, human overall scores are 3.5 versus 4.1 on a 1–6 scale; multi-agent scores are 3.6 versus 3.5. | Table 5, p. 9 |
| Substantial per-run resources | Reported budgets: 24–48 hours, 2–36 GPU-hours, average 113M API tokens, and estimated API cost $150–250 per run. | §4, “Compute cost,” p. 7 |
| Taxonomy helps one target’s search efficiency | On Nebula, taxonomy-guided versus taxonomy-free search reports +3.78 versus +3.45 dB PSNR gains, using 47.5M versus 85.5M tokens. | “Portability and Taxonomy Ablation,” p. 10 |

The evidence supports the feasibility of producing executable modifications and auditable drafts within a prepared research environment. It does not establish superior discovery efficiency under matched resources: §4 explicitly says search budgets are not equalized.

The blanket claim that every generated manuscript improves PSNR is inconsistent with the outcome breakdown and the included NeedleSwap manuscript, which reports a nine-scene mean PSNR regression of −0.184 dB alongside improved LPIPS (Fig. 4b, embedded Table 1). Results therefore need to be interpreted by contribution type.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Compute fairness is unresolved.** Table 1’s MSAA-Bilat variant adds 15K iterations, despite the surrounding claim of comparisons under original iteration counts and compute budgets. The supplied text does not show its compute-matched audited result.
- **Prior-system comparisons have limited scope.** Table 2 is a capability assessment; Table 4 compares writing from shared ideas and supplied materials. Neither establishes a resource-matched advantage in autonomous discovery.
- **Generalization remains narrow.** The taxonomy ablation covers one target. Additional non-3DGS runs are referred to an appendix unavailable in the supplied text.
- **Author-stated risks:** Building verified backbones requires field-specific resources; automated audits can themselves contain bugs; generated papers may overload peer review (§5).
- **Analyst inference:** The canonical-pool ablations support component usefulness, but missing repeated-run variability and detailed budgets limit causal and efficiency conclusions.
- **Analyst inference:** Selected top-paper reviews and an autonomous novelty screen cannot establish consistently novel, publication-quality output across all runs. Human reviewers score soundness and presentation below published references (Table 5).
- **Internal reporting inconsistencies:** The included AuditSplat manuscript variously associates ACID lift values with different sample sets; its later Table 3 distinguishes +3.98 dB on the official probe from +4.63 dB on the extended set. Traceability alone does not guarantee consistent prose.

## Open research questions

1. **Does SplatLab outperform simpler agents at equal total cost?** Current discovery budgets are unequal. Run identical targets with matched GPU-hours, tokens, baseline access, and blinded outcome assessment.
2. **How much does the verified substrate contribute independently?** Table 6 reports reproducibility benefits, but broader repeated trials are missing. Compare verified and unverified starting implementations across multiple targets and seeds.
3. **Can the workflow transfer beyond prepared 3DGS repositories?** Portability evidence is limited in the supplied text. Test a new domain while recording backbone construction effort, taxonomy development, failures, and total cost.
4. **Do audits catch scientifically invalid but logged claims?** Existing results emphasize provenance and predefined attacks. Seed experiments with compute confounds, leakage, incorrect aggregation, and unsupported causal interpretations, then measure detection rates.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The architecture and representative results are clearly described, but stronger conclusions are limited by inconsistent claims and unavailable supporting details.

Material missing information includes the referenced appendix/supplement, full run-by-run outcomes, exact LLM stack and prompts, reproduction tolerances, per-target budgets and seeds, complete compute-matched results, detailed ablation protocols, and independent inspection of released code and ledgers.
