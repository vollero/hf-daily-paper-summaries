# Heartbeat Thinking: Speculative Reasoning and Tool Execution While Listening in Realtime Voice Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-23.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.26233) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26233)


## One-sentence takeaway
Heartbeat Thinking increases reusable speculative tool work by letting computation continue across speech updates, reducing mean final-response latency on FDBench v3 while producing mixed task-quality effects across benchmarks.

## Problem and motivation
Voice agents often wait until speech ends before reasoning and calling tools, even when actionable information arrives earlier. The authors identify a scheduling bottleneck: chunk-bounded or update-interrupted generation can stop a tool call before its arguments are complete. Useful early preparation must also survive later constraints and intent revisions (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Overlapping preparation:** Periodic Thinker invocations read the latest ASR transcript and shared preparation state without interrupting earlier invocations. Completed reasoning and asynchronous tool results support subsequent calls, including dependent calls without new speech (§3; Appendix A.2).
- **Explicit response handoff:** At the detected speech endpoint, a separate Responder receives the final transcript and completed preparation, checks relevance, and executes remaining calls. Unfinished speculative jobs are cancelled; late outputs are discarded. Thinker and Responder models can differ (§3; Appendix A.3).
- **Empirical assessment:** Comparisons across three benchmarks measure reusable preparation, task quality, model sensitivity, and FDBench latency. The findings distinguish greater preparation reuse from better final answers (§4; Appendices C–D).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Higher reusable preparation across benchmarks | With Qwen3.6-35B-A3B Thinker and DeepSeek-V4-Flash Responder, Heartbeat versus Window Thinking STC: SpeechFCEval **43.69% vs 33.13%**; FDBench **50.67% vs 34.67%**; AudioCRAG **62.42% vs 20.05%**. AudioCRAG STC measures retrieval reuse rather than reference-call coverage. | Table 1; §4.1; Appendix B |
| Mixed final task quality | Same configuration: FDBench Quality **77.0% vs 72.0%**, Pass@1 **65.0% vs 57.0%**; SpeechFCEval accuracy **66.85% vs 67.95%**; AudioCRAG correct-answer rate **55.49% vs 57.24%**. | Table 1 |
| Lower mean final-response latency on FDBench | Heartbeat **4.39 s**, Window Thinking **6.90 s**, Pipecat **8.08 s**: reported reductions of **36.4%** and **45.7%**, respectively. Measured from aligned speech endpoint to final spoken-response onset; thinking disabled for both models. | Figure 4; Tables 4–5; Appendix B.2 |
| Earlier matching tool-call initiation | FDBench mean initiation offset: Heartbeat **−4.37 s**, Window Thinking **−2.74 s** relative to speech end. Conditional on a matching call; speculative calls qualify only when completed and included in Responder context. | Figure 3; Table 5; Appendix B.2 |
| Coverage advantage persists across tested Responders | Heartbeat exceeds Window Thinking in STC with each of three Responders on all three benchmarks; quality rankings vary. | Tables 2–3, 9; Appendix C.2 |
| Correct arguments do not ensure correct result use | Authors report erroneous or outdated result use in **4.0% of FDBench recordings**, despite correct tool-call arguments. A cart example combines an earlier purchase result with a later corrected search. | §4.2; Appendix D |

These results support increased reuse and lower mean latency in the evaluated FDBench configuration. They do not establish consistent quality improvements, cross-workload latency gains, or lower total computation cost. STC includes successful handoff and absence of repeated response-stage calls, so it measures more than early tool initiation alone (Appendices B, E.2).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Fixed scheduling and concurrency leave adaptive triggering unexplored; redundant inference may occur when little information changes (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** Snapshot handoff excludes late results, potentially wasting useful work from variable-duration tools (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** Latency evaluation covers only FDBench; broader workload and inference-cost trade-offs remain uncharacterized (Limitations).
- **Analyst inference:** State-changing speculation needs stronger safeguards. The documented cart action persists after a corrected search; revising reasoning does not reverse an executed action (Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference:** The scheduling comparison has limited breadth: Window Thinking follows Heartbeat’s schedule but discards late window outputs; direct experimental comparisons with other discussed speculative systems are absent (§4.1; Appendix F).
- **Analyst inference:** Uncertainty is difficult to assess because confidence intervals, repeated-run variability, and significance tests are not reported. FDBench includes 100 recordings, and its quality evaluation uses DeepSeek-V4-Flash as judge (Appendix B.2).

## Open research questions
1. **Can adaptive scheduling preserve reuse with less computation?** Fixed scheduling is the only evaluated policy. Compare transcript-change and tool-completion triggers against the fixed policy, measuring inference cost, STC, quality, and latency.
2. **When should the Responder accept late results?** Current handoff discards them. Vary tool execution times and compare snapshot handoff with incremental result delivery.
3. **How can agents safely revise speculative actions?** The cart case exposes persistent effects after intent changes. Test delayed commitment or reversible actions on requests containing late constraints, measuring incorrect state changes and task success.
4. **Why does higher reuse sometimes reduce answer quality?** Aggregate results and failure examples leave the mechanism unresolved. Ablate speculative reasoning records and tool-result inclusion separately, auditing stale-result selection and answer correctness.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported method and descriptive benchmark findings; causal explanations for quality changes remain uncertain.

Material missing information includes total inference and tool costs, latency distributions and variability, scheduling/concurrency ablations, latency results beyond FDBench, detailed AudioCRAG scoring procedures, and an explicit implementation-release location.
