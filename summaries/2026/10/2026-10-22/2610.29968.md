# CulturalRiddles: A Multicultural Benchmark of Riddles

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-22.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.29968) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29968)


## One-sentence takeaway
CulturalRiddles evaluates culturally grounded riddle solving across diverse communities, finding that strong overall model accuracy conceals severe community gaps and that reliable scoring requires accepting valid answers beyond exact matches (§§3–5).

## Problem and motivation
The authors argue that cultural understanding requires interpreting implicit local associations and combining clues, beyond recalling facts. They identify three gaps in existing benchmarks: narrow cultural coverage, insufficient validation of items and difficulty, and limited scalable human-informed evaluation (§1).

Riddles provide a test of applying cultural knowledge through abductive and compositional reasoning, although final-answer accuracy cannot isolate those abilities (§1; App. A.3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Community-authored benchmark:** 6,191 original riddles from 167 native speakers, covering 62 national-linguistic communities, 51 languages, and 45 countries. Each riddle is verified by another native speaker; shared and community-selected topics support breadth (§3).
- **Human-informed scoring:** Rules, native-speaker annotation, and two validated LLM judges accommodate alternate correct answers. Native speakers label 68,067 distinct answers covering 100,769 model responses (§4.2).
- **Comparative evaluation and extensions:** The authors evaluate 28 model configurations, analyze community and resource disparities, test English translations, and construct crossword variants (§§4–6).
- **Reusable release:** The paper reports public dataset, evaluation code, annotation artifacts, and a contribution workflow (§3.2).

The abstract’s description of entirely human-created content needs qualification: surveyed writers report AI brainstorming and occasional paraphrasing or co-writing (§3.1).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

| Leading averages conceal community failures | Across 57 human-evaluated communities, Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview achieves 79.4% accuracy, GPT-5.5 76.3%, and Claude Opus 4.7 Thinking 66.0%; no evaluated model exceeds 22% in Lumasaba or Mongondow. Accuracy uses scored responses accepted as fully correct. | Fig. 6; §§5.2, 5.5 |
| Language resources correlate with accuracy | Accuracy pooled over models and scored responses is 37.0%, 30.8%, 18.0%, and 12.7% for high-, medium-, low-, and extremely low-resource language groups, respectively. Groups use Common Crawl page counts. | §5.5; Fig. 8 |
| Exact match substantially undercounts correctness | Across human-evaluated communities, case-insensitive exact match misses about 47% of ultimately correct responses; review moves Claude Opus 4.7 Thinking from fifth to third. | §5.3; Fig. 6 |
| Judges approximate human agreement on coarse scores | On 4,018 doubly annotated test pairs, human accuracy agreement is κ = 0.80 ± 0.04; individual judge–human accuracy agreement is κ = 0.79–0.81. This measures agreement, not correctness against independent ground truth. | §5.1; Fig. 5 |
| Hallucination labels greatly outnumber abstentions | Among 132,940 rule- or human-scored responses, 24.19% are labeled hallucinated or irrelevant, versus 0.80% explicitly admitting uncertainty; jury-only responses are excluded. | Table 3; §5.4 |
| Translation helps but leaves substantial difficulty | Five models on five hard communities: among 2,049 responses scored in both conditions after excluding answer-revealing translations, accuracy rises from 22.1% to 32.4%, a paired gain of 10.3 percentage points (95% CI 7.6–13.0). | §6.1; App. B.4; Fig. 14 |
| Crossword constraints improve consistency more clearly than correctness | Across 53 puzzles and 11 configurations, crossword input versus answer-length input changes reference agreement by +1.6 points (95% CI −0.3–3.7) and crossing consistency by +7.2 points (4.8–9.6). | §6.2; App. B.3 |

The evidence establishes substantial performance disparities on these authored riddles and shows that scoring choices materially affect measured performance. It does not establish population-wide cultural competence, causally attribute gaps to training-data coverage, or separate missing knowledge from failed reasoning. The translation comparison uses different high-resource riddles as its reference and lacks native-speaker scoring of English answers (App. B.4).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope limits:** National-linguistic communities are an imperfect cultural unit. Writers are volunteers, usually few per community, and some riddles intentionally require regional or generational knowledge (§3.1).
- **Evaluation uncertainty:** Double annotation covers only 19 communities and omits most of the hardest ones. Fine-grained error types have weaker agreement and are used descriptively (§5.1; App. A.3).
- **Shared judge errors:** Both judges sometimes accept human-rejected answers and reject valid alternatives. Production scoring excludes unresolved answers, totaling 1.78% of all outputs (§§4.2, 5.1).
- **Run sensitivity:** Each configuration answers once under one English prompt; stochastic decoding and alternative prompts could change scores or rankings (App. A.2).
- **Translation and crossword caveats:** Translation can alter clues; crossword list-to-grid comparisons also change instructions and token budgets. Some crossword responses have unresolved provenance (Apps. B.3–B.4).
- **Analyst inference:** Community accuracy differences may partly reflect differences in authored item difficulty and contributor selection; the study supplies no representative human-solving baseline that would calibrate these factors.
- **Content-origin ambiguity:** Surveyed AI assistance qualifies the abstract’s human-only framing; item-level extent is not established (§3.1).

## Open research questions
1. **Do failures reflect missing facts or misinterpreted clues?** Final-answer scoring cannot distinguish them (App. A.3). A next experiment could supply verified local facts separately from explicit clue interpretations and compare recovery.
2. **How much do community gaps depend on item difficulty?** Independent verification establishes intended solvability, not representative human success rates (§3.1). Test the same items on diverse native-speaker samples and compare models after human-difficulty calibration.
3. **How stable are rankings across prompts and repeated runs?** The paper uses one prompt and one response per configuration–riddle pair (App. A.2). Repeat evaluation with multiple seeds and localized prompt variants.
4. **Can judging improve without losing valid local alternatives?** Judges share errors and reject some human-accepted alternatives (§5.1). Test expanded accepted-answer sets and targeted native review on held-out communities.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported benchmark design and descriptive findings; **medium** in explanations of why community gaps arise.

Material missing information includes representative human-solving baselines, repeated-run and prompt-sensitivity results, item-level AI-assistance records, comprehensive independent annotation across communities, and fully resolved provenance for the flagged crossword responses.
