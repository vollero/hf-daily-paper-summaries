# Multimodal Embodied Agents: A Unified Capability-centric Perspective from Computer-Use to Robot-Use

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-22.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.30108) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30108)


## One-sentence takeaway
PAPAV offers a functional framework for comparing computer-use and robot-use agents, with a survey of 64 benchmarks showing that explicit evaluation rarely measures action anticipation or agent-side outcome verification (§§1, 4.4).

## Problem and motivation
Computer-use agents and robotic systems increasingly combine multimodal reasoning, actions, and feedback, but their survey taxonomies remain separated by architectures and application settings. The authors argue that a shared functional vocabulary is needed to explain how capabilities change when actions have physical consequences (§1).

Physical interaction introduces partial observability, limited simulatability, irreversibility, real-time coupling, and uncertain outcome verification. These constraints make coordination across the interaction loop consequential for reliability (§§1, 3).

## Approach and contributions
This is a survey and analytical framework, rather than a new trained agent.

- **Functional taxonomy:** PAPAV distinguishes **Perceive, Anticipate, Plan, Act, and Verify** by their outputs and influence on task decisions, independently of architecture. Memory and orchestration form a coordinating “agentic harness” (§§1, 2.4).
- **Three-way comparison:** The authors compare multimodal agents, robotic systems, and their embodied intersection for each capability. They also define system-level membership criteria requiring persistent physical interaction, documented capability returns, and embodiment-grounded decisions (§§2.3, 3).
- **Benchmark audit:** A curated corpus is labeled using explicit, implicit, or absent capability evaluation. External success checkers do not count as agent-side Verify, and multimodal inputs alone do not establish explicit Perceive evaluation (§4, Table 2).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Corpus spans three system families | 64 retained benchmarks: 10 MMEA, 23 MMA, and 31 RS; collection cutoff was 8 September 2026. | §4; Table 2 |
| Act receives the strongest explicit coverage | Explicit in 10/10 MMEA, 16/23 MMA, and 29/31 RS benchmarks under the authors’ labeling protocol. | §4.4.1 |
| Anticipate and Verify receive little explicit evaluation | Anticipate: 5/64 (8%); Verify: 3/64 (5%). Anticipate remains implicit in every MMEA benchmark. | §§4.1, 4.4.1 |
| Explicit task-level Plan evaluation differs across families | Explicit in 7/10 MMEA benchmarks; no explicit cases among the 23 MMA or 31 RS benchmarks. | §4.4.1 |
| Most benchmark metrics provide limited capability attribution | 24 outcome-only, 25 outcome-augmented, and 15 process-diagnostic benchmarks. | §4.5.1; Figure 10 |

These are descriptive counts of benchmark protocols, not measurements of agent competence. They support the authors’ claim that anticipation and verification are poorly exposed by current evaluation in this corpus. They do not establish that systems lack those capabilities or that implementing PAPAV improves task performance (§§4.4.1–4.4.2).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Family comparisons describe the curated corpus rather than estimate the complete literature (§4.4.1).
- **Author-stated diagnostic limit:** Explicit evaluation need not isolate every source of failure; aggregate execution scores can still mix perception, planning, and control errors (§§4.1, 4.3).
- **Author-stated deployment limit:** MMEA benchmarks predominantly use simulation, with limited physical and sim-to-real evaluation (§4.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Capability counts depend on interpretive labeling boundaries. Table 2 supports recounting, but the supplied text does not report annotator agreement or sensitivity to alternative coding decisions.
- **Analyst inference:** Proposed reporting contracts and evaluation criteria are analytical recommendations; their practical benefit is not experimentally established in the visible text (§§3.1–3.5).
- **Source limitation:** The source is truncated during §5.1, with substantial later content omitted; this analysis does not cover the complete paper.

## Open research questions

- **Does agent-side verification causally improve recovery?** The paper distinguishes verdict accuracy from intervention benefit (§3.5.3). A next experiment could randomize verifier availability under matched tasks and budgets, measuring false acceptance, recovery success, and verdict latency.
- **Which predicted representations best support action selection?** Decision sufficiency remains task-dependent (§3.2.3). Compare compact state deltas and detailed rollouts on identical candidate actions, measuring ranking agreement and downstream outcomes.
- **How should physical memory balance persistence and freshness?** The visible §5.1 identifies stale beliefs versus repeated rediscovery. Compare memory revision policies under controlled occlusion and object movement, measuring belief accuracy and acquisition cost.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The visible framework and benchmark counts are well specified, but coverage is incomplete.

Material information unavailable in the supplied text includes most of the open-challenges discussion and conclusion, annotator agreement and adjudication details, sensitivity analyses for benchmark labels, and confirmation of any separate code or corpus release.
