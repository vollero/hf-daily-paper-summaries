# Agent World Model in the Wild: Computer-Use Agent Environment Synthesis

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-22.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.26828) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26828)


## One-sentence takeaway
WildAWM synthesizes executable GUI–MCP–CLI training worlds whose quality-gated tasks improve two Qwen agents across eight external benchmarks, although incomplete result tables and model-based verification limit independent assessment.

## Problem and motivation
Training computer-use agents directly on real systems creates reset, cost, reliability, and security problems; manually constructed environments are expensive to scale. The authors identify a gap in synthesis pipelines that focus on individual applications or interfaces rather than connected workspaces containing websites, services, and local files. They also emphasize that generated tasks need executable solutions, useful difficulty, and dependable reward functions (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Connected world synthesis:** A GLM-5.2 coding agent builds persona-driven, cumulative daily computer states using WebHarbor websites, synthesized MCP services, and local files. Replay checks, service health checks, and a reviewer agent validate each state (§3.1; Appendix A.3).
- **Execution-based task validation:** Tasks require GUI, MCP, and CLI interaction. Six heterogeneous agent rollouts support five gates: judge consistency, initial unsatisfaction, solvability, difficulty, and resistance to misleading counterfactual feedback (§3.2).
- **Training assets and recipe:** The authors report releasing 341 environment–task pairs with executable verifiers. They train two Qwen models through teacher-trajectory SFT followed by GRPO with checklist partial credit, format penalties, and adaptive rollout allocation (§§3.3–4; Appendix A.5). Segment-wise training handles context compaction (Appendix A.6).

Here, “world model” denotes executable synthesized environments; the described method does not train a learned predictive dynamics model (§3).

## Evidence and main results

All findings below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Training transfers across external tasks | Both Qwen models improve on all eight benchmarks; average gains are **10.58 points** for 35B-A3B and **8.21 points** for 4B. The average combines benchmark pass rates and mean scores. | §4, Main Results; Table 5 caption |
| Larger model improves substantially | Qwen3.6-35B-A3B’s eight-benchmark average rises from **42.84 to 53.42** after WildAWM training. | §4 |
| Selected frontier comparisons are favorable | Trained 35B-A3B matches GLM-5.2 on WebArena at **67.26% pass rate** and exceeds GPT-5.5 on Online-Mind2Web: **80.77% vs. 72.22% pass rate**. | §4; Appendix A.1 defines metrics |
| Accepted tasks remain difficult | Corpus contains **341 tasks**, including **273 hard** and **68 medium**; synthesis-rollout success averages **21.3% without hints** and **45.6% with hints**. | Table 4 |
| Frontier difficulty persists with retries | On WildAWM validation, GPT-6-Astra achieves about **60% pass@1** and still fails **27.3% of tasks after eight attempts**. | §5.1; Figure 4; Appendix A.1 |
| Verifiers agree strongly with agent judges | On **100 randomly sampled rollouts**, verifier agreement with the judge majority is **95%**, with **Cohen’s κ = 0.90**. | §5.1; reliability table captioned Table 6, but prose links Table 7 |
| Quality gates improve downstream training | Removing one gate at a time from SFT-data synthesis reduces Qwen3.5-4B’s average across four evaluation benchmarks by **2.60–9.30 points**. | §5.2; Table 7 caption |
| RL adds transfer without better in-domain fit | WildAWM-val pass@1 is **37.3% after SFT** and **36.0% after RL**; net external-task gains over base increase from **93 to 129**, excluding QwenClawBench. | Appendix B; Table 14 caption |

These results support transfer from the synthetic training pipeline under the authors’ evaluation setup. They do not establish human-verified reward correctness, unrestricted real-world reliability, or the isolated benefit of environment synthesis versus teacher demonstrations and the RL recipe. Several numerical table bodies are absent from the supplied text, so the complete comparisons cannot be reconstructed.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported capability ceiling:** No variant shows a clear gain on the hardest task quartile, largely comprising OSWorld 2.0 and ALE tasks. Larger-model experiments remain future work (§5.4; Appendix B).
- **Evaluation scope:** Several benchmarks use subsets; blocked Online-Mind2Web sites and unresolved infrastructure failures are excluded. Results therefore apply to the retained evaluation settings (Appendix A.1).
- **Analyst inference — correlated verification:** GLM-5.2 generates worlds and tasks and supplies both lightweight and agent judgments. Cross-model agreement provides additional evidence, but the reported reliability study supplies no human ground truth (§4; §5.1; Appendix A.4).
- **Analyst inference — possible shared-world overlap:** Tasks are randomly split, while multiple tasks can share personas and cumulative world states. Persona- or world-disjoint splitting is not established, limiting interpretation of in-domain generalization (§4; Appendix A.3).
- **Analyst inference — constrained interface evidence:** Successful validation trajectories must use all declared interfaces; that demonstrates observed usage rather than proving that every task has no alternative shortcut (§3.2).
- **Cost and reproducibility:** The authors use eight B200 GPUs and report substantial synthesis token consumption. Total wall-clock time and full monetary cost are unavailable; many referenced tables contain only captions in the supplied text (§4; Table 4).

## Open research questions

1. **Can verification withstand failures beyond misleading outcome claims?** Agreement with agent judges leaves human correctness and reward exploitation unresolved. A next experiment could combine blinded human audits with adversarially constructed final states (§5.1; Appendix A.4).
2. **Does transfer survive stricter separation of worlds and personas?** The random task split does not establish such separation. Repeat training with persona- and application-disjoint held-out sets (§4; Appendix A.3).
3. **How much benefit comes specifically from cross-interface synthesis?** Gate ablations do not isolate the three-interface design. Compare matched-compute training on single-interface and cross-interface tasks (§§3.2, 5.2).
4. **What enables progress on the hardest workflows?** Current variants show no clear hardest-quartile gain. Test larger students or stronger teacher trajectories while holding task data and evaluation budgets fixed (§5.4; Appendix B).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text explains the pipeline and reports consistent transfer, but lacks complete main-result and ablation tables, several figure values, full hyperparameter tables, uncertainty estimates for training gains, human verifier audits, and total synthesis cost. Table numbering is inconsistent, and actual downloadable asset contents were not verified.
