# Quant-Arena: Benchmarking LLM Agents for Quantitative Trading Research

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-22.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.30113) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30113)


## One-sentence takeaway
Quant-Arena evaluates complete LLM trading-research runs under bounded feedback and sealed historical assessment, finding that iteration usually improves benchmark scores but produces less consistent gains in investment returns.

## Problem and motivation
Existing benchmarks assess trading code, formulaic factors, or sequential trading decisions without necessarily testing the complete research loop: constructing features, revising strategies, and committing an executable strategy for unseen-period evaluation. The authors argue that predictive signals must be evaluated through their resulting portfolios because signal quality alone does not establish profitability (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **End-to-end benchmark:** Models write Python strategies from daily and raw Level-2 market data for a static universe of 3,042 A-share stocks, without a predefined factor library (§§2.1–2.2; Table 4).
- **Bounded research and financial evaluation:** Up to five evaluations provide feedback on 2022–2023; one frozen strategy is assessed on sealed 2024 data. Rankings drive a portfolio targeting 75 equally weighted stocks, with cash accounting, lot constraints, trading costs, and simulated market impact (§§2.3–2.4).
- **Empirical study:** Nine models are evaluated across 38 iterative and 38 one-shot model–effort configurations, with diagnostics of ranking quality, exposure, candidate selection, and trading styles (§3; Appendix B).
- **Public evaluation platform:** The authors report an arena accepting model and agent-harness submissions, with linked run records. The supplied text does not establish unrestricted release of the underlying data or evaluator code (§1; Appendix A).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Market outperformance is rare | Of 38 iterative configurations assessed on 2024, 13 have positive annualized return (ARR); only Sol medium exceeds the gross equal-weight market: 18.4% ARR versus 17.1%, with reported excess of +1.4 percentage points. | §3.2; Table 2 |
| Highest score differs from highest return | Astra xhigh leads total score at 51.29/100, but its 2024 ARR is 10.7%, with reported excess of −6.3 percentage points. | Table 2 |
| Iteration improves scores more consistently than returns | Comparing each model’s highest-scoring iterative and one-shot configurations, mean total score rises from 36.47 to 43.55; sealed-period ARR improves for four of nine models, with a mean improvement of 2.9 percentage points. Efforts can differ within pairs. | §3.5; Table 3 |
| Matched-effort comparisons also favor iteration | Across 38 matched model–effort pairs, iteration produces higher total scores in 34 and higher sealed-period ARR in 26. | Appendix B.2 |
| Average ranking skill poorly tracks portfolio return | Across the 38 iterative configurations, Spearman correlation between RankIC and ARR is 0.09; several best-scoring strategies have stronger middle-decile than top-decile returns. | §3.3; Figure 3 |
| Astra’s cash filter reduces downside and participation | Astra xhigh holds cash on 75% of trading days; January–February maximum drawdown is −1.7% versus −29.6% for the gross market, but full-year excess return remains negative. | §3.4; Figure 4 |
| More reasoning effort is not consistently better | Six of nine models achieve their highest iterative total score below their highest supported effort. | §3.2; Table 2 |
| Proprietary comparator substantially leads | Kepler-1 scores 77.56 and ends 2024 at NAV 1.93 versus the market’s 1.17; its reported Sharpe is 2.98 versus the best model Sharpe of 1.89. | §3.7; Figure 7 |

These results support evaluating executable portfolios alongside signal statistics and research progress. They establish outcomes for the reported historical simulations, not reliable live-trading profitability or general performance across market regimes.

The abstract and introduction claim that no model beats the market, but Table 2, §3.2, and the conclusion identify Sol medium as an exception. “None beats the market” applies to the highest-total-score configuration selected for each model’s diagnostics, rather than all evaluated configurations. Reported returns and excess returns also contain small rounding discrepancies.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Analyst inference—limited generalization:** One selected A-share universe and one sealed assessment year do not establish robustness across markets or regimes (Tables 1, 4).
- **Analyst inference—uncertain repeatability:** Appendix A specifies one run per submission; repeated-seed estimates or confidence intervals for the reported configurations are not supplied.
- **Explicit disclosure—partly opaque scoring:** The out-of-sample score uses an unpublished combination rule. Exact score reproduction is therefore unavailable from the text (§2.4).
- **Analyst inference—comparison boundaries:** Strategy returns include costs, while the equal-weight market reference is gross of costs. Excess return consequently combines selection, exposure, and implementation effects (§2.4).
- **Analyst inference—unclear proprietary baseline comparability:** Kepler-1’s construction, development budget, and information access are not detailed sufficiently to establish a controlled comparison (§3.7).
- **Author interpretation—research-period adaptation:** Iteration improves research-period scores more than sealed-period scores; the authors interpret some gains as specific to 2022–2023 (§3.5).
- **Analyst inference—attribution remains incomplete:** The supplied experiments do not isolate market timing, ranking, trading costs, and feature-diversity scoring through controlled ablations.

## Open research questions
1. **Does iteration improve returns reproducibly?** Single reported runs leave stochastic variability unresolved. Repeat matched-effort configurations across seeds and additional sealed periods.
2. **How much of Astra’s advantage comes from its cash filter?** The trajectory supports the authors’ timing explanation but does not isolate its causal contribution. Evaluate the frozen strategy with the filter removed and a simple filter-only comparator.
3. **Can portfolio-focused feedback improve the ranking tail?** Whole-universe RankIC poorly tracks ARR, and several strategies favor middle deciles. Compare standard feedback with feedback targeting the selected stocks, keeping research budgets fixed.
4. **How sensitive are conclusions to execution assumptions?** Outcomes use one portfolio and simulated-cost protocol. Re-evaluate frozen strategies across portfolio sizes, capital levels, and cost settings.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The protocol and tabulated outcomes are explicit, but conflicting headline claims and incomplete reproducibility details limit interpretation.

Material missing information includes the out-of-sample scoring formula, exact execution-cost and order-book simulation parameters, capital initialization, full prompts and harness settings, repeated-run uncertainty, Kepler-1’s development protocol, and confirmed availability of data, strategy code, and evaluator code.
