# Cache Engineering for Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-22.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.28577) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28577)


## One-sentence takeaway
The authors’ AKV system lets agents drop and reposition KV cache entries without recomputing retained states, reporting higher average task success and lower estimated inference cost than threshold-triggered summarization (§§3–5).

## Problem and motivation
Long agent trajectories accumulate KV states that increase attention, storage, and cache-read costs and can exceed positional limits. Trimming or summarizing earlier messages invalidates downstream cached states, requiring retained context to be recomputed; aggressive reductions can also discard useful information (§1).

The authors identify two gaps: agents lack explicit control over active cache, and ordinary token-prefix caching cannot distinguish states computed under different cache-operation histories (§§1, 3.4).

## Approach and contributions
- **Explicit cache operations:** Alongside token `append`, `drop` removes selected entries from future attention, while `repos` compacts retained keys’ position encodings through RoPE rotations. Logical text history remains intact, and retained hidden states are not recomputed (§3.3).
- **History-aware serving:** AKV accepts full message and operation histories through a stateless API. An extended radix tree distinguishes computational histories, drop-aware eviction preserves request isolation, and segment-batch attention restores missing states within one forward pass (§4; Appendix A.1).
- **Programmable retention policies:** The main policy keeps reasoning cache and recent tool responses while dropping older tool-response cache. Experiments also explore agent-issued cache commands and recursive policy refinement (§§5.1, 5.4).

These contributions concern cache control and serving; the paper does not introduce a new foundation model or benchmark.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Better average task success and lower estimated cost | Across three main models and four benchmarks, authors report **1.7 percentage points** higher average success and **27.7%** lower API-equivalent cost versus Standard summarization; cost is **21.9%** lower versus the lower-threshold Text Engineering baseline. | §1; §5.2; Table 1 |
| Gains vary by model and task | GPT-OSS-120B’s average pass rate rises from **51.0% to 53.1%**, with mean task cost falling from **11.2 to 7.3 cents**, versus Standard. Its xbench-DS pass rate falls from **38.5% to 37.5%**. | Table 1 |
| Higher peak serving throughput and lower latency | On Top-80 long-context SWE-Verified replay with GPT-OSS-120B, AKV at concurrency **14** achieves **369 output tokens/s** versus Standard’s **313** at concurrency **12**. P95 request latency is **28.49 versus 43.16 seconds**: reported **1.18×** throughput and **1.51×** latency speedup. | §5.3; Table 2; Appendix A.1.4 |
| Repositioning matters for BCP | On the first **100 BCP tasks** with GPT-OSS-120B, tool drop with `repos` achieves **62.0%** pass rate versus **55.0%** without it; corresponding costs are **10.9 versus 7.1 cents**. SWE pass rate remains **41.0%** in both conditions. | Table 3; §5.4 |
| Policy refinement finds additional savings | MiniMax-M2.7 policies refined on **20 BCP tasks**, then evaluated on **100 held-out tasks**, reduce per-task cost by **15.6%** and **17.8%** versus Standard at iterations **3** and **6**, while reportedly maintaining Standard’s accuracy. | §5.4 |
| Agent-controlled reduction can trade cost for success | On a **100-task BCP subset**, MiniMax-M2.7 Cache-As-Tool raises pass rate from Standard’s **56.0% to 64.0%**, while cost rises from **5.4 to 5.9 cents**. | Table 4 |

The evidence supports improvements over the evaluated summarization policies, with task-specific regressions and cost tradeoffs. It does not establish universal superiority over other cache-eviction methods. Costs are token-priced estimates covering agent and summary calls, rather than complete deployment expenses (Appendix A.3). Serving throughput counts logical input tokens, including dropped entries; output throughput is therefore a clearer measure of generated-token capacity (§5.3).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Broader policy exploration, training agents specifically for cache engineering, and serving support for linear-attention models remain future work (§6).
- **Scope:** Wall-clock serving results use one model and one hardware configuration. Replay selects the longest-context SWE tasks, and each method replays its own policy-dependent trajectories, so these are not identical token workloads (Appendices A.1.3–A.1.4).
- **Measurement comparability:** High-concurrency measurement windows differ: some AKV runs stop after a completion target, while degraded Standard runs use a one-hour cutoff with unfinished requests (Appendix A.1.4).
- **Analyst inference:** Rolling tool drop does not itself bound total active cache because retained reasoning continues accumulating; very long reasoning-heavy runs need further study (Appendix A.4.2).
- **Evidence precision:** Table 1 supplies no uncertainty estimates or significance tests. Repositioning correctness is argued under exact arithmetic, with finite-precision rounding explicitly acknowledged (Appendix A.1.2).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Several prose references point to mismatched figure or table numbers; notably, drop-target ablations appear in Table 3 although §5.4 cites Table 4.

## Open research questions
- **How should retention adapt to task requirements?** Rolling drop and small-scale policy refinement leave broader generalization unresolved (§5.4). Next experiment: compare fixed and adaptive policies on held-out benchmarks under matched cache budgets.
- **Does cache engineering remain effective over substantially longer reasoning histories?** Reasoning cache keeps growing (Appendix A.4.2). Next experiment: extend trajectory lengths and measure memory, success, and failure points under reasoning-retention budgets.
- **How much benefit comes from each serving component?** The supplied results do not isolate radix-tree extensions, eviction, and restoration individually. Next experiment: ablate these components on matched operation traces and memory budgets.
- **How stable is repeated repositioning numerically?** The correctness argument allows rounding differences (Appendix A.1.2). Next experiment: compare restored and directly executed operation sequences across precision settings and repeated rotations.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text explains the method and reports substantial evaluations, but lacks main-table uncertainty estimates, complete RSI policy implementations and search budgets, component-level serving ablations, and empirical numerical-error measurements for repeated repositioning. Code availability is author-reported and was not independently checked.
