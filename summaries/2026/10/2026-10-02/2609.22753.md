# Replacing Large Language Models with Jev Decision Models for Low-Latency Edge Service Orchestration

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.22753) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22753)


## One-sentence takeaway
Jev reduces interpretation latency and improves deadline completion for fresh edge-service requests with short intent contracts, but wider contracts sharply reduce its accuracy and caching largely removes its latency advantage (§§5.3, 5.6).

## Problem and motivation
Natural-language service interpretation consumes the same deadline budget as admission waiting, payload transfer, and execution. For bounded service catalogs, schedulers need a few selected values rather than generated explanations. The authors investigate whether decision models can replace structured-output LLMs while preserving correct, timely service delivery (§1).

Their identified gap is evaluation across the complete admission path: interpretation accuracy and latency alone do not establish service completion under load, changing catalogs, or cached requests (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Shared orchestration integration:** Convert request text into four to eight typed intent fields, then apply the same validator, admission policy, cache, and scheduler to every interpreter. Waiting, interpretation, communication, and execution share one deadline timeline (§3; Table 2; Fig. 2).
- **Controlled comparison and benchmark:** Introduce EdgeIntent v1, with 8,280 generated-and-verified English requests including development cases, and compare three existing decision models with three hosted LLMs across 33 conditions. Additional references include rules, same-weight generative readout, embeddings, and classifiers (§§4.1–4.2).
- **Catalog and service-level evaluation:** Test request-time service catalogs, unseen options, interface limits, modeled execution, and a real three-worker OCR service (§§4.3, 5.4, 5.6).
- **Cache boundary analysis:** Apply one identical-text reuse policy across backends to identify when faster fresh interpretation still matters (§§3.2, 5.6).

The contribution is integration and comparative evaluation of existing models; the paper does not introduce Jev’s underlying model architecture or training method.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Lower hosted decision latency, with an accuracy tradeoff | From base inputs to 16,384 tokens, Jev p50 rises from 0.274 to 0.418 s versus DeepSeek’s 0.378 to 0.638 s; exact match is 0.910–0.940 versus 0.973–0.990. | §5.1; Table 4 |
| Bundling preserves Jev’s message latency | From one to eight requests/message, Jev p50 changes from 0.272 to 0.290 s versus DeepSeek’s 0.379 to 0.819 s; at eight requests, request-level exact match is 0.955 versus 0.985. | Table 5 |
| Wide contracts limit substitution | Across constraint densities, eight-field Jev exact match is 0.527–0.577 versus DeepSeek’s 0.900–0.930; respective p50 ranges are 0.264–0.272 s and 0.455–0.483 s. | §5.3; Table 8 |
| Implicit-default wording is a shared weakness | On default-baiting requests, Jev exact match is 0.610, GLM 0.653, DeepSeek 0.530, and Qwen 0.470; Jev’s unsafe-locality rate is 0.043. | Table 6 |
| Request-time catalogs support new service selection | At both churn levels in a 64-service catalog, Jev and the hosted LLMs achieve 1.000 seen and unseen service top-1 accuracy. Jev’s unsupported-request F1 across catalog sizes is 0.723–0.929. | Tables 9–10 |
| API savings depend on setting | On clean four-field requests, fees per 1,000 exactly correct decisions are $0.03612 for Jev versus $0.1065 for DeepSeek. At 254 services, they are $0.3798 versus $0.1206. | Table 11 |
| Faster interpretation sustains modeled completion under load | With four interpretation slots, 2 s deadlines, and caching off, Jev’s exact, on-time completion is 0.907–0.953 across 1–16 requests/s. At 16 requests/s, DeepSeek, GLM, and Qwen reach 0.093, 0.023, and 0.003. | §5.6; Table 13 |
| Real-service gains remain bounded by OCR accuracy | Under steady changing text, cache off, Jev correctly completes 92/180 OCR requests (0.511), matching recognizer accuracy; DeepSeek, GLM, and Qwen achieve 0.411, 0.450, and 0.317. | §5.6; Table 14 |
| Caching largely equalizes outcomes | For repeated descriptions with caching enabled, every interpreter except Laya reaches 0.489–0.500 correct OCR completion; reported median request latency falls to 0.06–0.09 s. | §5.6; Table 14 |

These results support replacing the interpreter in the evaluated bounded admission system, especially when requests require fresh decisions and deadlines are tight. They do not establish universal decision-model superiority: accuracy, catalog size, serving configuration, and cache reuse materially change the comparison. Real OCR completion includes output correctness; modeled completion does not. Latency comparisons on shared successes are conditional on both backends succeeding (§§4.4–4.5).

## Limitations and validity risks
**Author-stated limitations:** Requests are model-generated and filtered by model agreement, potentially favoring easily parsed wording. Each case is evaluated once, with one arrival trace per service cell. The real testbed covers three workers and one service family. Sequential hosted runs include changing provider and network conditions. API fees exclude local compute, communication, and maintenance (§6.3).

**Analyst inferences supported by the text:**
- “Unseen services” means absent during benchmark development; absence from model pretraining is not established (§4.2).
- Self-hosted results on an H100 NVL do not establish performance on resource-constrained edge hardware (§4.7).
- Classifier comparisons reflect a small training set and fixed training recipe without tuning, so they do not establish a general classifier limit (§4.7; Table 10).
- GLM retains its lowest reasoning setting, unlike the other hosted LLMs; its latency includes reasoning tokens (§4.7).
- Cache hits can repeat semantically wrong but valid decisions (§3.2). Shared-success latency summaries also omit failures (§4.5).
- The reported zero seen–unseen gap for SemIf under churn is uninformative because it returns no valid outputs (Tables 10, 12).

## Open research questions
- **Do gains persist on independently authored requests across days?** Generated wording and single runs leave this open (§6.3). Collect human requests and repeat matched workloads across multiple days and network conditions.
- **Can wide-contract accuracy improve without losing latency gains?** Eight-field performance deteriorates sharply (Table 8). Test field-specific error interventions and a Jev-to-LLM fallback policy, measuring exact match and deadline completion.
- **How competitive is optimized local inference on edge hardware?** Shared-accelerator contention limits current self-hosted completion (§5.6). Compare optimized serving and bounded extractors under matched memory, power, and concurrency budgets.
- **Where does caching cease to equalize backends?** Reuse experiments cycle a small set of identical descriptions (§4.7). Sweep repetition rates and paraphrase diversity while measuring incorrect reuse, completion, and latency.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported setting-specific findings; **medium** in generalization to deployed edge systems.

Material information unavailable in the supplied text includes Jev’s internal architecture and training data, complete prompts and benchmark examples, raw timing traces, confirmed repository contents and licenses, repeated-day measurements, human-authored evaluation, and total deployment costs.
