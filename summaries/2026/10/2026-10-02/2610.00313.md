# Rules to Tools: Executable Checks for LLM Agents in Scientific Computing

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.00313) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00313)


## One-sentence takeaway
Prepared executable scientific checks improved complete repair from 26/30 to 29/30 across two matched SciCode cohorts, but gains were concentrated in three task IDs and computational costs varied (§5; Tables 5–6).

## Problem and motivation
Scientific programs can execute successfully while violating equations, boundary conditions, or physical requirements. Agents therefore need measurements of scientific correctness during revision. The authors ask whether a prepared checker helps beyond receiving the same written requirements and ordinary Python execution access (§1).

The identified gap is controlled evidence isolating access to an executable implementation while holding scientific criteria, starting programs, model, and budgets fixed.

## Approach and contributions
- **Executable scientific checks:** R2T packages public requirements into callable diagnostics returning the tested relation, probe configuration, observation, and status. Checks cover numerical residuals, boundaries, physical relations, and artifact requirements; separate benchmark evaluators grade final programs (§3).
- **Matched repair design:** SciCode groups share written criteria, probes, tolerances, starting modules, model, and budgets; the tool group additionally receives a prepared implementation. Additional arms provide an initial report or checker source executable through Python (§4; Appendix D).
- **Outcome and cost analysis:** The study measures complete repair, benchmark-native partial progress, model usage, and public CPU consumption across SciCode, PDEAgentBench, MDArena, and AInsteinBench. These panels have different support designs and should remain separate (§§4–5).

The contribution is the scientific-check packaging and controlled evaluation within an established feedback-and-revision workflow, rather than feedback-driven agents generally (§2).

These results establish task-dependent benefits from the complete executable-support package. They do not establish a reliable general improvement or show that detecting the original bug caused the gains: tasks 77 and 11 improved despite initially clean check reports. In the first cohort, removing task 77 eliminates the mean repair advantage (§5; Appendix C).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-reported exposure:** First-cohort checkers were authored after failed starts and earlier context records existed; no blinded authoring log was retained. Task 77 also shares a domain family with a development task (Appendix C).
- **Limited diagnostic coverage:** Initially clean reports occur on failed programs. Successful revisions sometimes address code outside checker coverage; particular probes also exclude higher-degree or other parameter regimes (§5; Appendix B).
- **Small samples:** Task-level uncertainty is wide, and the authors explicitly caution that two continuations per access arm cannot rank delivery forms (Appendices C–D).
- **Nonuniform comparisons:** Flow selection favors tools and changes capture gates. Historical panels retain feedback-trigger conflicts and different support forms (Appendices F–G).
- **Incomplete cost accounting:** Checker authoring and manual-review time were not recorded; some API usage is missing, and initial-report preparation is excluded from that arm’s trajectory costs (§6; Appendices D–H).
- **Analyst inference:** Generalization across models and broader task populations remains unestablished because controlled runs use one stated model configuration and archived failed programs (§4; Reproducibility Statement).

## Open research questions
- **What produces gains when initial checks pass?** Traces show repairs outside checker coverage without isolating the mechanism. A next experiment could compare executable checks with matched nonexecutable reports on initially clean failures.
- **How well do checks transfer without author exposure to failures?** First-cohort authoring was unblinded. A next experiment could freeze checks before revealing candidate programs and evaluate new failure realizations.
- **Do repair gains justify full preparation and execution costs?** Authoring time is missing and CPU use rises. A next experiment could record checker development, validation, and repair costs over repeated reuse.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported designs and outcomes; limited confidence in broad efficacy or causal explanations.

Unavailable in the supplied text are the actual supplement files, complete checker implementations, full trajectory and per-run exports, timed authoring/review records, some interrupted-request usage, and controlled evidence across other models.
