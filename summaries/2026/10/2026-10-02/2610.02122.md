# Argo-Bench: Evaluating Data Agents on Enterprise-Scale Workflows

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.02122) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02122)


## One-sentence takeaway
Argo-Bench evaluates consequential data-agent workflows in a simulated enterprise warehouse, where the strongest tested model solves 34.8% of tasks, but simulator assumptions and grading choices limit conclusions about real enterprise performance (Table 2; §5).

## Problem and motivation
Enterprise analytics requires reconstructing business facts across linked tables, applying statistical methods, and making decisions. The authors argue that existing benchmarks emphasize query generation over public datasets and often rely on fallible answer keys, leaving end-to-end enterprise workflows poorly measured (§1).

Sensitive ERP records are difficult to release. Simulation supplies a coherent warehouse plus hidden ground truth, allowing the benchmark to assess both reconstructed facts and the modeled consequences of decisions (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Enterprise warehouse:** A simulator calibrated using public records, distributions, and economic anchors produces a NYC food-delivery business exported into Oracle EBS tables. The evaluated world contains 235 tables and 7.49 billion rows (§2; Table 5).
- **Consequence-based evaluation:** Agents explore through SQL and Python, then explicitly file forecasts, enforcement decisions, allocations, or dashboard data. Grading uses withheld simulator state, including unseen outcomes and modeled decision costs (§2.3; Appendix G).
- **Benchmark and artifacts:** The authors introduce 210 tasks, evaluate 14 models, and report executable warehouse-only reference solutions. They release a sibling world’s warehouse, tasks, reference solutions, and harness; the simulator and graders remain private (§1; reproducibility statement).

The claimed novelty is the combination of a coherent enterprise-scale warehouse, hidden simulator state, and grading of consequential actions—not simulation or synthetic databases individually (§1).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strongest model leaves substantial headroom | Claude Opus 5.5 at extra-high effort: mean score **59.5**, **34.8%** of 210 tasks scoring ≥95; forecast scores floored at zero as a block. Family-bootstrap 95% CIs: **[52.0, 67.1]** and **[27.3%, 43.3%]**, respectively. | Tables 2, 9 |
| Paired comparisons support the leading model’s advantage | Opus leads GPT-6 Astra by **7.7 points [1.4, 13.7]**; Astra, Sonnet 5.5, and GPT-6.1 Sol are not separated by the reported paired intervals. | Appendix I.1 |
| Forecast intervals under-cover outcomes | Across **4,553 filed series**, **3,346 runs**, and **72 tasks**, nominal **80%** intervals cover outcomes **44.8%** of the time; series share one simulated world. | Appendix I.7 |
| Correct structure frequently hides incorrect dashboard values | **1,171 of 1,750** structurally valid data sources (**66.9%**) score zero on values; dashboard grading is all-or-nothing. | Appendix I.5; Appendix G |
| Objective selection changes modeled business outcomes | On an unhinted **$9.6 million** quest-cut task at extra-high effort, Astra loses **$86,281**, while Opus saves **$3,089,763**; a uniform cut saves **$402,037**. Scores are **0** and **99**. | Table 12 |
| Forecast grades depend strongly on reference scale | Holding **47** June base-pay forecasts and outcomes fixed, changing the reference lowers mean grade from **84.7 to 6.0**, despite median absolute percentage error of **1.57%**. | Appendix I.7; Table 14 |

These results establish difficulty and recurring failure modes within this benchmark. They do not establish real-company performance, reliable behavior across repeated runs, or causal benefits from individual prompt hints. The authors explicitly describe prompt comparisons as descriptive and simulated savings as outcomes under a frozen model (§5; Appendices I, I.4).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** One city, one year, one ERP format, no foreign-currency transactions, and no mixed-business deployment; the warehouse deliberately excludes legacy inconsistencies (§§2.2, 5).
- **Author-stated simulation risk:** Aggregate calibration does not guarantee realistic tails. Membership assumptions are weakly grounded, and simulator simplifications may make tasks easier than real counterparts (§5).
- **Author-stated evaluation uncertainty:** Each model-effort setting has one run per task. Confidence intervals measure task selection, not repeated-run variability (§5; Appendix I.1).
- **Documented task artifacts:** Injected refund shares expose the candidate storefront set, narrowing the intended fraud-search challenge; one kitchen-capacity prompt was underspecified (Appendices I.5, J).
- **Grading qualifications:** Seven fixed forecast references were set with outcomes in view, qualifying the general claim that references precede outcomes. Removing their six tasks changes no setting’s score by more than 3.8 points. Forecast floors distort incentives, and reference scales affect grades (Appendices G, I.7).
- **Reproducibility boundary:** Private graders, simulator, and evaluation world prevent independent reproduction of exact headline results; released code reproduces the procedure on a sibling world (p. 10).
- **Analyst inference:** Overall ranking reflects the chosen mixture of task types and scoring scales; it should be interpreted alongside domain scores and forecast errors rather than as a universal measure of enterprise competence (Table 2; Appendix G).

## Open research questions
1. **How stable are rankings across runs and worlds?** Only one run per setting-task and one evaluation world are reported. Repeat leading settings across multiple runs and seeds, separating run, task, and world variance.
2. **Does cross-table verification causally improve performance?** Successful traces often check definitions against another source, but this strategy is not isolated experimentally. Compare otherwise identical agents with and without required independent checks (§3.2).
3. **How much fraud performance depends on generator artifacts?** Refund injection reveals candidates. Correct the refund-generation mechanism and rerun matched searches with unchanged task objectives (Appendix J).
4. **Can forecast scoring preserve incentives while reducing scale sensitivity?** Reference substitution changes grades sharply, and clipping introduces distortions. Compare alternative scoring designs on identical filings, reporting coverage, absolute error, and ranking stability (Appendix I.7).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the summary of the supplied paper; **medium** in broader validity conclusions.

Unavailable evidence includes real-warehouse transfer results, repeated-run reliability, multi-world ranking stability, independently executable private graders and simulator, and an evaluation after correcting the refund-generation artifact.
