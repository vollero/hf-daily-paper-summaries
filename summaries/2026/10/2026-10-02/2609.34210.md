# RLE-Bench: A Qualifying Exam for Coding Agents as Robot Learning Engineers

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.34210) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34210)


## One-sentence takeaway
RLE-Bench evaluates coding agents across simulated robotics engineering workflows, finding strong performance differences alongside persistent failures in policy robustness and mechanically coupled design.

## Problem and motivation
The authors argue that existing robotics benchmarks mainly assess individual policies or controllers, leaving broader engineering capabilities insufficiently tested: building tools, training policies, interpreting sensors, and designing hardware under resource constraints. RLE-Bench targets these iterative workflows through executable artifacts and physical simulation feedback (§1; Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Broader engineering benchmark:** 51 tasks across nine families and four workflows—interactive control, policy development, perception and estimation, and mechanical design. Four families repurpose existing assets; five are hand-designed (§§3.1–3.2; Figure 2).
- **Artifact-based evaluation:** Each task specifies instructions, development resources, an artifact contract, a private evaluation environment, and a verifier. Harbor environments separate development from hidden evaluation of scenes, dynamics, seeds, or embodiments (§3.3).
- **Hierarchical scoring and behavioral analysis:** The RLE Index averages tasks within families, families within workflows, and then equally weights the four workflows. Evaluation covers 11 model–harness combinations, with capability profiles, resource accounting, and case studies (§4; Appendix B).

The contribution is an evaluation suite and protocol, rather than a new general-purpose robotics controller or learning algorithm.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|

| Leading systems are closely matched in policy development | Whole-body tracking/nanoVLA family rewards are **78.9/63.9** for Astra, **77.6/65.4** for Fable, and **76.8/63.0** for Opus 5. This supports closeness among these systems, not uniform parity across all models. | Figure 8 |
| Development feedback helps but leaves robustness gaps | Submitted policies improve over the first development evaluation in **29 of 36 sessions**. | RoboTwin robustness remains weak | Final hidden-evaluation success rates for Astra are **67.3% open-design versus 18.7% robustness**; for Fable, **78.3% versus 13.8%**. These are distinct tracks with different conditions, rather than a controlled perturbation ablation. | Figure 13; task card, p. 17 |
| Depth helps some estimators, but not uniformly | RGB-only versus RGB-D final rewards are **66.1 versus 86.1** for Astra and **27.9 versus 53.2** for Opus 5; Sol decreases from **13.1 to 8.0**. Rewards include inference-speed deductions and interface gates. | Figure 14; task card, p. 18 |

The evidence establishes comparative performance under the benchmark’s simulation, artifact, and budget contracts. The authors interpret visual grounding as a major contributor to the leaders’ advantage, but the reported comparisons do not isolate it causally. The RLE Index is a composite engineering score, not a common success percentage or proof of real-world qualification.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:** Evaluation is exclusively simulated; scene diversity cannot cover all deployment conditions; the four workflows represent only part of robotics engineering; and planned public release creates future training-contamination risk (§5).

**Analyst inferences grounded in the text:**

- **Model and harness effects are intertwined:** Systems use different harnesses and context-management strategies; no crossed model-by-harness experiment is reported (§4.1).
- **Run variability is unclear:** Some appendix plots explicitly report one selected run. Selection criteria, repeated development-run counts, and uncertainty intervals are unavailable, although individual verifiers use multiple trials or seeds (Figures 9, 12; Appendix A).
- **Scoring calibration remains incomplete:** Four of five motion-tracking threshold sets are marked provisional (task card, p. 16).
- **Summary contracts contain inconsistencies:** Table 2 lists nanoVLA checkpoints and validation loss, whereas its detailed task card requires a retrained Python recipe and evaluates success rate. The main description names Franka Research 3 for mobile-base design, while the task card names Panda (Table 2; §3.2; pp. 17, 20).
- **Cost comparison is incomplete:** Grok and Gemini costs and token counts are absent because of harness incompatibility (Table 5).

## Open research questions

- **How much of the ranking comes from the harness?** Current comparisons couple models with harnesses. Run compatible models across shared harnesses with matched tools, context policies, and budgets.
- **Which feedback improves unseen robustness?** Development gains leave substantial official-score gaps. Compare clean feedback, perturbation feedback, and agent-generated perturbation proxies under equal rollout budgets.
- **Can explicit stability analysis improve mechanical design?** Every evaluated system receives zero static-stability credit. Test access to stability-analysis tools or diagnostic feedback against the existing design setup.
- **Do artifact scores predict physical deployment performance?** Simulation-only evaluation leaves this unresolved. Validate selected controllers, estimators, and designs on matched hardware tasks.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** Task contracts and per-task results are detailed, but causal explanations and ranking reliability are less established.

Material missing information includes repeated-run uncertainty and selection procedures, exact evaluated harness versions and model identifiers, detailed hidden-perturbation distributions, complete machine-readable diagnostics, cost coverage for two systems, and physical-world validation. Public asset availability is not established by the supplied text.
