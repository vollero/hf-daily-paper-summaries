# Architect-Ant: Editable Automatic Furnishing of Architectural Floor Plans

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.10953) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10953)


## One-sentence takeaway
Architect-Ant generates editable furniture layouts using multimodal fine-tuning and rule-derived preferences, with large gains from supervised adaptation but modest, category-dependent benefits from preference optimization (§§4.2–4.4).

## Problem and motivation
The task is to place a requested furniture inventory within existing room geometry while respecting collisions, openings, accessibility, and functional relationships. Editable objects matter because designers need to inspect and change placements directly. The authors identify a shortage of real architectural floor plans with furniture-instance annotations suitable for this task (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Data and representation:** AntPlan-270 contains 270 residential plans converted into room geometry and manually reviewed detector-generated furniture pseudo-labels. An editable DSL represents furniture categories and axis-aligned boxes in metric coordinates (§3.1; Appendix B).
- **Task adaptation:** Per-room Qwen3.5-9B LoRA adapters learn from text, structure-only room images, and procedural placement traces containing local failure-and-correction examples (§§3.2, 4.1).
- **Controlled preference training:** A deterministic geometric scorer supplies DPO preferences. Main-method pairs differ in exactly one furniture box while retaining identical reasoning traces, isolating the placement change (§§3.3–3.4).
- **Generation and visualization:** The scorer selects among sampled layouts; optional FLUX.2-dev LoRA rendering produces blueprint-style images while the DSL remains editable (Figure 3).

The distinctive contribution is this combination of corrected weak annotations, recovery traces, and localized geometric preferences; the authors explicitly discuss prior structured-layout and preference-optimization methods (§2).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| SFT supplies most of the measured improvement | CubiCasa5K, 100 rooms per type across four types, K=10: overall mean rule score rises from −8.02 zero-shot to 1.02 SFT; average best-of-K rises from 2.04 to 7.27. | §4.2; Table 2 caption |
| Synthetic DPO adds modest rule-score gains | Same setting: mean score reaches 1.42 and best-of-K reaches 7.34; best-of-K improves over SFT in bedroom, bathroom, and kitchen. | §4.2 |
| Visual preference is category-dependent | Gemini pairwise judgments on CubiCasa5K, 100 rooms per type: DPO preferred over SFT for bathroom and kitchen, nearly tied for living room, and worse for bedroom. Exact rates are absent. | §4.3; Table 3 caption |
| Recovery traces and room images help in the bedroom ablation | AntPlan-270 bedroom validation, n=42, best-of-6 rule score: text-only SFT without recovery 4.65; with recovery 5.23; with structure image 5.98; synthetic DPO 6.24. | §4.4; Table 5 |
| Higher verifier scores can accompany poorer layouts | Same bedroom validation: model-pair DPO scores 6.81 versus synthetic DPO’s 6.24, but authors report less stable and implausible arrangements qualitatively. | §4.4; Table 5; Appendix D.3 |
| Frontier comparisons are mixed and budget-confounded | Ours uses K=10 versus frontier baselines’ K=2. Authors report stronger visual preference than GLM in most categories, but a kitchen deficit against Kimi; exact rates are missing. | §§4.1, 4.3; Table 4 caption |

These results support task adaptation and small additional gains under the authors’ scorer. They do not establish uniformly better functionality, matched-budget superiority, or guaranteed validity. Visual judgments offer a separate signal, but documented judge errors and absent preference-rate tables limit interpretation.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** The scorer only partially captures quality and can be exploited; broader preference pairs demonstrate this risk (§§3.3, 4.4).
- **Author-stated:** Kitchens expose ambiguous overlaps and coupled fixture relationships; the visual judge can misread valid dense arrangements (§4.2; Appendix E).
- **Scope:** Quantitative evaluation covers four residential room types. Boxes lack continuous rotation and mesh geometry; original source images are not redistributed (§3.1; Appendices A–B).
- **Analyst inference:** Corrected detector pseudo-labels leave supervision quality uncertain because annotations were not exhaustively redrawn (§3.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Appendix D.1 constructs strict-pair preferences from “validation” prompts. Whether those prompts also enter reported evaluation is unclear, creating a potential contamination concern for that ablation.
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Appendix B describes greedy decoding, whereas §4.1 specifies stochastic candidate sampling; their relationship is unexplained.

## Open research questions
- **Do scorer gains reflect expert usability?** Visual judgments are mixed and sometimes fail. Next experiment: blinded architect comparisons with explicit accessibility and functional-use ratings.
- **Does DPO help under equal sampling budgets?** Frontier baselines receive fewer candidates. Next experiment: compare all methods across matched K values and report quality alongside inference cost.
- **Can richer geometry improve kitchens?** Axis-aligned boxes cannot fully express layered fixtures or continuous orientation. Next experiment: add oriented boxes or fixture relations and evaluate kitchen errors separately.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The method and headline findings are explicit, but supplied text omits most table bodies, including per-category scores, variability, preference rates, dataset counts, and preference-pair statistics. Artifact access, renderer training details, human validation, computational costs, and strict-pair validation/evaluation separation are also unavailable or unclear.
