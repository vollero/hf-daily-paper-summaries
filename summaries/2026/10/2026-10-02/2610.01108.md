# AgSpec: Pushing the Limits of Retrieval-Based Speculative Decoding in Coding Agent Pipelines

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.01108) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01108)


## One-sentence takeaway
AgSpec combines session and emission-aware workspace retrieval with adaptive draft lengths, achieving up to 4.37× and 4.76× autoregressive generation throughput at batch sizes 1 and 16, respectively, on the evaluated repository coding pipelines (§4.2, Fig. 4).

## Problem and motivation
Coding agents repeatedly emit code, patches, logs, and earlier attempts across model calls. Existing retrieval-based speculative decoders miss reusable text when their corpora cover only individual calls or store files in forms that differ from emitted patches. Their draft-length policies also fail to track acceptance differences across agents and turns, wasting verification compute, especially at larger batches (§§1–2).

The authors identify the gap as corpus and draft-length policies around existing retrieval engines, rather than the matching engines themselves.

## Approach and contributions
- **Agent-aware corpus policies:** Separate session, workspace, and static global corpora retain earlier calls and opened artifacts. Workspace indexing includes raw files and scaffold-specific emission forms, such as diff-prefixed lines. Retrieval favors live corpora unless a global match exceeds them by a margin (§3.1).
- **Adaptive draft-length control:** Offline trajectory replay supplies model- and agent-specific caps; online verification feedback updates a scale applied to match length. The quantile interpretation is explicitly idealized (§3.2, §G.1).
- **Engine-independent integration and evaluation:** The framework runs over SAM-Decoding and SuffixDecoding without changing their retrieval structures. Experiments and ablations examine corpus scope, emission forms, length control, and broader coding settings (§§4.1–4.4).

The contribution is the combination of pipeline-aware policies; retrieval matching and speculative verification are inherited mechanisms.

These results support generation-stage acceleration in the tested configurations, with ablations explaining complementary sources of improvement. They do not establish equivalent reductions in complete task latency: throughput excludes prefill, and tool execution remains outside the reported decoding metric. Output-distribution preservation is an author claim; task-success comparisons are not reported.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Comparison fidelity — analyst inference:** REST and FastCoder use chain approximations of their token-tree policies; EAGLE-3 uses a depth-three chain rather than its published tree. These adaptations limit conclusions about the original implementations (§A, Table 2).
- **Narrow serving coverage — analyst inference:** Main evaluation uses 30 held-out tasks per repository benchmark, three quantized models, greedy decoding, one GPU configuration, and two batch sizes (§A, Table 1).
- **Controller calibration mismatch:** Reported thresholds were fixed before final runs, but final remeasured break-even probabilities differ from those implied by the used controller targets. SWE-bench also uses identical maximum caps across roles and shares Devstral’s measured threshold across models (Table 3). **Analyst inference:** the experiments provide limited isolation of the benefit of agent-specific offline caps.
- **Idealized controller interpretation — author qualification:** Rounding, clipping, caps, finite continuations, and nonstationary sessions perturb the quantile argument; the scale tracks a moving target rather than converging (§G.1).
- **Statistical scope:** Appendix replay tests use uncorrected multiple comparisons. Main throughput results provide no uncertainty intervals in the supplied text (Tables 4–6, Fig. 4).
- **Deployment concern — authors:** Session corpora can contain sensitive repository content and tool outputs, requiring isolation and disposal after sessions (ethics statement).

## Open research questions
- **How much do offline role-specific caps contribute?** SWE-bench’s reported caps are uniform. Compare uniform caps, profiled role-specific caps, and online-only control under identical corpora and budgets (Table 3).
- **Do gains survive faithful tree-based baselines?** Chain approximations leave this unresolved. Repeat comparisons with native REST, FastCoder, and EAGLE-3 trees under matched serving conditions (§A).
- **How much complete-session latency is saved?** Generation-only throughput cannot answer this. Measure wall-clock task latency, including prefill, tools, and corpus preparation, alongside task success (§4.1).
- **When does cross-session workspace retention help?** The authors leave repeated use of the same repository open. Test isolated, version-aware caches across many sessions on shared repositories (§B).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the method summary and reported generation-throughput findings; **medium** in broader comparative and deployment conclusions.

Material missing information includes a public code/artifact release, exact held-out task identifiers, LiveCodeBench and Terminal-Bench sample counts and corpus splits, main-result uncertainty estimates, complete-session latency and success measurements, and live-corpus peak memory at larger concurrency.
