# Removing the NEEDLE in the Haystack: Backdoor Removal in LLMs via Weight Orthogonalisation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.00348) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00348)


## One-sentence takeaway
NEEDLE uses known triggers and white-box weight edits to achieve the lowest mean backdoor attack success among the tested defences while limiting capability and distribution changes, although refusal-related attacks retain residual success and safety costs (Tables 1–2).

## Problem and motivation
Poisoned LLMs can behave normally until a trigger induces an attacker-selected response. The authors identify two gaps in existing removal methods: inconsistent effectiveness across attacks and insufficient evaluation of changes to ordinary outputs, capabilities, and safety. Their central observation is that removing a backdoor activation direction can also damage refusal because the representations overlap (§1, Figure 1).

The setting is targeted removal after trigger discovery; NEEDLE does not detect unknown triggers (§3.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Backdoor representation:** Estimate a layer-specific direction from triggered versus ordinary response activations, removing its projection onto the ordinary activation mean. For targeted refusal, use the same ordinary response under both prompt variants to reduce refusal confounding (§4.1, Appendix C.1).
- **Protected weight editing:** Construct a refusal subspace from a mean refusal direction and additional SVD directions. Orthogonalise attention and MLP output weights against the backdoor while preserving their refusal projections (§§4.2–4.3).
- **Sequential correction:** Edit layers in order and fit ridge-regression MLP updates to correct refusal-activation drift caused by earlier edits. This uses calibration forward passes without gradient-based optimisation; Gemma requires a first-order normalisation correction (Appendix C.3).
- **Joint evaluation:** Compare removal, accidental triggering, capabilities, harmful responses, and output-distribution shift, with component ablations and limited larger-model/full-fine-tuning extensions (§5, Appendix D).

The contribution is the combination of targeted removal and explicit refusal preservation; the paper acknowledges preceding activation-steering and constrained model-editing methods (§2, Appendix C.3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Lowest mean ASR among tested defences | Across six LoRA attack conditions per model, Gemma ASR falls from **99.50% to 1.67% ± 2.41**; Qwen from **99.08% to 5.00% ± 6.16**. Standard deviations describe variation across attacks. | Table 1 |
| Small average capability and distribution changes | Relative general-capability loss averages **0.48%** on Gemma and **0.72%** on Qwen; clean-prompt KL averages **0.03** and **0.11 nats/token**, respectively, versus **0.38–0.73** for fine-tuning baselines. | Table 1; Appendix B.3 |
| No observed code-injection successes | **0% ASR** for both triggers on both models, each evaluated on **200 held-out triggered coding prompts** with exact target-string scoring. Qwen’s best baselines retain **74%/33%** ASR; Gemma’s achieve **2.5%/0%**. | Table S9; Appendix B.3 |
| Refusal attacks remain harder | Qwen targeted-refusal ASR is **11.50%/15.50%** for BadNet/Sleeper, versus **0.50%/0.50%** for OSFT. These use keyword-based scoring on **200 prompts per condition**. | Table S9; Appendix B.3 |
| Refusal protection reduces, but does not eliminate, safety damage | On Gemma BadNet sentiment steering, harmful-response increase falls from **14.01 percentage points** without protection to **11.62** with one protected direction and **5.83** with full NEEDLE. | Table 3 |
| Limited evidence beyond main LoRA models | BadNet sentiment-steering ASR falls from **100%** to **0.50%** on Gemma-12B LoRA and **5.00%/2.00%** on fully fine-tuned Gemma/Qwen-4B. | Table S4 |

These results support a favourable removal–preservation trade-off in the tested settings, not universal superiority or guaranteed eradication. Notably, §5.2’s code-injection comparison of 74%/33% applies to Qwen; Table S9 shows substantially stronger Gemma baselines.

Safety preservation is approximate: mean harmful-response change is **+3.95 percentage points** on Gemma and **−3.74** on Qwen across six attacks (Table 1). Clean-prompt KL measures teacher-forced next-token similarity to the backdoored model, rather than equivalence of complete generated responses (Appendix B.3).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Accurate trigger and insertion-rule recovery is required. Experiments cover limited synthetic attacks with explicit associations; adaptive attacks and naturally occurring spurious behaviours remain outside the evaluation (Appendix A).
- **Author-stated:** Persistence after further fine-tuning or re-poisoning is untested (Appendix A).
- **Analyst inference:** Broad refusal keywords can count ordinary refusal language as attack success, limiting interpretation of targeted-refusal ASR as exact attacker-target reproduction (Appendix B.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Preserving the backdoored model’s refusal projections is not a guarantee of broader safety. Safety is measured with one classifier and harmful-prompt evaluation set, and targeted-refusal backdoors can themselves suppress harmful outputs (Appendix B.3, Table S14).
- **Analyst inference:** Attack-averaged standard deviations do not establish robustness across training seeds. Larger-model and full-fine-tuning evidence covers only BadNet sentiment steering (Tables 1, S4).
- **Analyst inference:** The edit formula requires a nonzero backdoor component outside the refusal subspace; near-complete overlap may create numerical or preservation difficulties that are not systematically tested (§4.3).

## Open research questions
- **How tolerant is NEEDLE to imperfect trigger recovery?** Exact trigger knowledge is assumed. Next experiment: vary reconstructed trigger strings and insertion positions, then measure removal and ordinary-output changes.
- **Can it remove attacks distributed across several directions or designed to overlap refusal?** The evaluated attacks are synthetic, and refusal attacks already show a trade-off. Next experiment: test multidirectional and adaptive attacks against alternative removal-subspace ranks.
- **Does removal persist through subsequent training?** The authors explicitly leave this unresolved. Next experiment: perform clean fine-tuning and controlled re-poisoning after editing, tracking ASR and safety.
- **Does refusal preservation generalise across safety evaluations?** Evidence uses WildGuardTest and WildGuard labels. Next experiment: evaluate held-out harmful-prompt distributions, jailbreak settings, and independently assessed response harmfulness.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported comparisons and method within the supplied settings; broader effectiveness remains uncertain.

Material information unavailable includes runtime and peak-memory comparisons, repeated-seed uncertainty, systematic near-degenerate-overlap tests, adaptive-attack results, and post-edit training persistence. The text claims an open-source implementation, but its availability and agreement with the reported experiments were not independently verified.
