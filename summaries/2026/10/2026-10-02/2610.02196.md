# InterEvolve: Test-Time Evolution of Reward Programs for Humanoid Loco-Manipulation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.02196) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02196)


## One-sentence takeaway
InterEvolve combines LLM edits and numerical tuning of staged reward programs to raise a frozen humanoid controller’s simulation success from 34.6% for calibrated initial agent programs to 86.5% across eight task families (Table 2).

## Problem and motivation
A pretrained humanoid may possess useful motor skills yet fail on new tasks because its task interface cannot express the necessary contacts, objectives, and stage transitions. Conventional reward revision requires training another policy, making repeated experimentation expensive. The authors instead investigate whether execution feedback can improve task specifications while controller weights remain fixed, and whether successful specifications help later tasks (Secs. 1, 3.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Object-aware motor model:** Train object-conditioned residuals for the actor and forward/backward representations on human-object interaction data, retaining frozen body branches. Rewards become latent prompts for the resulting fixed controller (Sec. 3.3; Appendix A).
- **Reward-program evolution:** Represent tasks as rewards, completion conditions, and tunable constants across stages. An LLM revises structure; CMA-ES tunes constants. Separate search, confirmation, and final evaluation scenarios support selection against a fixed verifier (Secs. 3.2–3.4, C.3).
- **Accumulated task experience:** Store programs, verifier outcomes, and design lessons in a text skill library used during subsequent searches. Experiments examine tracking, goal-conditioned tasks, composite tasks, and selected physical executions (Secs. 4, D.6).

The claimed novelty is this combination of reusable reward-executing control and test-time program search, rather than the invention of FB representations, CMA-ES, or LLM reward generation individually.

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Evolution improves goal-conditioned success | Eight-family macro-average SR: 86.5% versus 34.6% for calibrated initial agent programs and 18.0% for calibrated human programs; metrics averaged over three evaluation runs. | Table 2; Secs. 4.1, D.1 |
| Higher success requires additional search compute | Per task family, full evolution costs 2.1 GPU-h and 0.23 million tokens, versus 0.6 GPU-h and 0.03 million tokens for calibrated initial agent programs. | Table 2; Sec. C.4 |
| Tracking success improves, but ULTRA retains lower position errors | On 50 held-out large-box clips, evolved InterEvolve reaches 72% SR versus 60% without correction and 66% for ULTRA. Its body/object errors are 19.80/24.92 cm versus ULTRA’s 15.68/21.45 cm. | Table 1; Sec. D.2 |
| Staging and tuning materially affect success | Eight-family SR falls from 86.5% to 44.7% with single-stage programs and to 51.6% without CMA-ES. | Table 3; Sec. D.5 |
| Library content helps composite-task search | With three search rounds, full-library outcomes are 8/10 relocation, 4/10 stacking, and 5/10 carry-place-kick, versus 0/10, 0/10, and 1/10 without a library. | Table 5 |
| Unchanged programs transfer unevenly across objects | Eight-family mean SR falls from 86.5% on the search object to 37.5%, 35.9%, and 59.0% on plastic box, small box, and suitcase; these objects were seen during controller pretraining. | Table 14 |
| Physical execution is demonstrated qualitatively | Selected pushing and kicking programs run with onboard sensing and off-board computation; simulation-generated latent sequences are replayed while control uses estimated state. No hardware success rate is reported. | Fig. 4; Sec. D.8 |

These results support reward-program search on the tested controller and simulation tasks. Ablations support staging, calibration, and library reuse, but the fixed-program comparison also involves substantially different search compute. Physical demonstrations establish feasibility for selected executions, not quantified hardware reliability or online physical evolution.

## Limitations and validity risks
**Author-stated:** Search is bounded by the controller’s motor repertoire, state-bank coverage, and available measurements. Simulation and LLM costs prevent real-time replanning during physical execution (Sec. E). Reward tilting is a biased heuristic whose concentration depends jointly on reward scale and tilt (Sec. B.1).

**Analyst inferences supported by the text:**
- Quantitative evaluation centers on box-like objects; dexterous GRAB manipulation is qualitative, limiting evidence for broader manipulation (Sec. 4.1).
- Observation and residual-capacity diagnostics use smaller training budgets than the main model, so their main-model comparisons are not matched controls (Sec. A.4).
- Cross-object improvements in Table 14 use **manual adaptation**, so they do not establish autonomous adaptation to new objects.
- Physical deployment replays simulation latent prompts; the text does not establish live reward projection or feedback-driven program evolution on hardware (Sec. D.8).
- “Carry, place, and kick” actually requires ground pushing followed by kicking, with lifting prohibited (Table 15).
- Composite reporting needs caution: Table 5 gives counts out of ten, while Table 16 uses 64 scenarios and counts a scenario successful if any of three rollouts succeeds. Their numerical relationship is not fully explained.

## Open research questions
- **Can search generalize across object geometry autonomously?** Unchanged transfer is uneven and reported repairs are manual. Next experiment: run the same evolution budget on each new object and compare against unchanged and manually adapted programs.
- **Can physical execution recompute reward prompts robustly?** Hardware uses replayed latent sequences. Next experiment: compare replay against live reward projection under target shifts, pose noise, and interrupted contacts.
- **How much does rare-behavior bank coverage determine success?** Bank diagnostics use an oversampled pool for feet-only behavior (Sec. B.3). Next experiment: compare ordinary replay and targeted banks across multiple rare-contact tasks at matched size and compute.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported simulation comparisons and method description; **medium** for broader generalization and deployment claims.

Material missing information includes hardware trial counts and success rates, uncertainty intervals for headline comparisons, the exact noise threshold used to accept edits, the sampling protocol behind Table 5’s ten trials, and confirmation of public code, checkpoint, benchmark, or program releases.
