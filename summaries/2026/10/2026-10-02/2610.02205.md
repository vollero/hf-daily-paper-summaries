# ROWBench: Do Video Models Render What the Program Specifies?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.02205) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02205)


## One-sentence takeaway
The paper’s PROWBench benchmark finds that explicit visual proxies improve short-video adherence to programmed entity trajectories, while event execution, entity reappearance, and identity across views remain unreliable.

## Problem and motivation
Correct program execution does not guarantee that a video generator depicts the resulting states and interactions faithfully. A plausible video may omit an interaction, misplace a participant, or violate an event timeline.

The authors identify a gap in benchmarks that assess plausibility or instruction adherence without replayable entity states and timestamped event records. They also seek to compare representations and viewpoints while holding world evolution fixed (§1; Table A.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Benchmark:** PROWBench contains 170 evaluation cases, 200 selected camera views, and 600 synchronized proxy videos. Each view has coarse-3D, semantic CWM-proxy, and colored oriented-bounding-box representations derived from shared records. Cases cover short clips, long horizons, multiple views, and a domain-specific gunfight set (§3; Appendix A.1).
- **Evaluation metrics:** Logic-Render Alignment (LRA) checks whether prescribed actions appear in their timeline segments. Interaction Success Rate (ISR) requires both the recorded action and expected end state. Their grounded variants multiply each sample’s score by its entity BBox IoU. Additional metrics assess placement, camera control, reappearance, and appearance consistency (§3.2).
- **Reusable data engine:** The framework separates world synthesis, recording, camera selection, and rendering. Saved states support replay and alternative synchronized observations without rerunning behavior controllers when the necessary information is recorded (§3.1).

This is a benchmark and evaluation framework, rather than a newly trained video generator.

| Event success and placement yield different rankings | On Verified-FF, LingBot 2.0 leads ISR at 0.733 but has BBox IoU 0.327; MiniMax-H3 leads IoU-weighted gISR at 0.334, versus LingBot’s 0.241. | Table 1; §4.3 |
| Domain specialization helps on the tested event | On 20 first-frame-conditioned gunfight cases, PWM achieves BBox IoU 0.602 and ISR 0.850, versus MiniMax-H3’s 0.526 and 0.600. | Table 4; §4.5, T4 |
| Long-horizon spatial fidelity is weak | Across five methods on ten 30-second recordings, BBox IoU is 0.175–0.280 and Reappearance IoU is 0.053–0.131. | Table 2; §4.4 |

| The proxy comparison is inconclusive | MiniMax-H3’s three-proxy study uses ten Verified-FF pairs with matched first frames, seed, and generation settings. BBox IoU spans 0.503–0.514; behavioral differences are not significant by sign test, p ≥ 0.25. | Table 5; §4.5, T5 |

These results support the authors’ claim that placement, behavioral fidelity, and appearance consistency require separate evaluation. They do not establish that proxy conditioning alone causes every advantage: models receive different controls and prompt formats. The gunfight result supports specialization in that setting, but does not establish that dedicated training is universally necessary.

## Limitations and validity risks
**Author-stated limitations:** Behavioral and appearance scores use a VLM judge without human calibration. Camera estimation has nonzero error even on engine renders. MiniMax-H3 supplied all first frames and informed Unverified-FF curation, creating potential favoritism. Challenge sets contain only ten recordings or scenes each (§4.5, T7).

Long-horizon protocols differ between methods, so the results measure complete generation pipelines rather than isolated memory. Returning-state judgments cover only 17–21 events per method. Appearance scores exclude absent or unjudgeable targets and must be read alongside miss rates (§4.4; Appendix B).

**Analyst inference:** Cross-model comparisons cannot isolate architecture or training effects because native controls, resolutions, and prompts differ; some prompt conversions remove timestamps and identity bindings (Table A.3; Table C.1). Engine events establish authored execution, not independently verified physical causality. Also, the 20 gunfight cases derive from ten source recordings, limiting their independence (Appendix A.1; Table A.1).

## Open research questions
- **How accurate are ISR and LRA judgments?** Human calibration is missing. A next experiment could compare blinded human event annotations with judge outputs, stratified by occlusion and event type.
- **Which proxy best binds identity, orientation, and articulation?** The controlled representation study is small and statistically inconclusive. A larger fixed-generator experiment could vary these cues separately (§4.5, T5).
- **What improves recovery after an entity leaves view?** Reappearance scores are low and continuation protocols differ. Compare explicit persistent-state conditioning with a common window-chaining protocol on matched recordings (§4.4).
- **Does joint view generation improve participant identity?** The evaluated views are generated independently. Compare joint and independent generation under identical controls, reporting appearance scores, miss rates, and geometry agreement (Table 3).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the benchmark design and reported descriptive results; **medium** for causal explanations and behavioral rankings.

Material gaps include human-judge agreement, controlled isolation of input and prompt effects, a complete long-horizon protocol specification for every method, and generation cost or latency measurements. The supplied paper calls the benchmark **PROWBench**, while the requested title and metadata say **ROWBench**.
