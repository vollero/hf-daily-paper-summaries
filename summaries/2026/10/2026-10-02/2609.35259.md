# On-Policy or Off-Policy Learning? A Systematic Study of Distillation Dynamics

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.35259) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35259)


## One-sentence takeaway
In controlled language-model distillation experiments, KL direction and learning rate explain performance, forgetting, and update sparsity more consistently than rollout source, although student-generated rollouts improve transfer to harder Countdown tasks.

## Problem and motivation
Comparisons of supervised fine-tuning with reinforcement learning often attribute better retention, sparser updates, and generalisation to on-policy data while simultaneously changing objectives, supervision, and optimisation. The authors isolate rollout source through distillation, where these factors can be varied separately. This matters because continual student generation adds computational cost, so its benefits need to be established independently (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled empirical comparison:** Independently vary student versus teacher rollouts, forward versus reverse token-level KL, and learning rate. Main experiments fully fine-tune Llama and Qwen students with frozen, task-specific teachers on medical, scientific, and arithmetic reasoning (§4; Appendix D).
- **Objective-dependent sensitivity analysis:** Derive token-level gradients and conditional stability results for updates that stop gradients through sampled trajectories. A continuous student–teacher rollout spectrum tests the resulting predictions (§5.1–5.2; Appendices B–C).
- **Characterisation beyond final accuracy:** Compare repeated-sampling coverage, harder-task transfer, subsequent RLVR, and incidental Spanish-style adoption, with ablations for sampled KL, clipping, longer reasoning, and generation temperature (§§5.3–6; Appendix A).

The contribution is a controlled study and analytical account, rather than a new general-purpose distillation algorithm.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| No consistent final-accuracy advantage for on-policy rollouts | After 150 steps, best mean held-out accuracy across MedReason, Science, and Countdown-3 is **72% OnPD versus 73% OffPD**. Forward KL spans **71–73%**, reverse KL **35–72%**, across rollout sources and learning rates; three seeds per configuration. | §4.2, Fig. 1 |
| Learning rate dominates measured forgetting and sparsity | In the same Llama comparison, mean retention-score change is at most **1.3 percentage points** at LR **10⁻⁵**, versus declines of **11.2–14.0 points** at **5×10⁻⁵**. Sparsity at threshold **10⁻⁶** is **85.3–89.6%** versus **51.9–60.0%**, respectively. | §4.2, Fig. 1 |
| Forward KL tolerates a broader rollout spectrum | On Countdown-3, held-out accuracy remains **above 80%** across the tested spectrum; at LR **10⁻⁵**, variation is **5.2 percentage points**. Reverse KL varies substantially and favours student-generated rollouts at the lower learning rate. | §5.2, Fig. 3 |
| KL direction affects repeated-sampling coverage | At LR **10⁻⁵**, student-favoured Countdown-3 configurations have similar pass@1 under both objectives, but forward KL achieves higher pass@10. | §5.3, Fig. 5; Qwen replication in Fig. 16 |
| Student-favoured rollouts improve harder-task transfer | Countdown-3 checkpoints evaluated without further training on Countdown-4E show author-reported **10–15% higher pass@k accuracy** for more on-policy rollouts under both objectives at LR **10⁻⁵**. The text does not clearly distinguish relative percent from percentage points. | §5.3, Fig. 5; Appendix A.4 |
| Initial transfer advantage does not reliably survive RLVR | Some OffPD initialisations achieve the strongest sustained Countdown-4 **training rewards**, despite lower initial accuracy. Curves reflect varying distillation seeds with a fixed RLVR seed, rather than held-out accuracy. | §5.3, Fig. 6; Appendix D.6 |
| OnPD with reverse KL suppresses Spanish-style adoption | Spanish-response rates are **0.0% and 0.1%** at distillation LRs **10⁻⁵ and 5×10⁻⁵**. Other configurations range from **61.7% to 100%**. One training run per condition; GPT-5 annotates 1,000 responses each. | Fig. 24; Appendices A.11, D.7 |
| Longer-reasoning evidence is suggestive | Qwen mathematical distillation reaches a selected peak MATH-500 accuracy of **59.0%** with low-LR OnPD/reverse KL, versus an untrained **53.1%** baseline. Each condition has one run, and checkpoint selection uses the reported evaluations. | §6; Appendix A.3, Figs. 10–11 |

These results support configuration-dependent benefits of on-policy distillation within the tested regime. They do not establish universal equivalence between rollout sources or explain SFT–RL differences causally outside distillation. The theoretical results control one-step expected semi-gradients under stated assumptions; they do not guarantee similar final models or accuracy (Appendix C.2–C.4).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Students are at most 1.5B parameters, teachers remain fixed, and larger models, longer reasoning, and other student–teacher combinations remain unexplored (§7).
- **Limited replication:** Longer-math and Spanish-style experiments use one run per condition; Qwen rollout-spectrum evaluations also use one distillation seed (Appendices A.3, A.5, D.7).
- **Author-stated selection risk:** Peak MATH-500 results select and report checkpoints using the same evaluations, potentially favouring variable learning curves (Appendix A.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Subsequent-RLVR claims establish training-reward behaviour under one RLVR recipe and seed, leaving held-out generalisation and optimisation robustness unresolved (Appendix D.6).
- **Analyst inference:** Retention is an unweighted mean of heterogeneous benchmark scores, and “sparsity” means small accumulated changes rather than exactly zero updates (Appendices D.1.2, D.4).
- **Protocol sensitivity:** Main teacher and student temperatures differ. The MedReason temperature-swap ablation supports low-LR forward-KL robustness but shows substantial reverse-KL sensitivity; it does not test every domain (§A.12).

## Open research questions
- **Does the rollout advantage grow with model scale or reasoning length?** The bounded model regime and single-run longer-math experiment leave this unresolved. Next experiment: replicated matched comparisons with larger students and longer completion budgets.
- **Can likelihood-ratio control stabilise reverse KL?** Appendix C.4 gives a conditional bound, but does not establish an intervention’s training benefit. Next experiment: measure extreme ratios and compare explicit ratio-control strategies under matched rollouts.
- **Which distillation initialisations improve held-out RLVR outcomes?** Existing evidence uses training rewards and a fixed RLVR seed. Next experiment: vary RLVR seeds and recipes while measuring held-out Countdown accuracy.
- **How broadly does incidental-style suppression hold?** Evidence covers one Spanish instruction, one teacher/student pair, and one judge. Next experiment: replicate across styles and model pairs with independent annotation and task-accuracy measurements.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the main controlled findings within the tested regime; **medium** for broader generalisation and mechanism claims.

The supplied text does not establish public code, checkpoint, or prepared-dataset releases; measured wall-clock or compute savings; held-out post-RLVR results; independent language-judge validation; or multi-seed confirmation of the longer-math and style-transfer findings. Exact numerical values and formal significance tests are unavailable for several figure-only comparisons.
