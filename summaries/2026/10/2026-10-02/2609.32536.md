# Do Audio LLMs Listen Before They Act? Diagnosing Acoustic-Context Gating in Voice Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.32536) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32536)


## One-sentence takeaway
VGBench shows that recognizing a spoken tool command does not ensure appropriate execution, while supervised post-training substantially improves controlled action gating without establishing reliable generalization across acoustic scenes (§§4, 6–7).

## Problem and motivation
Voice agents must decide both what a spoken command means and whether it warrants action: bystander speech, quotations, and self-talk can contain valid commands without authorizing execution. The authors identify a gap in evaluations that reward tool recognition but do not test whether acoustic and conversational context changes the decision to act (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Action-level benchmark:** VGBench contains 1,018 items: 395 side-talk recordings, 223 self-talk recordings, and 400 speaker-switch pairs. All use a shared silence/tool/answer interface. Speaker-switch conditions preserve specified words while jointly changing source, distance rendering, and a temporal boundary (§§3.1–3.3).
- **Behavioral diagnosis:** Six raw Audio LLMs and three local training-free adaptations are evaluated with mute rates alongside positive tool-selection controls, exposing recognition and execution-policy failures separately (§4; Table 1).
- **Post-training case study:** VoxGate combines LoRA supervised fine-tuning on VGBench and WearVox with exploratory counterfactual-pair GRPO. Factorized cue tests and an equal-budget cue-balanced SFT control investigate which acoustic cues affect action (§§5–6). The authors present this as learning an action boundary, rather than a new speaker-identification mechanism.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong tool recognition can coexist with weak gating | On all 400 speaker-switch pairs, Step-Audio-R1.1 selects the target tool on 96% of same-speaker and 97% of text-only controls, but mutes only 1% of switched commands. | §4; Table 1 |
| Raw and training-free systems rarely suppress switched commands | Highest raw switch mute rate: Kimi, 14%, with 53%/44% same-speaker/text-only tool selection. Highest adaptation switch mute rate: TwS, 6%, with 90%/82% controls. | Table 1 |
| SFT learns much of the controlled switch boundary | On 80 held-out pairs, joint SFT mutes 73/80 switched commands (reported as 91.3%) and selects the target tool on every same-speaker and text-only control. | §6; Table 2 discussion |
| GRPO adds modest descriptive gains | On the fixed held-out split, switch muting becomes 74/80; side-talk correctness rises from 68.4% to 70.9%, and self-talk muting from 52.0% to 60.0%, relative to SFT. One run, without unpaired-GRPO control. | §§5.2, 6 |
| Source and distance each affect action | On the original 80-command cue-validity set, at near-field rendering and a fixed gap, source change raises muting from 1.25% to 50.0% for SFT and 53.75% for GRPO. Same-source far-field muting reaches 60.0% for both. | §6; Table 3 discussion; Appendix G |
| Cue balancing improves some conditions but remains scene dependent | Across eight scene-consistent conditions, each with 80 commands, equal-weight proximity-rule accuracy rises from 39.22% to 66.88%. Yet cue-balanced SFT mutes only 3.75%/5.00% of same-source far-field triggers at 0/600 ms. | §6.1; Table 4 caption; §7 |
| Downstream tasks remain functional | On the same 384 WearVox items, task-only SFT/GRPO score 63.28%/67.71% overall; joint SFT/GRPO score 72.14%/76.30%. Overall uses binary outcomes, thresholding translation scores at 0.85. | §6.2; Table 5 caption and discussion |

These results support the authors’ claim that command recognition and context-sensitive action selection are distinct behaviors under the standardized interface. They also establish learnability on the held-out benchmark items. They do not establish isolated speaker-identity gating, a robust cross-scene proximity policy, natural-interaction transfer, or a causal advantage from pair-aware GRPO.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated boundaries:** Results use one split and one run per setting; GRPO lacks unpaired and repeated-seed controls (§§5.2, 7).
- **Limited generalization evidence:** The original split establishes item separation, not held-out voice, template-family, or tool-family generalization. The cue-balanced test holds out wearer voices but does not vary rooms, templates, natural bystander speech, or open-set speakers (§3.3; §7; Appendix H).
- **Scoring constraints:** VGBench checks tool names rather than arguments. Side-talk free responses use an LLM judge without reported human agreement (§3.4).
- **Comparison constraints:** Interface compatibility affects raw-model scores; Audio Flamingo 3’s tool-selection zeros reflect a format mismatch. Training-free adaptations are local implementations, not official reproductions (§4; Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference:** The aggregate switch score cannot identify a single learned authorization rule because source, distance, and boundary change together, and distance sensitivity differs between trigger-only and whole-scene rendering (§§6–7; Appendix H).
- **Policy mismatch in the control:** Cue-balanced training includes far-field wearer/tool labels, although proximity-rule evaluation expects silence for those cases; this control probes cue dependence rather than directly training that policy (§5.1; Appendix H).

## Open research questions
- **Does gating transfer to natural scenes?** Room and natural-bystander generalization remain untested (§7). A next experiment could use recorded interactions with unseen rooms and speakers, reporting false execution and false rejection separately.
- **Does pair-aware GRPO outperform ordinary GRPO?** The current experiment lacks an unpaired control and repeated seeds (§5.2). Compare paired and unpaired grouping with matched data, rollout budgets, and multiple seeds.
- **Can source and proximity gating remain reliable across renderings?** Far-field rejection differs sharply across acoustic scenes (§6.1). Train explicitly consistent authorization labels and evaluate source-by-distance conditions across several room renderings.
- **Do gains extend to complete executable calls?** Benchmark tool scoring excludes arguments (§3.4). Evaluate correct arguments and action authorization jointly on held-out commands.
