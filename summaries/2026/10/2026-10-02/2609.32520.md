# When Users Change Their Minds: Measuring and Repairing Intent Drift in LLM Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.32520) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32520)


## One-sentence takeaway
IntentFlux exposes task failures when agents must recover revised user intent from dialogue, while StateForge partially improves performance through active-state maintenance without establishing superiority over matched recap baselines (§§5–7).

## Problem and motivation
Agents must distinguish current requirements from values and constraints that users have replaced or withdrawn. The authors call continued influence from obsolete requirements **intent drift**. They argue that existing multi-turn evaluations measure overall task failure without adequately distinguishing stale-intent effects from other errors (§1).

The practical concern is that outdated requirements can affect final answers, tool arguments, and intermediate plans even after a user corrects them (§§6, 8.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **IntentFlux benchmark:** Converts verifiable source tasks into dialogues containing Add, Delete, and Replace edits while preserving the final task and original grader. Alternative values become superseded “variants”; withdrawn “decoys” are retained only when obeying them changes the grader verdict (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Controlled evaluation:** Uses paired clean and evolving-dialogue conditions, increasing joint stale-information budgets, and a turn-matched no-drift control. Measures normalized mean task score and full-credit current-intent alignment rate, CAR; IDG is clean CAR minus drift CAR (§§3.3–5).
- **StateForge harness:** Tracks active requirements after each user turn, invalidates conclusions dependent on obsolete requirements, and injects the updated state before generation. Separate format tracking and a regeneration gate supplement the state tracker (§7, Figure 2).
- **Two transfer paths:** Studies on-policy distillation into a smaller external tracker and thinking distillation into a standalone agent. These use different configurations and evaluation protocols and are not direct system comparisons (§§7.2–7.3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Increasing joint stale load reduces performance | On the same 627 calibration tasks, Gemini 3.5 Flash mean task score falls from 0.476 easy to 0.384 hard; variants, decoys, and dialogue length increase together. | §§1, 3.3, 5; Table 5 referenced |
| Clean–drift loss appears across models | Authors report positive paired CAR gaps for eight models at every difficulty level; supplied table bodies do not provide model-specific estimates or intervals. | Abstract; §5; Tables 1 and 6 referenced |
| Extra turns alone do not explain the loss | Qwen3.5-122B, General-Test: mean scores are 0.778 single-turn, 0.734 turn-matched no-drift, and 0.469 corresponding drift. | §5.1; Table 10 referenced |
| Interactive evidence concerns partial credit | Qwen3.5-122B, 100 VitaBench tasks: no-restatement rubric score is 0.103 below clean; all three non-oracle conditions have CAR 0.190. Closing restatement masks the rubric gap. | §5.2; Appendix C |
| StateForge partially repairs performance | Qwen3.5-122B, medium-difficulty General-Test: mean score rises from 0.367 bare execution to 0.467; paired gain 0.100, 95% CI [0.030, 0.171]. Deep Agents scores 0.354; OpenHarness 0.392. | §§6–7.1; Table 2 caption |
| Exact state leaves substantial residual error | Same main setting: oracle final-state injection scores 0.549 versus 0.778 clean single-turn, retaining dialogue history. | §7.1 |
| Matched recaps challenge mechanism attribution | Separate 125-case component re-run: full StateForge mean 0.428, generic recap 0.403, neutral recap 0.441; both paired recap differences have intervals including zero. Oracle mean 0.551, gain 0.123 [0.044, 0.202]. | §7.4; Appendix D, Table 12 |
| Tracker adaptation improves the modular stack | With a frozen 122B generator, one OPD iteration improves the 2B tracker stack’s full-episode score from 0.266 to 0.445; reference 122B tracker scores 0.428 under that protocol. These are not the main task-score protocol. | §7.2; Appendix D |
| Standalone distillation improves drift performance with a clean tradeoff | General-Test mean task score for the 35B agent rises from 0.296 to 0.461 on drift; clean score falls from 0.745 to 0.718. | §§7.2–7.3; Appendix D |

The evidence supports a performance cost associated with recovering current intent and a partial benefit from the complete StateForge system. It does **not** attribute every failed task to a specific stale item, isolate variants from decoys, or establish that structured state representation outperforms equally resourced recaps (§§3.1, 7.4). Mean score mixes native metrics and supports comparisons only under the fixed benchmark composition (§4).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ limitations:** Intent is modeled as finite atomic goals; construction couples edit types and length; wording and ordering remain potential factors. Harness and interactive experiments each use only Qwen3.5-122B, with replayed trajectories for harness comparisons (§8.3).
- **Authors’ attribution caveat:** Component ablations do not isolate explicit state representation as the source of improvement. Exact-state injection does not establish tracking as the dominant cause of the clean–drift gap (§7.4).
- **Evaluation sensitivity:** BFCL improves under increased load, while SummHay remains near a low-score floor. ToTTo and SummHay attain no full-credit successes in the reported family IDG comparison, limiting CAR’s sensitivity there (§5.1; Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference:** Simulator validity remains imperfectly characterized because quality checks cover sampled utterances rather than complete traces. The reported audit finds 96% fidelity and 97% coherence across 100 utterances; final-intent recovery succeeds for 95% of General-Test (§3.2; Appendix E).
- **Reproducibility gap:** Several numerical tables and implementation details are referenced but absent from the supplied text.

## Open research questions
1. **Which edits cause the largest losses?** Joint budgets prevent separate attribution. A factorial experiment could vary variants, decoys, ordering, and length independently (§§3.3, 8.3).
2. **Does structured state beat matched recap at sufficient statistical power?** Current paired intervals include zero. A larger component-matched study could compare task success and item-level state accuracy (§7.4).
3. **Why does exact state fail to recover clean performance?** The oracle retains history, leaving residual causes unresolved. Compare exact-state generation with history retained, removed, or selectively filtered (§7.1).
4. **How broadly do the mitigations transfer?** Harness and interactive studies use one backbone, and training-scale behavior remains open. Evaluate additional backbones and unseen edit regimes under a shared protocol (§§7.3, 8.3).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied prose supports the central findings and caveats, but most referenced tables contain only captions. Missing information includes model-specific CAR/IDG values and intervals, complete per-family results, tracker-scale estimates, detailed prompts and training settings, serving and seed configurations, resource costs, and a confirmed accessible release of code, data, or trained models.
