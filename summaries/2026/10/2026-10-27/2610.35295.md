# Did the Model Use That? Counterfactual Training Boosts Self-Attribution in Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.35295) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35295)


## One-sentence takeaway

CREDIT improves final-answer disclosure of external information’s measured influence through automatic counterfactual supervision, but transfer remains uneven and the results do not establish introspection (§§6–7; Limitations).

## Problem and motivation

A model can agree with supplied information either because that information changed its answer or because it would have answered identically anyway. The authors argue that useful self-attribution must distinguish these cases, avoiding both undisclosed influence and unnecessary attribution (§1).

Their identified gap is that supporting citations and acknowledgment in reasoning do not necessarily reveal dependence in the final answer users see. They therefore evaluate attribution against a removal intervention rather than source support or answer agreement (§§2–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Behavioral evaluation:** Remove each external information piece and sample four counterfactual answers. A piece is influential when the original answer matches its supported answer but at most one counterfactual does; it is non-influential when the original answer disagrees or at least three counterfactuals match. Intermediate cases are unscored. Balanced accuracy counts omissions and false attributions equally (§3).
- **CREDIT training:** Generate model responses, automatically measure influence, and rewrite only the final attribution statement while preserving the answer and reasoning. Apply one epoch of LoRA fine-tuning across user suggestions, passages, tool outputs, and another AI’s answers (§4).
- **Empirical characterization and transfer:** Compare eleven prompted frontier models and four fine-tuned open models; test reasoning supervision, prompting, shuffled labels, unseen information types, benchmark passages, and conflicting sources (§§5–7).

The evidence supports improved prediction and disclosure of **removal-defined answer dependence**, including cases where agreement alone is uninformative. It does not establish internal causal access, improved factual correctness, reduced susceptibility to external influence, or better user decisions. Under competition, source-aware answer matching still matches or exceeds CREDIT overall, although CREDIT distinguishes dependence within the agreeing subset (Appendix K.8).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ scope limitation:** Reinforcement without answer change counts as non-influential; answer changes that do not adopt the supplied answer also fall outside the positive label (§3.1; Limitations).
- **Authors’ generalization limitation:** Evaluation centers on English short-answer questions and small trained models. Transfer weakens with paraphrasing, fails for system-prompt notes, and depends on training-type coverage (§7; Limitations).
- **Measurement risk:** Labels depend on a small counterfactual sample and LLM judgments. Human checks and threshold analyses support robustness, but the error audit identifies semantic-matching mistakes (Appendices F and I).
- **Selection and replication risk:** DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B was selected using Test-set screening; its Fresh and Large results are cleaner estimates. Most models have one training seed (Appendix G).
- **Comparison qualification:** VFT uses one counterfactual answer in the Table 1 comparison, versus CREDIT’s four. A CREDIT one-sample ablation retains most of the gain, but this is not a perfectly matched label-budget comparison (Table 1 caption; Appendix K.1).
- **Authors’ explicit exclusions:** The incomplete RL variant cannot establish superiority over the referenced RL method. User effects and training on system-prompt notes remain untested (Limitations).
- **Analyst inference:** Since models receive their own influence labels, cross-model scores compare different behavioral case populations. Shared-population checks help, but do not make every cross-model result a comparison on identical labels (Appendices D and I).

## Open research questions

1. **Can direct coverage fix system-prompt attribution?** Such notes were excluded from training and remain poorly disclosed. Train a matched condition including them, then evaluate unseen wording and placement (Limitations).
2. **Does CREDIT work for open-ended, interacting evidence?** Supported answers are mostly known by construction, and jointly necessary sources require additional interventions. Test long-form responses with extracted answer claims and source-combination removals (Limitations; Appendix K.9).
3. **Does self-attribution reveal model-specific information beyond prompt cues?** Prompt-only classifiers already predict influence substantially. Evaluate dependence that matched prompt-only predictors cannot reliably infer (Appendix I).
4. **Do users benefit from these disclosures?** No user study was conducted. Compare calibrated attribution, inaccurate attribution, and no attribution on user verification and trust decisions (Limitations; Ethical Considerations).

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported behavioral improvements; **medium** for broad generalization.

The supplied text omits many table bodies, including complete per-model baseline results, training-example counts, API identifiers, and external-information templates. Artifact availability and contents cannot be verified from the text alone. Evidence for larger trained models, open-ended deployment, multilingual transfer, and user outcomes is unavailable.
