# CRM-Agent-Bench: Benchmarking Tool-Using Agents in Stateful Enterprise Environments

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.32004) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32004)


## One-sentence takeaway
CRM-Agent-Bench evaluates stateful enterprise agents through task-specific execution contracts, finding that response-only grading overstates strict success by 10.5–11.0 percentage points for four evaluated systems (§4.2, Figure 6).

## Problem and motivation
Agents with authority to modify business records must execute the correct transaction, follow required procedures, and preserve unrelated state. The authors argue that existing evaluations cover these requirements incompletely, allowing convincing answers or apparently correct actions to conceal failed transactions (§1).

The proposed evaluation separates outcome, trajectory, policy compliance, and operational viability, so similar overall success rates do not obscure different execution risks or costs.

## Approach and contributions
- **Stateful benchmark:** 555 synthetic, multi-turn tasks across nine business domains and 36 capability tags, using resettable environments and typed tools without a live CRM backend (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Executable contracts:** Each task selects applicable checks for actions, arguments, ordering, responses, final state, and forbidden effects. Strict success requires every declared check to pass; undeclared dimensions receive no assessment (§2.3, Appendix A.2).
- **Evaluation framework:** On-policy simulated users interact with an agent system; programmatic graders assess execution, while a fixed semantic judge assesses visible responses. Latency and cost are reported separately, and an exposure audit checks whether forbidden tools exist in the reference graph (§2.3, Appendix A.7).

The contribution is primarily a benchmark and evaluation design, rather than a new trained agent.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strict success varies substantially | Sixteen systems, one execution per task: 64.32%–83.60% across 555 tasks; every first-round run has valid grades for all tasks. Gemini 3.8 Flash leads. | §4.1; Appendix A.3 |
| Response grading misses execution failures | For four systems, response-only grading exceeds strict success by 10.5–11.0 percentage points on the same 555-task suite. Partial stages treat undeclared or unavailable verdicts as neutral. | §4.2; Figure 6 |
| Leading aggregate performance is not sustained task-level reliability | Gemini averages 85.53% strict success across three rounds, sample SD 1.66 points; only 434/555 tasks succeed in all three rounds, or 78.20%. | Appendix C |
| Compliance differs from completion | Across 2,640 trials on 165 policy-constrained tasks and sixteen systems, 2,408/2,640 (91.2%) pass negative checks, but 1,764/2,640 (66.8%) satisfy the full contract. | §4.4; Figure 8 |
| Domain profiles differ from overall ranks | Customer-support mean strict success is 56.35% across systems. Muse Glimmer 30B leads that domain at 85/125 (68.00%), despite ranking twelfth overall. | §4.1; Figure 5 |
| Costs differ sharply | Across thirteen priced first-round runs, estimated tested-agent suite cost spans $2.14–$191.00. Gemini achieves 83.60% success for $16.12; accounting excludes simulator and judge usage. | §4.3; Appendix B.1 |
| State checks detect a concrete duplicate transaction | In one Claude Sonnet 4.6 trace, action, argument, order, and response checks pass, but duplicated checkout produces 310 loyalty points instead of the required 155. | Appendix D.2; Figure 1 |

These results establish that the benchmark’s declared execution checks detect failures missed by response grading and reveal distinct reliability profiles. They do not establish deployment reliability, intrinsic model safety, or statistically significant differences among closely ranked systems. The main leaderboard is descriptive; repeated runs provide additional consistency evidence.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ scope boundary:** Evaluation uses synthetic, headless, structured-tool environments. Human validation and broader visual, voice, document-rich, and live-service evaluation are described as ongoing extensions.
- **Incomplete state coverage:** Only 200/555 tasks declare expected or forbidden state checks, including 45 with protected-state assertions. Strict success therefore establishes satisfaction of heterogeneous contracts, not comprehensive state integrity (Appendix A.2).
- **Safety attribution:** A prohibited tool may remain unused because of refusal, inaction, or routing. The graph-wide exposure audit does not establish turn-level exposure or intrinsic model safety (§5, Appendix A.7).
- **Judge error:** The authors identify a semantic-judge contradiction false negative in the duplicate-checkout example (Appendix D.2).
- **Simulator validity:** An earlier, non-leaderboard trace shows simulator credential drift contributing to a retry loop. Its frequency in the reported leaderboard is not established (Appendix D.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Without controlled scaffold or simulator comparisons in the supplied results, performance differences cannot be attributed solely to base models. Component rates also use different task subsets, and exclusive failure labels depend on precedence (Appendices A.7 and B.2–B.3).
- **Operational accounting:** Costs exclude simulator and judge usage; self-hosted runs lack comparable prices. Reported latency does not isolate provider or implementation effects (§4.3).

## Open research questions
1. **How sensitive are rankings to simulated-user behavior?** The reference protocol uses one simulator, and a qualitative trace shows credential drift. A next experiment could repeat matched tasks with alternative simulators and human users, comparing strict success and dialogue deviations.
2. **How much does scoped routing contribute to compliance?** Graph-wide reachability does not establish actual exposure. Compare the same models under scoped routing and a full tool catalog, logging turn-level exposure and prohibited attempts.
3. **What failures remain outside declared state checks?** Most tasks lack explicit state assertions. Add broader state-difference checks to a matched task subset and measure newly detected unintended effects.
4. **Can response judging reliably detect contradictions?** Appendix D.2 documents a missed contradiction without quantifying prevalence. Human-audit sampled transcripts and compare judge variants on contradiction detection and strict outcomes.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the benchmark design and principal reported findings, but several referenced tables contain captions without their numeric bodies, and figures are not fully transcribed.

Material gaps include the complete leaderboard and per-model repeated-run values, full protocol settings, detailed contract inventories, measured simulator/judge sensitivity, human-validation results, and a direct benchmark repository URL.
