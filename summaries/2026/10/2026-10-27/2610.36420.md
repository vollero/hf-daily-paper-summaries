# SO-101 Bench: Accessible Spatial Manipulation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.36420) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36420)


## One-sentence takeaway

SO-101 Bench preserves the observed real-robot ranking of five simulation-trained systems in a small matched study, while exposing weaknesses in spatial placement and object generalization and a substantial success–latency tradeoff for its leading LLM agent.

## Problem and motivation

The author identifies a gap between accessible SO-101 hardware and accessible evaluation: existing physical benchmarks require costly rollouts, while available policies generally need task-specific adaptation for spatial, language-conditioned manipulation. A simulated benchmark could make evaluation repeatable and cheaper, provided its policy rankings predict hardware performance. The paper also seeks to distinguish grasping competence from relational placement, unfamiliar-object handling, and planning (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Benchmark and datasets:** An Isaac Lab digital twin supports bin placement, next-to placement, between placement, and directional movement. Separate single- and four-object bin conditions yield five task families. Evaluation covers 138 rigid objects across seen, unseen-instance, and unseen-class splits, with automatic scoring and ROBUST, PLAN, and MEM subsets (§3).
- **Two forms of sim–real validation:** Independently trained real-only and sim-only GR00T policies test correspondence in condition difficulty and failure composition; identical-weight deployments of five systems test whether rankings survive transfer (§§4–5).
- **Controlled leaderboard:** Nine learned policies receive the same simulated demonstrations and fine-tuning compute budget. Two LLM agents combine an existing code-as-policy harness with a learned grasping policy (§6).
- **Harness and model analysis:** Paired ablations examine direct motion tools, code-based control, and delegation of grasping. The contribution is this benchmark-specific integration and evaluation, rather than a newly introduced foundation model or code-as-policy paradigm (§7).

The evidence supports ranking correspondence **within the tested setup**, not general simulator validity or accurate absolute success prediction. The hybrid’s large simulation advantage is supported by paired harness comparisons; its smaller hardware advantage is not statistically established by the reported test. The equal-budget leaderboard measures performance under a resource constraint rather than maximum attainable capability.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Hardware validation covers five systems, 45 layouts, and one real environment; the benchmark uses one arm and rigid objects (§8).
- **Author-stated simulation mismatch:** A lighter physical bin and simulated downscaling of some objects help explain optimistic simulation results. The paper’s “upper bound” interpretation is empirical for this study, not a demonstrated universal bound (§5).
- **Author-stated training constraint:** Equal compute produces unequal training exposure; some weak entries appear under-trained (§6; Table 13).
- **Author-stated timing constraint:** Agent computation pauses control-time accounting, hiding deployment latency in success scores (§§5, 7–8).
- **Analyst inference:** ROBUST appearance groups use different layouts, so their differences cannot isolate the causal effect of visual changes; Table 11 explicitly identifies this lack of pairing.
- **Analyst inference:** Harness ablations use selected easy/hard episodes rather than a representative random sample. Some non-Sol direct-tool runs stop early, limiting complete cross-model comparisons (Table 14).
- **Evidence caution:** Figure 20’s automated failure analysis explicitly awaits validation against human labels; it should not support firm diagnostic conclusions.

## Open research questions

1. **Do rankings survive changes in hardware and environment?** Only one real environment is tested. Repeat matched deployments across independently calibrated arms, rooms, and additional policies.
2. **How much does unequal training exposure explain leaderboard gaps?** Some models remain under-trained. Compare fixed-compute results with convergence-based learning curves and sample-matched training.
3. **Does the hybrid retain its advantage under practical deadlines?** Computation pauses are excluded from task limits. Evaluate success under shared wall-clock deadlines and report time to successful completion.
4. **Which components drive PLAN and MEM gains?** Aggregate subsets do not isolate perception, memory, planning, and grasping. Ablate these components on matched layouts while holding the language model fixed.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** in the descriptive summary; **medium** in broader transfer conclusions.

Material gaps include cross-site replication, repeated-rollout variability, convergence-trained comparisons, controlled component ablations, complete pretraining exposure for several policies, and validated automated failure labels. The supplied text names released assets and checkpoint revisions, but does not establish their actual accessibility or independently verify reproducibility.
