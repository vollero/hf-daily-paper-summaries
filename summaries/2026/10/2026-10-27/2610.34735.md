# iPrefBench: A User-Centric Benchmark for Personalized Mobile Use Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.34735) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34735)


## One-sentence takeaway
iPrefBench separates history-inferable preferences from details requiring clarification, revealing that strong mobile task execution does not ensure correct personalization and that encouraging questions improves success while increasing unnecessary requests (§§3–4).

## Problem and motivation
Personal mobile agents must resolve underspecified requests using both past activity and current user input. Guessing unavailable information can produce incorrect outcomes, while asking about established preferences burdens the user.

The authors identify an evaluation gap: existing benchmarks do not jointly provide history, interactive clarification, and explicit annotations of which missing details require each source, preventing assessment of whether questions are necessary (§§1–2; Table A.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Benchmark with controlled information sources:** Each omitted requirement has an intended value and exactly one annotated source: history-inferable or ask-only. Tasks also test conflicting instructions, changing preferences, and unavailable preferred options (§§3.1, 3.3).
- **Executable personalized environment:** Built on iOSWorld, the benchmark contains 130 tasks across 26 mock apps, grounded in one fictional persona and 3,051 activity events. Agents use screenshots, GUI actions, and `ask_user`; a profile-grounded simulator supplies requested information (§§3.2–3.5).
- **Outcome and interaction evaluation:** Rubrics distinguish action completion from personalization; full success requires every criterion to pass. Interaction recall measures coverage of ask-only requirements, while precision penalizes inferable, irrelevant, and repeated requests (§3.4; Appendix E).
- **Diagnostic experiments:** Seven agents share an evaluation harness; additional studies examine pre-execution clarification, history exposure, and personalization failures (§4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Execution exceeds personalized success | Across seven agents under the shared full-history harness, action completion is 77.5–91.6%, versus full task success of 26.2–47.7%. Claude Opus 5 achieves 91.6% action completion, 69.4% personalization, and 47.7% success. | Table 1; §§4.1–4.2 |
| Ask-only decisions are harder | Opus 5 scores 76.3% on inferable personalization versus 51.3% on ask-only personalization. Across agents, interaction recall is 10.6–44.2% and precision is 60.4–77.1%. | Table 1 |
| Clarification improves success but reduces precision | Adding pre-execution clarification raises Sonnet 5 success from 39.2% to 53.8% and recall from 25.7% to 85.0%, while precision falls from 60.4% to 42.5%. Qwen3.8-27B shows the same directional tradeoff: success 26.2%→35.4%, recall 21.2%→69.0%, precision 70.6%→60.9%. | Table 2; §4.4 |
| Relevant history helps inference more than clarification | For Qwen3.8-27B, inferable personalization rises from approximately 35% without history to 63% with the top-50 retrieved events. The authors report a subsequent plateau; ask-only performance barely changes, while recall declines from 33.6% to 21.2% with greater exposure. | §4.4; Figure 4 |
| Simulator and judges receive supporting validation | Simulator responses pass grounding and scope checks in 86/89 interactions (96.6%) from four agents. On 100 sampled trajectories, judge–human agreement is 95.7% for rubric criteria and 97.0% for task success, with κ=0.89 and 0.94 respectively. | §4.5; Appendices C.4, E.4 |

Completion scores are macro-averaged across tasks; interaction recall and precision are micro-aggregated over requirements and requests (Appendix E). These results establish a personalization and clarification gap within this constructed environment. They do not directly measure real-user satisfaction or establish performance across diverse users.

There is a reporting inconsistency: the introduction and §4.2 state maximum recall of 44.3%, whereas Table 1 reports 44.2%; the table value is used above.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Scope—analyst inference:** One fictional persona and mock apps limit evidence for generalization to diverse users and real applications (§3.2; Appendix C).
- **Constructed certainty—analyst inference:** Reviewers strengthen weak or mixed historical evidence and assign each requirement one resolution source. This supports clear scoring but leaves ambiguous preferences and partially inferable decisions underexplored (§3.3; Appendix D.1).
- **Interaction proxy—analyst inference:** Precision treats requests for inferable details as unnecessary; the study does not establish whether real users might welcome confirmation or how request counts translate into perceived burden (§3.4).
- **Model coverage—authors’ reported constraint:** Specialized GUI agents were excluded because of context limits or invalid actions under full-history input, restricting the main comparison (Appendix F.1).
- **Validation limits—analyst inference:** Simulator validation covers a subset of agents. Human judge validation assigns disjoint trajectory subsets to individual annotators, so inter-human agreement is not established (Appendices C.4, E.4).

## Open research questions
- **Can agents improve recall without sacrificing precision?** Pre-execution clarification worsens precision (Table 2). Compare it with a policy that explicitly tracks unresolved decisions and their evidence sources.
- **Does the infer-versus-ask distinction generalize across users?** Only one persona is evaluated. Repeat matched tasks across multiple personas, including ambiguous and conflicting histories.
- **Does metric improvement predict user satisfaction?** Interaction necessity is assessed against annotations rather than user judgments. Run a human study comparing success, perceived burden, and satisfaction under different clarification policies.
