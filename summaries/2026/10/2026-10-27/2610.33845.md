# Build, Don&#x27;t Play: Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop a Hearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.33845) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33845)


## One-sentence takeaway
The authors report that LLM research agents built a simulator and PPO player with strong live Battlegrounds results, although the small, indirect top-player comparison and lack of research-system ablations limit broader claims.

## Problem and motivation
Can LLM agents develop a competitive system from raw game data through deployment, rather than solve isolated research benchmarks? Battlegrounds requires a simulator covering interacting mechanics, partial observability, stochastic outcomes and long decision sequences. The authors identify a missing full-game training environment and a gap in evidence that research agents can produce systems validated against people in a changing real environment (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **AutoTavern:** Specialised research agents coordinate through versioned research memory. Build, compatibility, comparison, held-out evaluation and live-replay gates check changes; detected mistakes become persistent lessons or additional checks. Humans set goals, resolve ambiguous mechanics and execute the policy’s named shortcuts during live play (§4; Appendix A).
- **TavernGym:** A deterministic Rust simulator implementing the target patch’s full content, with Python bindings, legal-action masks, replay and batched training interfaces. Fidelity is checked through mechanic tests and live-game discrepancies (§5; Appendix B).
- **TavernAgent:** An entity-transformer policy trained with standard PPO against opponent libraries calibrated to encountered human opponents. The contribution centres on constructing and correcting the training environment and opponents, rather than a new RL algorithm (§§2, 6).
- **TavernBench:** Simulator-labelled rule and decision tasks comparing specialised play with direct LLM answers, including latency and simulator-tool evaluations (§7; Appendix E). TavernGym and TavernBench are **promised for release**; the text does not establish completed release.

The evidence supports strong specialised play, useful opponent calibration and substantial engineering output. It does not isolate the causal contribution of research memory or gates. The leaderboard window supports competitive recent performance, rather than establishing sustained superiority over top players. Benchmark results compare whole systems with different interfaces: TavernAgent receives engine state and uses simulator lookahead for some categories; direct LLMs receive text.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** One game, one patch and calibrated library opponents; live execution requires human shortcut presses (Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference—causal attribution:** No controlled removal of memory, role specialisation, gates or reality feedback establishes which research-system components drive progress.
- **Analyst inference—live comparison:** Policies change during the ladder climb. Client failures materially affect results, and the strongest top-player claim rests on a short window of inferred placements rather than matched head-to-head trials (Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference—benchmark dependence:** Labels rely heavily on TavernGym; strategic labels depend on TA-21 continuations and exam-lobby states. Hero/trinket labels use human averages despite reported differences between agent and human hero strengths (Appendices C, E).
- **Analyst inference—latency and budget sensitivity:** LLM timings are estimates, with fallback medians for unlisted models. Output-budget overruns also affect accuracy, so results establish performance under this protocol (§7; Appendix E).
- **Resource constraint:** The study used eight H200 GPUs for main-line training and about 100 million research-agent output tokens; accessibility under smaller budgets remains untested (Appendix F).

## Open research questions
1. **Which research-process components cause improvement?** Their effects are not isolated. Run matched-budget projects removing memory, gates or live feedback separately.
2. **Does top-player competitiveness persist?** The strongest comparison uses a short tracker window. Freeze a policy and collect a longer prospective evaluation with logged placements and failures.
3. **How well does the system adapt across patches?** Only one patch is evaluated. Introduce a later patch and measure repair effort, simulator discrepancies and recovered live performance.
4. **Does benchmark success generalise beyond its labelling system?** Simulator and continuation-policy dependence remain. Evaluate independently checked live-state tasks and alternative continuation policies.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text provides detailed methods and results, but material gaps include controlled research-system ablations, independent simulator-fidelity measurements, uncertainty for headline live comparisons, exact leaderboard cut-off and server identification, the precise LLM output-budget cap, and confirmation that promised assets are publicly available.
