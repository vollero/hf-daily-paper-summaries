# Joint or Per-Option? Compact Encoders for Closed-Set Decisions from 17 Million to 1 Billion Parameters

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.30970) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30970)


## One-sentence takeaway
At 32M parameters, per-option scoring improves closed-set decision accuracy over joint scoring only with reranker initialisation in the tested settings, trading order invariance for substantially higher mean and tail latency (§5).

## Problem and motivation
The paper studies compact encoders that return probabilities over changing sets of allowed answers, including tool choices and classification labels. Joint readers process all options together cheaply; per-option readers repeatedly encode the context but avoid option-order dependence. The authors identify a lack of controlled comparisons between these designs for typed decisions, alongside uncertainty about how small useful decision encoders can be (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled reader study:** Compare joint and per-option readers at 32M parameters with shared data, heads, objectives and teacher targets, using three seeds per arm, step/compute controls, and a reader-by-initialisation experiment (§5).
- **Crivo model family:** Release six joint encoders spanning 17M–1B parameters, fine-tuned from Ettin rerankers. Each option marker receives a logit, normalised across allowed options; smaller models also use distillation (§§4.1–4.4).
- **Evaluation and benchmark diagnosis:** Audit external benchmark overlap, measure robustness, calibration and latency, and introduce Prumo, a parameter-free rule engine that probes what the private leaderboard rewards (§§3, 6–7).

The typed-decision interface and option-marker readout are explicitly inherited from prior work; the contributions are the controlled evidence, trained models and diagnostic evaluation (§1.1).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Per-option wins with reranker initialisation | At 32M, three seeds per arm: public-suite sample skill improves by **0.058**, 95% Welch CI **[0.050, 0.065]**, against the step-matched joint control; by **0.028 [0.015, 0.042]** against the approximately compute-matched control. | §5.2; Table 3 |
| Unseen-task reader gains remain uncertain | Against the compute control: unseen S1-Arena task-mean accuracy difference **+0.011 [−0.009, +0.031]**; unseen BTZSC macro-F1 difference **+0.025 [−0.034, +0.084]**. | §5.2 |
| Initialisation changes the reader result | From the raw encoder, compute-matched public-suite skill difference is **−0.011 [−0.038, +0.015]** for per-option minus joint; intervals include zero on all three evaluation sets. | §5.2; Fig. 4 |
| Compact joint models beat the principal baseline on all S1-Arena tasks | On **209 tasks**, Crivo-68M/150M achieve task-mean accuracy **0.466/0.475**, with reported paired differences **+0.049/+0.059** versus 435M DeBERTa zs-v2; both survive task-level Holm correction. | §6.1; Table 4 |
| Smaller-model unseen performance is best read as parity | On **145 unseen tasks**, 68M/150M margins are **+0.027/+0.028**, but neither survives task-level Holm correction; results also depend on task versus question aggregation. | §6.1 |
| Larger models perform strongly on unseen BTZSC datasets | On **four unseen datasets**, dataset-mean macro-F1 is **0.636/0.686** for Crivo-400M/1B versus **0.618** for the best cited published zero-shot row; comparisons are unpaired. | §6.2 |
| Joint scoring remains order-sensitive | Across **3,841 questions**, random option permutations change **12–23%** of Crivo answers; the studied per-option reader has no changes by construction. | §6.3; Fig. 6 |
| Median latency conceals per-option cost | Paired A100 measurements over **1,000 requests**: per-option/joint 32M medians **10.7/10.4 ms**, means **59.0/13.7 ms**. On the shared **80-request**, 16-thread CPU sample, Crivo-17M/DeBERTa medians are **13.5/728.3 ms**. | §5.3; Table 7 |
| Unseen-task probabilities are unreliable | Every Crivo has an unseen S1-Arena Brier score no better than uniform prediction, whose score is **0.619**. | §6.4 |
| Private leaderboard strength has limited scope | Prumo and Crivo-17M obtain same-skill private scores of **15.78/15.88**, while Prumo is near chance externally; the authors interpret this as evidence of format-specific solving. | §§7.1, 7.4 |

These results support a conditional accuracy–cost trade-off, rather than universal superiority of either reader. The authors’ explanation that reranker input-format compatibility drives the advantage is plausible but explicitly untested. Seen-source performance and private same-skill scores do not establish generalisation to new tasks.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated controls gap:** The reader study uses one size and training subset. Compute matching repeats joint-reader examples rather than supplying equally many distinct examples; compute is approximate, and three-seed intervals have limited precision (§§3.6, 5.1, 8).
- **Authors’ stated comparison gap:** Closest same-interface encoders and GLiClass were not evaluated externally. Family comparisons mix backbone, training data and readout; the small LLM baselines use a plain letter readout without chat templates or debiasing (§§3.4, 8).
- **Exposure and selection risks:** Audits use source names and string matching. Most BTZSC datasets are seen; DeBERTa’s S1-Arena exposure was not audited. Internal development panels failed, and private scores influenced the revised recipe (§§3.3, 4.4, 7.3).
- **Measurement and scope limits:** Private tests are inaccessible and single-run; CPU latency uses a small sample; throughput is unmeasured. Evaluation is English-only, and models are over-confident on unfamiliar tasks (§8).
- **Analyst inference:** The reported audit does not establish absence of exposure inherited through backbone pretraining or teachers; the text specifically notes API-Bank exposure in Crivo-17M’s earlier teacher (§4.3).

## Open research questions
1. **Does the reader advantage persist with full training and larger models?** Neither was controlled here. Train both readers with the full recipe at multiple sizes and matched budgets (§8).
2. **How much does repeated data disadvantage the joint control?** Replace repetition with additional distinct training examples while matching encoded tokens and updates (§8).
3. **Can joint scoring retain efficiency while becoming order-invariant?** Compare the current reader with masked-attention and permutation-invariant variants under the same robustness protocol (§§6.3, 8).
4. **Can unseen-task confidence support reliable routing?** Evaluate target-task calibration and selective prediction on newly held-out domains, since unseen-task Brier scores are poor (§§6.4, 8).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the summary of reported findings; broader generalisation remains uncertain.
