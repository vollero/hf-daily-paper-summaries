# Rethinking Verification in Agent Harnesses

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.34577) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34577)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across the evaluated coding and visual tasks, a separate critic generally improves agent outputs more cost-effectively than completion-time self-checking, although benefits vary by model and evaluation setting (§5).

## Problem and motivation
Agent harnesses decide when agents check their work, what evidence they inspect, and whether they revise before completion. The authors identify limited comparative evidence for choosing these mechanisms or determining whether their gains justify additional computation (§§1–2).

The practical problem is that more execution does not necessarily produce better verification: agents can overlook requirements, repeat inadequate checks, or damage a previously valid deliverable (§§5.2–5.3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Verification framework:** Four lifecycle layers—initial instructions (L0), execution-time reminders (L1), completion gates (L2), and independent review (L3)—organize intervention timing, checking responsibility, and revision (§3).
- **Comparative study:** The authors compare 17 designs across seven coding models on selected Terminal-Bench 2.1 and SWE-bench Verified tasks, measuring solved-task accuracy and API cost (§4).
- **Independent-review designs and scaling:** A critic starts with fresh context, inspects the live sandbox without modifying the deliverable, and returns feedback for agent repair. Experiments vary context, review strategy, successive rounds, and independent critics (§3; Appendix E.4–E.5).
- **VisualBench:** An internal collection of 18 visual and interactive tasks adds blinded human comparisons of functionality and visual quality (§4; Appendix C). Public release is not established.

## Evidence and main results
Here, **pp** means percentage points; coding gains are reported averages across the evaluated models, relative to the baseline harness.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial system instructions provide modest coding gains | TB accuracy **+3.3 pp** at **1.08×** baseline cost; SWE **+3.9 pp** at **0.94×** cost. | §5.1; Tables 2–3 and Figure 2, as described in prose |
| After-edit system reminders improve coding accuracy | TB **+3.5 pp**; SWE **+3.6 pp**. Across all three L1 designs, output tokens fall **14–25%** on TB and **7–10%** on SWE. | §5.2 |
| Strong completion gates can be expensive | Early user rejection yields **+6.0/+6.2 pp** on TB/SWE. Across rejection designs, cost is **1.60–1.76×/1.48–1.56×** baseline, respectively. | §5.3; Figure 2 |
| Independent review offers favorable measured accuracy–cost tradeoffs | Across four L3 designs, TB gains **+5.9–9.5 pp** at **1.13–1.25×** cost; SWE gains **+3.9–6.6 pp** at **1.00–1.04×** cost, including review and revision. | §5.4 |
| Independent review also improves visual artifact preference | On VisualBench’s 18 tasks, L3 net preference over baseline is **+13.2–23.8 pp**, versus **−3.2 pp** for evaluated L0 and L1 designs and **+2.7 pp** for evaluated L2. | §5.4; Appendix C.4 |
| Changes-based visual review outperforms trajectory-based review | Without required visual inspection: **+21.2 versus +13.2 pp** net preference; with required inspection: **+23.8 versus +21.6 pp**. | §5.4; Appendix C.4 |
| More review improves average TB accuracy, with different costs | One critic/round: **62.7%**, **$0.47/trial**; four critics: **65.8%**, **$0.62**; four rounds: **66.1%**, **$0.77**. | Appendix B.4; Figure 23 |

These results support the authors’ recommendation to use independent review in the tested harness. They do not establish universal superiority across models, full benchmark populations, critic choices, or equal-compute alternatives. VisualBench preference measures comparative artifact quality rather than absolute task success. Several cited tables and figures appear only as captions or links in the supplied text; the results above are supported by its prose.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Evaluation scope:** TB uses a difficulty-balanced subset of 31 tasks. SWE initially uses 30 tasks, then adjusts the set using baseline performance; the final count is unclear. **Analyst inference:** these selected samples limit population-level generalization (Appendix A.1).
- **Critic dependence:** Qwen3.7-Plus serves as the critic for every coding model. **Analyst inference:** the study does not isolate whether benefits generalize across critic models or arise specifically from this critic–agent pairing (§4).
- **Model-dependent outcomes:** Additional review can regress individual models despite improving averages; Appendix B.4 reports such a regression for Kimi-K2.7-Code.
- **Human evaluation:** VisualBench uses internal company raters and a small task collection. Blinding and randomized sides help, but rater counts and agreement statistics are unavailable in the supplied text (§4; Appendix C).
- **Acceptance boundaries:** Standard single-pass review accepts the agent’s revision without another review; the width sweep likewise leaves subsequent repairs unrechecked. Finding a defect therefore does not guarantee a correct final repair (Appendix E.4–E.5).
- **Incomplete causal separation:** After-edit system/user comparisons change both message role and an automated marker. **Analyst inference:** their individual effects cannot be separated by that comparison (§3).
- **Budget and cost limits:** A documented review case finds a defect but times out before repair. Reported monetary tradeoffs use specified API pricing and do not establish latency tradeoffs (§5.4; Appendix A.2).

## Open research questions
1. **How much does critic identity matter?** Only one coding critic is evaluated. A next experiment could cross several critic models with the same agents under matched cost budgets.
2. **Do the gains persist on broader task populations?** Coding subsets are selected partly through baseline performance. Evaluate the designs on full benchmarks or a separately sampled held-out set.
3. **When should verification stop?** More review improves averages but can hurt individual models, and single-pass repairs are unrechecked. Compare fixed review budgets with adaptive stopping and post-repair regression checks.
4. **What causes the advantage of fresh-context review?** The comparisons do not isolate fresh context from extra computation and critic identity. A matched-budget experiment could compare fresh-context self-review, separate-model review, and continued self-checking.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied prose clearly supports the principal reported trends, but independent assessment is constrained by missing table bodies and figure data.

Material unavailable or unclear information includes final SWE task count and task IDs, full baseline and per-design accuracies, uncertainty estimates or significance tests, human-rater counts and agreement, exact pricing values, complete budget settings, latency measurements, and public availability of code and VisualBench assets.
