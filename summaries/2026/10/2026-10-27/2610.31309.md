# Extreme Compression of Per-Layer Embeddings via Cross-Layer Latent Vector Quantization

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.31309) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31309)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors report that OPTIC’s shared token codes and layer-specific decoders compress Gemma 4 E4B’s per-layer embeddings by about 100× while retaining 97.8% of its capped seven-task aggregate performance, versus 66.3% for product quantization (§4.1).

## Problem and motivation

Per-layer embeddings (PLE) store a separate vector for each token at each decoder layer, accounting for 35.2% of Gemma 4 E4B’s parameters and 45.8% of E2B’s (§1). At extreme compression, conventional row-wise quantizers allocate only a few bytes to each token–layer vector.

The authors identify two opportunities: share a token’s representation across layers, and optimize reconstruction where PLE affects model computation—after its gate and output projection—rather than solely in stored embedding coordinates (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Cross-layer latent coding:** OPTIC stores one product-quantized latent code tuple per token. Separate affine decoders reconstruct its embeddings at each layer, removing the layer multiplier from assignment storage while preserving layer-specific reconstruction (§2.2).
- **Output-path fitting:** Codebooks and decoders minimize gated projection-output error with the backbone frozen. Optimization alternates continuous updates with discrete code reassignment, initialized using output-weighted PCA and grouped K-means (§§2.3–2.4).
- **Additional representations and deployment:** The paper introduces output-path-weighted row-wise PCA-PQ and frequency-tiered OPTIC, and demonstrates serving directly from compact codes without materializing the full PLE tensor (§2.5; §6).

## Evidence and main results

“Retention” below means the equal-weight geometric mean of seven task-score ratios against the matched uncompressed checkpoint, with each ratio capped at one (§3.3).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong quality at approximately 100× | BF16 E4B: OPTIC 97.8% retention versus PQ 66.3%; OPTIC’s resident PLE compression is 99.94×. | §2.2; §4.1; Table 1 discussion |
| Compatible with backbone quantization | Official W4A16 E4B, approximately 100× PLE compression: OPTIC 96.9% versus strongest conventional baseline 82.8%. | §1; §4.1 |
| Sampling intervals remain separated | BF16 E4B: OPTIC 95% paired-bootstrap interval 95.5–99.0%, versus PQ 60.6–70.5%, near 100×. | §4.1; Table 2 discussion |
| Quality persists at higher compression | E4B at 594× and 1,002×: BF16 retention 90.5% and 84.1%; W4A16 retention 87.7% and 81.8%. | §4.2 |
| Gains include generation and vision tasks | BF16 E4B near 100×: GSM8K accuracy 92.8% versus dense 92.9% and PQ 73.5%; InfographicVQA ANLS 67.5% versus 69.7% and 38.4%. | §4.1; Appendix E.2 |
| Shared representation beats independent codes | E4B near equal storage and matched fitting recipe: BF16 retention 97.8% versus 79.3%; W4A16 96.9% versus 86.8%. | §5.2; Table 3 discussion |
| Output-aware fitting adds a smaller gain | BF16 E4B near 100×, fixed codes and common initialization: full output-path fitting gains a paired mean 1.98 percentage points over Euclidean fitting and 0.31 over diagonal weighting across three fits. | §5.3; Appendix E.1 |
| Compact serving reduces total GPU memory | BF16 E4B near 100×, RTX 4090, fixed 1 GiB KV cache: 29.7% less process GPU memory and at most 0.40% added wall time across eight workloads. | §6; §8; Appendix B.3 |

These results support substantial PLE compression within the tested Gemma checkpoints. Compression ratios concern **PLE storage**, while the smaller memory reduction concerns the full serving process. The aggregate retention metric does not imply uniform preservation across tasks, and bootstrap intervals quantify benchmark sampling uncertainty rather than fitting variability.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Evaluation covers Gemma 4 E2B and E4B; generalization to other embedding structures remains untested (Limitations; §8).
- **Author-stated uncertainty:** Main comparisons use one fitting seed. Three-fit controls use a different, common Euclidean initialization, so they do not directly quantify variation of the main recipe (Limitations).
- **Author-stated ablation caveat:** Removing sharing also changes allocation of bytes between codes and decoders. Its advantage cannot be attributed to sharing alone (Limitations).
- **Author-stated objective boundary:** Gates remain fixed during fitting, and reconstruction error is measured before normalization and residual addition. The fixed-code objective ablation covers continuous fitting only (Limitations).
- **Author-stated comparison caveat:** Baselines are PLE adaptations; the AQLM comparison uses its Euclidean beam encoder rather than establishing superiority over its complete original compression pipeline (§7; Limitations).
- **Analyst inference:** Runtime portability is uncertain because serving measurements use one GPU type and one serving stack with controlled workloads (Appendix B.3).

## Open research questions

1. **Does cross-layer coding transfer beyond Gemma PLE?** Only two Gemma scales are evaluated. Apply matched-byte OPTIC and row-wise controls to another architecture with aligned layer embeddings (§8; Limitations).
2. **How sensitive is the main recipe to fitting and calibration choices?** Main results use one fit seed and a fixed mixed calibration policy. Repeat the complete recipe across seeds and vary natural-text sources and synthetic-record proportions (§3.2; Limitations).
3. **Would updating gates or optimizing downstream outputs improve extreme-compression quality?** The current loss freezes gates and stops at projection outputs. Compare cached-gate fitting with periodically refreshed gates or downstream distillation under matched storage and fitting budgets (§2.3; Limitations).
4. **How portable are serving savings?** Test compact decoding across GPU types, serving engines, and request distributions, since the reported measurements use an RTX 4090 with vLLM (Appendix B.3).

## Confidence and missing information
