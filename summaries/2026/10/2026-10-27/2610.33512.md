# Eat Your Precision: Precision Objective Mitigates Reward Hacking in Rubric-based RL

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.33512) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33512)


## One-sentence takeaway

Fork combines rubric coverage with the fraction of necessary, factually correct response units, improving LLM-judged preference and reducing verbosity in medical and science QA, with some loss of coverage relative to coverage-only training (Tables 1–2).

## Problem and motivation

Rubric-based RL rewards satisfying predefined requirements, but can leave unnecessary or incorrect additions unpenalized. The authors identify this as a recall-heavy objective that encourages verbose responses with high rubric coverage but poor overall quality. Their proposed gap is explicit assessment of whether **all generated content** is warranted and correct, beyond whether required content appears (§§1, 3.1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Precision-aware reward:** Fork combines weighted rubric recall and response precision through an Fβ harmonic mean, then uses that score for GRPO training (§§3.3, 4.1).
- **Rubric-grounded necessity estimation:** Responses are split into approximately sentence-sized units. Each rubric distributes one credit across matching units; an LLM-selected threshold determines necessity. Precision counts units judged both necessary and factually correct (§3.2). The appendix prompt additionally removes later duplicates (Figures 6–7).
- **Empirical assessment:** Medical and science experiments compare coverage rewards, preference rewards, precision variants, aggregation choices, and selected evaluator variations (§4). The abstract reports source-code availability; no new dataset is introduced.

The distinctive contribution is this rubric-grounded precision formulation and its integration into rubric-based RL; precision–recall rewards themselves have precedents acknowledged in §2.

## Evidence and main results

All win rates below compare trained responses against the base policy; main evaluations use GPT-5.4. Rubric metrics are reported as macro scores.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Medical precision and preference improve substantially | RubricHub-Medical, Fork β=1 versus Standard: precision **53.2% vs 25.5%**, recall **32.5% vs 35.7%**, F1 **37.5% vs 26.4%**, win rate **68.0% vs 3.1%**. | Table 1 |
| Medical responses become shorter | Same comparison: average length **3,533 vs 9,195**; the supplied text does not specify the length unit. | Table 1 |
| External medical preference improves with similar recall | HealthBench, Fork β=1 versus OnlineRubrics: win rate **74.1% vs 22.1%**, recall **25.2% vs 25.7%**. | Table 1 |
| Science gains involve a coverage tradeoff | RubricHub-Science, Fork β=1.25 versus Standard: precision **45.1% vs 33.5%**, recall **30.4% vs 31.4%**, win rate **61.5% vs 38.6%**. On ResearchQA: win rate **67.1% vs 16.1%**, recall **58.9% vs 64.4%**. | Table 2 |
| Recall weighting changes the balance | RubricHub-Medical: Fork β=1.25 achieves recall/precision/F1 of **35.8/49.0/38.6%**, versus **32.5/53.2/37.5%** for β=1. Simple averaging yields **26.2/55.3/32.8%**. | Table 6 |
| Credit-based necessity has supporting removal evidence | For **723 units from 40** base-policy medical responses, authors report nearly unchanged recall until **40%** of units are removed in predicted-unnecessary-first order. | §4.3, Figure 4 |

These results support improved judged quality and concision in the tested settings, rather than elimination of reward hacking. Coverage preservation depends on the dataset and β. The authors’ Pareto-optimality claim concerns the evaluated comparisons, including a training-grader analysis in Figure 3.

There are reporting inconsistencies: §4.2 attributes **38.6% F1** to the main medical comparison, while Table 1 reports **37.5%** for β=1; **38.6%** belongs to β=1.25 in Table 6. The abstract’s medical win-rate comparator is OnlineRubrics, although its wording invokes the original recall reward.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated uncertainty:** Selecting the optimal β remains future work (§4.3).
- **Analyst inference—judge validity:** Training and evaluation rely on LLM judgments without reported human validation. Grader selection uses a small medical sample; necessity agreement is weaker than several other judgment types (Appendix B, Table 7).
- **Analyst inference—scope:** Reported policy experiments use one base model and two domains. Broader model, domain, and task generalization is unestablished (§4.1).
- **Analyst inference—rubric dependence:** Useful content omitted from the predefined rubrics can receive no necessity credit under the formal method; its treatment is not directly tested (§3.2).
- **Analyst inference—limited robustness evidence:** Alternative judge prompts preserve preference over the base policy, but do not establish uniform superiority over every baseline; coverage baselines are omitted from that comparison (§4.3, Table 4).
- **Specification ambiguity:** Main-text binary correctness and threshold-only necessity differ from appendix prompts containing additional correctness categories and duplicate removal; their exact integration is unclear (§3.2, Figures 6–9).

## Open research questions

- **How should β be selected?** The paper leaves this unresolved and shows differing tradeoffs. Next experiment: select β on held-out data and test transfer across domains and graders.
- **Does rubric-grounded precision suppress useful unlisted content?** Zero-credit units fail the formal necessity threshold. Next experiment: deliberately omit useful rubric items and obtain expert judgments of retained versus removed content.
- **Do gains survive human evaluation and model changes?** Evidence is concentrated in one policy model with automated judges. Next experiment: repeat training across model families and conduct blinded expert preference and factuality assessments.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The method and main tabulated results are clear, but inconsistencies and evaluator dependence limit confidence in broader conclusions.

Material information unavailable or unclear in the supplied text includes uncertainty intervals, repeated-run variability, human validation, grading cost and wall-clock overhead, response-length units, exact appendix-to-formula implementation mappings, and confirmation of released checkpoint availability.
