# DynaFlow: Learning Free-Form Multi-Agent Orchestration

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.34467) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34467)


## One-sentence takeaway
DynaFlow trains a main agent to generate executable collaboration workflows with frozen sub-agents, achieving 88.08% accuracy across 688 questions, although the specific benefit of demonstration curation remains unisolated (Table 1; §3.2).

## Problem and motivation
Fixed agent roles and communication structures can poorly match tasks requiring different amounts of parallel work, sequential reasoning, or verification. The authors identify a gap between generating task-specific workflows and repeatedly adapting unrestricted workflow programs using execution feedback (§1).

They also argue that correct final answers alone do not establish good orchestration: demonstrations can contain unsupported premises, biased verification, or broken information transfer (§3.2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Executable orchestration as an action:** The main agent can answer, call ordinary tools, or synthesize JavaScript workflows specifying sub-agent instructions, parallelism, dependencies, and verification. Returned outputs and execution traces inform subsequent decisions (§3.1; Appendix B).
- **Orchestration training with frozen executors:** Two-stage supervised fine-tuning distills teacher-generated first-turn reasoning and workflow actions, then continues training on demonstrations screened for orchestration defects. Only the main agent changes (§3.2).
- **Selective collaboration and workflow analysis:** Dynamic mode chooses direct answering or collaboration; Forced mode requires collaboration. Structural and judge-based analyses characterize changes in information reuse and verification (§§5.3–5.4).

The contribution is this combination; the paper does not establish that executable actions or adaptive workflows individually originate here (§2).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

| Mathematics gains are strong within the evaluated sets | Forced solves **90/90 AIME questions**; Dynamic PolyMATH accuracy is **91.50%**, versus the strongest listed baseline, Self-Refine, at **87.50%** on **200 questions**. | Table 1; §5.1; Appendix A |

These results support improved coordination under the tested backbone and runtime while executor weights remain fixed. Structural findings describe generated instructions, rather than proving faithful downstream information use. The near-equal Dynamic and Forced scores do not establish statistical equivalence, and fewer agents do not establish proportional token, latency, or monetary savings.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-identified failures:** Incorrect intermediate conclusions can misdirect verification, and final synthesis can override correct evidence. Training supervises first turns rather than multi-turn recovery from disagreements or errors (§§5.1, 6).
- **Curation attribution remains unresolved:** §3.2 explicitly calls for an equal-size random-subset continuation control, but no result for that control appears. **Analyst inference:** Stage 2 gains cannot be assigned specifically to quality filtering rather than additional training.
- **Comparison scope:** Tool-free baselines lack equivalent retrieval access. AFlow uses transferred released workflows without task-specific workflow search (§4). These choices limit broader superiority claims.
- **Training-data inconsistency:** §4 reports 2,031 Stage 1 examples; Appendix C and Table 3 report 2,257; Appendix E identifies 2,254 workflow-containing demonstrations. Their relationship is unexplained. Training includes HotpotQA, also an evaluation benchmark, but train–evaluation overlap checks are not supplied.
- **Measurement and reproducibility gaps:** Workflow ratings use an LLM judge without execution results, and role labels use keywords. Manual agreement is promised in §3.2 but not reported in Appendix E. Repeated-run uncertainty, concrete runtime resource caps, and end-to-end inference costs are unavailable.

## Open research questions
- **Does curation outperform extra training?** The required continuation control is unreported. Compare curated and random subsets with matched example counts, training steps, and multiple seeds.
- **Can multi-turn supervision improve recovery?** Current supervision targets first actions despite documented verification and synthesis failures. Train on trajectories containing conflicting or erroneous outputs, then measure correction and final-answer selection.
- **Does selective routing save actual computation?** The paper reports agent counts rather than complete inference costs. Measure tokens, latency, tool calls, and monetary cost alongside accuracy under matched budgets.
- **Does orchestration transfer across executors?** Evaluation fixes one executor backbone. Swap executor models while holding the trained main agent constant and measure accuracy and information-transfer failures.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the main accuracy comparisons and method description, but causal interpretation and reproducibility are weaker.

Material missing information includes reconciled training counts, train–evaluation overlap checks, the random-subset control, judge–human agreement, repeated-run uncertainty, runtime limits, end-to-end costs, and confirmed artifact availability.
