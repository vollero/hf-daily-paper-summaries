# DialFork: Going Beyond Factuality to Evaluate Procedural Hallucination in Conversational Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.21561) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21561)


## One-sentence takeaway
DIALFORK benchmarks conversational workflow deviations using controlled synthetic dialogues, showing strong fork-severity ranking with turn-level matching while exposing persistent localization errors and false alarms.

## Problem and motivation
An assistant can produce plausible, truthful responses while skipping required steps, following the wrong workflow, or pursuing a conflicting goal. The authors call this **procedural hallucination** and argue that factuality benchmarks and content-based dialogue similarity fail to measure it adequately (§§1–2).

The intended applications are monitoring conversational agents, retrieving procedurally similar conversations, and detecting deviations during an interaction. These are evaluation settings demonstrated on synthetic data, rather than validated deployment outcomes.

## Approach and contributions
- **Formalization and taxonomy:** Model a workflow through states, actions, transitions, and an objective; define workflow identity corruption (WIC), step selection policy corruption (SSPC), goal replacement (GR), and conflicting system constraints (CSC) (§3).
- **Benchmark and construction pipeline:** Generate conformant conversations from STAR workflows and scenario instructions using Gemma4-31B for both participants. Replay each scenario with seeded generation and inject a corruption at four positions, producing 36,278 dialogues across 24 tasks and 12 domains (§4).
- **Three evaluation tasks:** Measure set-to-set deviation, dialogue-to-dialogue deviation, and the first off-track turn using raw transcripts. Task 3 separates conformant training dialogues from test dialogues and their associated forks (§5; Appendix A.1).
- **Metrics and baselines:** Introduce FLOWKL, which compares distributions of likelihood under an induced transition graph, and FLOWSCORE, which matches turn embeddings using a BERTScore-like calculation. Also evaluate DTW alignment, streaming transition detectors, and zero-shot LLM detectors (§5). Crucially, the implemented FLOWSCORE embeds turns independently and **does not capture turn order** (§5.2).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Human ratings support fork ordering, particularly outside CSC | Thirty annotators judged 428 conversations from five tasks. For WIC/SSPC/GR, mean conformance rose from 14/100 at the earliest fork to 58 at the latest, versus 94 for conformant dialogues; quartet-level τb = 0.64 over 23 quartets. | §4; Appendix N.7 |
| Injection timing is an imperfect proxy for visible onset | Among confirmed true-instruction forks, humans identified onset exactly at injection in 67% and within one assistant turn in 77%; 96% of annotated onsets were at or after injection. | Appendix N.7; Figure 8 |
| FLOWKL is competitive, without established superiority | Mean set-ranking τ across tasks/types: FLOWKL 0.882, FBD 0.913, PRD 0.878. FLOWKL differences versus FBD and PRD were nonsignificant: corrected p = 0.43 and 0.79. | Table 2; Appendix J |
| FLOWSCORE best recovers constructed dialogue-level rankings | Across seven encoders, mean τ = 0.812–0.845, versus 0.519–0.747 for full-dialogue embeddings; improvements over every evaluated alternative were significant per encoder, corrected p < 0.001. | Table 3; §6.2; Table 7 |
| Streaming detectors offer useful but imperfect localization | On the Task 3 test set, LaBSE 3-gram IT with node confidence achieved 1.94-turn hallucination MAE, 94.80% fork coverage, and 16.31% conformant false alarms. Plain SS had the best streaming composite Score: 73.69/100. | Table 4; §6.3 |
| Workflow access helps offline LLM detection | Without workflows, five zero-shot LLMs had hallucination MAE of 4.50–6.48 turns. With workflows, Llama-3.3-70B achieved 1.83 turns, 93.89% coverage, 21.06% false alarms, and Score 77.09/100. | Table 4 |
| Ranking success does not establish the strongest human alignment | Pooling annotated forks across sources, tasks, and types, embedding methods correlated with human-perceived deviation at Spearman ρ = 0.58–0.67; FLOWSCORE was at 0.58 and mean-turn matching at 0.67. This differs from within-source fork ranking. | Appendix N.7 |

The evidence supports controlled deviation measurement and useful initial detection baselines. It does not establish performance on naturally occurring failures or a direct measure of real deployment risk. FLOWSCORE’s ranking advantage also cannot establish sensitivity to ordering, because its evaluated implementation lacks that capability (§5.2).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope:** One source corpus, one generator, synthetic interactions, and one injected corruption per dialogue restrict generalization. Compound failures, other languages, and naturally occurring agent mistakes remain unevaluated (Limitations, p. 10).
- **Ground-truth uncertainty:** Injection can precede observable deviation. Human confirmation was not universal, and onset labels matched injection only imperfectly (Appendix N.7).
- **CSC ordering is less clearly validated:** Human conformance ratings changed little with CSC fork position, weakening the interpretation of the same severity ordering across all four types (Appendix N.7).
- **Analyst inference—construction dependence:** Matched synthetic forks provide a narrower test than retrieving procedurally similar, independently produced conversations. Shared-prefix and length controls address particular shortcuts, but do not establish transfer to that retrieval setting (§§5.2, 6.2).
- **Analyst inference—comparison scope:** Streaming detectors learn from conformant training examples; offline LLMs are zero-shot. Their comparison therefore combines differences in method, learning access, and future-context access (§5.3; Appendix A.1).
- **Baseline and reporting limits:** The authors evaluate simple detectors rather than optimized alternatives. The statistical-testing description and Table 7 caption describe different Holm-correction groupings, leaving the exact correction family ambiguous (Appendices J, L; Table 7).

## Open research questions
1. **Do detectors transfer to real conversations?** All benchmark conversations use one synthetic generator. Next experiment: evaluate frozen detectors on independently annotated deployed-agent logs and dialogues regenerated with another model.
2. **Does explicit order modeling improve procedural similarity?** FLOWSCORE’s current implementation ignores order. Next experiment: compare contextualized turn encoders against independent embeddings on controlled reorderings that preserve turn content.
3. **Can false alarms fall without sacrificing detection?** Streaming methods retain substantial false alarms on held-out conformant dialogues. Next experiment: compare threshold calibration methods at matched fork coverage, reporting results by task.
4. **How should delayed and compound deviations be labeled?** Injection timing can precede visible onset, and each dialogue contains only one corruption. Next experiment: annotate first observable deviation separately from injection time in dialogues containing sequential or overlapping corruptions.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported construction and benchmark results; **limited external-validity confidence** for deployment claims.

The supplied text does not establish performance on real-user interactions, unseen task families, multiple generators, non-English dialogues, or compound failures. It also leaves some implementation details unspecified, including clustering and density-estimation settings and the exact treatment of missing predictions in MAE aggregation. Artifact availability is reported by the authors and was not independently verified.
