# InteractiveWorld: A Verifiable Human-Centric Environment for Building Better Interactive MLLM Assistants

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.33966) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33966)


## One-sentence takeaway
InteractiveWorld evaluates assistants through language-guided simulated human actions and verifiable outcomes, revealing low task success among tested MLLMs while offering preliminary evidence that its human executor approximates real participants in simulation.

## Problem and motivation
Static egocentric benchmarks cannot test whether an assistant adapts successfully when its guidance changes subsequent actions and observations. Real participant studies close this loop but are costly to scale. The authors target a reproducible evaluation of perception, memory, planning, and communication working together during physical tasks (§1; Appendix A).

## Approach and contributions
- **Human-centric simulator:** InteractiveWorld combines Isaac Sim, a kinematic SMPL+H human, autonomous navigation and manipulation, and 36 action primitives. An MLLM Human interprets the evaluated Assistant’s natural-language guidance; the Assistant cannot directly control the simulator (§2; Appendix B).
- **Interactive benchmark:** InteractiveBench contains 120 frozen, reviewed episodes, with 20 each in Guidance, Planning, Accessibility, Decision, Prevention, and Finding. Scoring checks physical states and, where required, action order or intervention timing (§3; Appendices C.1–C.4).
- **Evaluation and diagnosis:** The authors compare 12 assistants, analyze initial failures and action efficiency, and test memory augmentation, executor variation, static adaptations, and real participant executors (§4; Appendix D).

The claimed “first” status is the authors’ novelty claim; the supplied text does not independently establish priority.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low overall completion | Across 120 tasks with Gemini 3.1 Pro as Human, Claude Fable 5.1 leads 12 assistants at 41.7% strict success; all others score ≤33.3%. | §4.1; Table 2 |
| Assistance controls separate success from no-help behavior | The same Human achieves 100% pass with an Oracle Assistant and 0% without assistance under the evaluation protocol. | Table 2 caption |
| Long-horizon categories are difficult | Best reported category success is 20.0% for Guidance and 25.0% for Planning, versus 85.0% for Decision. | §4.1; Table 2 |
| Perception and memory dominate diagnosed failures | Together they account for 70.4%–85.7% of initial errors across models, among valid model-attributable failed episodes. | §4.1; Figure 4; §D.1 |
| Successful interaction can require substantial extra actions | On individually passed tasks, Fable 5.1 uses 6.75× oracle actions in Accessibility and 1.20× in Prevention. Success sets differ across models. | §4.1; Figure 3; §C.3.3 |
| Simple memory has mixed effects | Across three models, authors report 5–15% success improvements on Prevention and Finding, but 5% Planning declines for two models; relative versus percentage-point changes are not explicit. | §4.2; Figure 5; §D.2 |
| Longer reference horizons correlate with lower success | Across eight models and 120 tasks, family-adjusted success decreases 43.1 percentage points over the normalized horizon range; 95% bootstrap CI: −58.1 to −28.9; one-sided permutation p<0.0001. | §D.4; Figure 9 |
| Simulated and real executors show preliminary agreement | On the same 36-task subset, Fable 5.1’s outcomes with Gemini versus three humans average 84.3% agreement; human–human pairs average 85.2%. | §D.5; Figure 10 |

These findings establish difficulty under the specified simulator, information boundaries, and Human restrictions. They do not establish physical-world assistant reliability. Failure attribution and capability comparisons are diagnostic rather than causal; the horizon analysis is explicitly associative and provisional. Static adaptations are described, but their numerical results are absent from the supplied table content (§D.3).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope:** Participant validation uses three people executing tasks inside the simulator, not physical-world deployment; descriptive agreement is not a statistical equivalence test (§D.5).
- **Authors’ stated uncertainty:** Longer tasks also differ in search, state changes, interactions, and constraints; some horizon-analysis labels were reconstructed from traces (§D.4).
- **Analyst inference:** Human restrictions deliberately prevent independent reasoning or plan repair. This improves attribution to the Assistant but narrows correspondence to autonomous real users (§C.2.2). User behavioral calibration remains future work (Appendix E).
- **Analyst inference:** A fixed-shape male body, kinematic movement, and engineered action execution limit tested variation in bodies and motor behavior. “Collision-free” is an operational tolerance guarantee (§§B.1.1–B.1.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Efficiency measured only on successful episodes cannot characterize effort across failures or support clean cross-model comparisons when success sets differ (§C.3.3).
- Important result tables and prompt boxes are missing their contents in the retrieval, limiting independent scrutiny despite detailed protocol descriptions.

## Open research questions
- **Do rankings transfer to physical-world users?** Existing participant validation remains in simulation (§D.5). Next experiment: repeat matched tasks with real wearable observations and physical actions.
- **Which memory mechanism improves complete-task success consistently?** Appended textual memory produces mixed category effects (§4.2). Next experiment: compare structured state memory and retrieval against appended memory with matched visual and inference budgets.
- **Does horizon itself cause failures?** The reported association is confounded by task demands (§D.4). Next experiment: vary distraction length within otherwise matched task templates while holding objectives and constraints fixed.
- **How robust are assistants to user autonomy and diversity?** Current executors follow restrictive policies, and varied personalities and skills are future work (§C.2.2; Appendix E). Next experiment: systematically vary initiative, clarification behavior, and execution errors, then compare with participant behavior.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the architecture, protocol, and quoted findings, but omits most numerical table bodies, several figure values, and reproduced prompt boxes. Also unavailable are uncertainty estimates for main model comparisons, repeat-run variability, detailed participant characteristics, and independent verification of the linked release artifacts.
