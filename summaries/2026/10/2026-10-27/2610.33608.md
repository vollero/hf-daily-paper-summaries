# Mycelium: A Meta-Study of Hallucination Detection Across Provenance, Typology, and Granularities

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.33608) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33608)


## One-sentence takeaway
Hallucination detector scores depend on benchmark construction and evaluation semantics, and the proposed Span Coverage F1 rewards contained span refinements while requiring overlap metrics to expose incomplete localization.

## Problem and motivation
Benchmarks called “hallucination detection” differ in their evidence, error distributions, labels, annotation units, and scoring rules. Consequently, similar headline scores can measure different capabilities. The authors identify a gap in benchmark-centered analysis: explaining how these choices define the prediction task and constrain cross-benchmark comparisons (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Measurement framework:** Organizes benchmarks by provenance, typology, and granularity, and formalizes scores as properties of detector–benchmark pairs, including output alignment and aggregation (§2, Eq. 1).
- **Benchmark and detector synthesis:** Maps benchmark construction, annotation, and metrics to detector mechanisms, evidence requirements, model access, supervision, and transfer assumptions (§§3–4; Figs. 2, 4). This is an analytical inventory rather than a new detector.
- **Span Coverage F1:** Introduces a directional, instance-level containment metric. Prediction coverage counts predicted spans contained within references; reference coverage counts references containing predictions; their harmonic mean forms the score (§3.2, Eqs. 3–4).
- **Metric diagnostics:** Uses multi-annotator English Mu-SHROOM annotations to compare containment, exact matching, character overlap, and split–merge-aware metrics (§3.2).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Boundary nesting occurs in the analyzed annotations | English Mu-SHROOM: 154 examples, 1,665 annotator-level span sets; 1,403 of 6,280 non-empty, non-identical pairs (22.3%) are boundary-nested with equal connected-component counts. | §3.2, p. 6 |
| Reference construction changes metric behavior | Macro LOO majority → union: Span Coverage F1 **0.410 → 0.750**; exact span F1 **0.288 → 0.071**; DetEval-1D **0.574 → 0.239**. Held-out annotator predictions remain fixed. | Table 1, p. 6 |
| Containment receives full credit on the designated nested subset | For 376 LOO cases with strictly contained predictions and equal connected-span counts: Span Coverage F1 **1.000**, character IoU **0.503**, exact span F1 **0.032**. | Table 1; §3.2, pp. 6–7 |
| Perfect containment can conceal severe undercoverage | One-character-per-reference diagnostic retains **3.1%** of reference characters on average, yet scores **1.000** Span Coverage F1 versus **0.031** character IoU. | Table 1; §3.2, p. 7 |

These results establish that scoring semantics and reference boundaries materially affect evaluation of the same localization. They do **not** establish better detector accuracy or superior agreement with human judgments of useful localization: annotators serve as predictions, and majority/union constructions are explicitly diagnostic references rather than alternative gold standards.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated limitation:** Containment ignores recovered extent; even a single character inside each reference can earn full credit. The authors recommend reporting an overlap-sensitive metric alongside it (§3.2).
- **Scope assumption:** Narrower contained predictions are accepted when annotations describe admissible regions rather than uniquely minimal errors. That assumption need not suit every annotation policy (§3.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Empirical evidence from English Mu-SHROOM alone does not establish robustness across languages, domains, annotation protocols, or actual detector outputs.
- **Reporting gaps:** The supplied text gives no confidence intervals or significance tests, human utility validation, or systematic detector transfer experiments. Its broad comparison framework is primarily analytical (§§3–5).

## Open research questions
- **Does containment track useful localization?** The one-character diagnostic exposes a mismatch between region contact and extent. Next experiment: obtain human usefulness judgments for contained predictions of varying lengths and compare metric agreement.
- **Do these effects generalize across languages and annotation policies?** Empirical analysis is English-only. Next experiment: repeat LOO diagnostics across languages using both minimal-span and broader-region annotation instructions.
- **How do metrics change detector rankings?** Current experiments score held-out annotations rather than competing detectors. Next experiment: evaluate fixed detector predictions under all compared metrics and reference constructions.
- **Which benchmark axes drive transfer failures?** The framework identifies provenance, typology, and granularity without controlled transfer measurements. Next experiment: vary one axis at a time while fixing evidence access, calibration, and output alignment.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported definitions and metric diagnostics; **medium** in broader generalization.

Material missing information includes uncertainty estimates, multilingual replication, detector-ranking experiments, human validation of containment semantics, and an explicit systematic literature-selection protocol. Public code and materials are claimed, but their contents were not supplied or verified.
