# Work Agents: Systems, Capabilities, and Optimization

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.35845) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35845)


## One-sentence takeaway
This survey proposes a framework for analyzing delegated digital work through model–harness systems, persistent responsibility, environment discovery, and verifiable outcomes, rather than introducing an experimentally validated agent.

## Problem and motivation
The authors argue that research organized around model outputs, tool calls, interfaces, or execution length lacks a unified account of how agents complete delegated work. Real assignments involve linked artifacts, changing workspace states, permissions, handoffs, and evidence that requirements were satisfied (§1, pp. 4–5).

Their central distinction is between delivering an output and fulfilling a responsibility: a correct artifact may still require approval, revision, or continued maintenance (§§2.1–2.2).

## Approach and contributions
The contributions are conceptual organization and literature synthesis; independent novelty relative to other surveys cannot be established from the supplied text.

- **System framework:** Six elements—task, model, harness, workspace, outcome, and verification—connect delegated requirements to observable evidence. The agent comprises the model and harness (§2.1; Fig. 1).
- **Working conditions and work forms:** Persistence describes when responsibility closes; openness describes what must be discovered during execution. Five work forms span conversation, atomic action, multi-step objectives, workflows, and workspaces (§§2.2, 3).
- **Capability and optimization taxonomy:** Five capability families form a feedback cycle, with improvements organized around model training, harness changes, and joint adaptation (§2.3; Figs. 3, 5).
- **Outcome-oriented evaluation agenda:** The authors advocate assessing artifacts, state changes, process completion, and constraints. The detailed evaluation and frontier sections are largely missing from the truncated source (§1, p. 5).

## Evidence and main results

Numerical findings below are **results attributed to cited studies**, not experiments established as conducted by this survey.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Persistence and openness describe different demands | Responsibility closure versus discovering relevant objects, interfaces, rules, and actors; illustrated through four combinations | §2.2; Fig. 2 |
| Capability demands change with working conditions | Persistence requires continuity and re-verification; openness requires discovering dependencies, authority, and evidence sources | Table 1, p. 12 |
| Composed training environments improve reported task success | CompoWorld: AutomationBench success rises from **12.83% to 33.33%**, comparing single-service with composed environments using the same backbone, training procedure, and **1K SFT trajectories** | §4.1.1, p. 26 |
| Combining verifier types improves reported data selection | WorkForge matched **1K-trajectory** ablation: ALE score **22.11** with programmatic and semantic checks, versus **15.62** semantic-only and **14.89** programmatic-only | §4.1.1, p. 26 |
| Rubric sensitivity varies by error type | GraphForge corruption audit: deleting a worksheet reduces the target rubric score by **0.377**; finer row, numerical, and citation errors change scores by **less than 0.03**, on a **0–1** scale | §4.1.1, p. 26 |
| Training-target choices affect downstream agent performance | Nemotron **100B-token** ablation: masking tool responses as prediction targets while retaining them as context raises SWE-bench Verified Pass@1 from **27.5% to 37.8%**, after identical SFT | §4.2.2, p. 30 |

These examples support the authors’ emphasis on environment structure, verification quality, and training design. They do not validate the proposed taxonomy causally or establish that any one optimization recipe generalizes across work domains. The excerpt does not provide full experimental protocols or uncertainty estimates for these cited comparisons.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Coverage limitation:** The source is truncated during §4.2.3; most later substantive discussion is unavailable. This analysis does not cover the complete paper.
- **Authors’ cautions:** Dataset counts represent different pipeline stages and are difficult to compare; some longer-trajectory comparisons also differ in task content and decision depth (§4.1.1).
- **Authors’ cautions:** Executable environments can contain inconsistent requirements or permissive verifiers. Construction and evaluation should be separated, including by shared workspace or source family (§§4.1.1–4.1.2).
- **Analyst inference:** The supplied text illustrates the framework but does not demonstrate that its categories reliably predict failures or improve system design.
- **Analyst inference:** Reported findings from heterogeneous cited studies cannot establish a common ranking of model, harness, and joint optimization.

## Open research questions
- **Do persistence and openness predict distinct failures?** Their separation is conceptual (§2.2). A next experiment could vary event-driven responsibility and discovery requirements independently while holding the underlying task fixed.
- **Can dependency-aware verification reduce checking cost without missing errors?** The authors propose selective rechecking but provide no corresponding result in the excerpt (§4.1.3). Compare full re-verification with dependency-based checks under controlled source changes.
- **Which failures require model updates versus harness changes?** The paper identifies both optimization targets without a general allocation rule in the supplied text (§4; Fig. 5). A matched experiment could compare model-only, harness-only, and joint changes under equal resource budgets.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The framework and cited numerical examples are explicit, but coverage is incomplete.
