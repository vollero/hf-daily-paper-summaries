# EmbodiedLab: Benchmarking Embodied Agents That Act to Know

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.35385) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35385)


## One-sentence takeaway
EmbodiedLab evaluates robots that physically experiment to obtain evidence, finding that even the strongest evaluated model–agent system succeeds on only 43.3% of tasks with native control, with apparatus interaction and bench restoration remaining major bottlenecks (§4, Table 2).

## Problem and motivation
The authors identify a gap between manipulation benchmarks, which specify a target physical state, and scientific-discovery benchmarks, which provide symbolic actions or numerical queries. Neither fully tests how physical action determines what an agent can learn and how those observations guide subsequent action (§§1–2).

EmbodiedLab targets this coupled process: agents receive an experimental objective without a prescribed procedure and must manipulate apparatus, obtain readings, analyze evidence, and leave the required bench state. Resampling hidden system parameters makes each answer instance-dependent (§3.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Task formulation:** Measure a hidden property, predict a response, or configure a system using experiments chosen by the agent. Task cards describe apparatus and rules without specifying a method (§3.1).
- **Benchmark and scoring:** Thirty experiments across five disciplines use 14 apparatus platforms in Isaac Sim. Success requires an accurate result, valid evidence from the same episode, and the required final state, including restoration. Public-interface reference solutions validate solvability (§§3.2–3.4).
- **Diagnostic evaluation:** T0 provides camera observations and joint control; T1 adds segmentation, privileged depth, and motion planning; T2 adds manipulation skills derived from successful stronger-agent runs. Six model–agent systems are evaluated at T0/T1, with three also evaluated at T2 (§§3.2, 4.1).

The contribution is principally a benchmark and diagnostic protocol, rather than a new general-purpose agent architecture.

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Native-control success remains limited | Across all experiments at T0: Claude Opus 5.5 43.3%, GPT-6 Astra 40.0%, GPT-6.1 Sol 28.3%, DeepSeek and Kimi 10.0% each, GLM 0.0%; success averages runs within experiments, then experiments equally. | Table 2; §4.1, Eq. 4 |
| Perception and planning provide modest aggregate gains | T0→T1 success increases by 1.7–10.0 percentage points across the six systems; Claude reaches 53.3% at T1. | Table 3 |
| Manipulation skills help the three weaker systems | T1→T2 success gains: DeepSeek +26.7 points to 40.0%; Kimi +13.3 to 33.3%; GLM +10.0 to 13.3%. T2 uses skills distilled from successful GPT/Claude runs on the same experiments. | Table 3; §3.2 |
| Restoration separates the stronger systems | Their rates of reaching an accurate result with all non-restoration gates satisfied are similar, 51.7–55.0%; GPT-6.1 Sol leaves the bench unrestored after an accurate result in 23% of all its T0 runs. | §4.3 |
| Physical-loop errors dominate attributed failures | Acting, observing, and restoring together account for 75–100% of each agent’s T0 failed runs **with an attributed cause**; knowing accounts for at most 25%. | §4.5; Appendix F, Fig. 10 |
| Tasks are demonstrably solvable within the simulator | Reference solutions solve all 30 experiments through the public interface without hidden-parameter access; reference error stays within half the tolerance except one experiment at 0.55 of tolerance. | §3.4; Appendix B, Fig. 8 |

These results support difficulty under the evaluated interfaces and the authors’ diagnosis that reliable physical interaction constrains performance. They do not establish real-world robot performance or isolate foundation-model ability from the coding harness. T2 provides diagnostic evidence about assistance, but its reuse of same-experiment solutions limits generalization claims.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Explicit scope restriction:** The authors identify T2 as diagnostic because its skills reuse successful runs on the same experiments; it is excluded from the released evaluation protocol (§3.2).
- **Analyst inference—simulation validity:** Experiments use standard physical models, including paraxial optics, lumped thermal models, and ideal-solution chemistry. Transfer to real apparatus and unmodeled effects is not tested in the supplied text (Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference—comparison confounding:** Models run in different developer-provided coding agents with their respective middle reasoning settings. Rankings therefore compare deployed systems rather than isolated models (§4.1; Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference—bundled interventions:** T1 combines perception and planning, so its gains cannot identify their separate contributions. T2 similarly bundles multiple manipulation skills (§3.2).
- **Reporting gaps:** The text does not explicitly specify run counts, numerical wall-clock limits, uncertainty intervals, or failure-attribution agreement. Consequently, ranking stability and attribution reliability cannot be assessed fully.

## Open research questions
- **Do manipulation skills generalize to unfamiliar apparatus?** T2 reuses same-experiment successes. A next experiment could build skills on separate apparatus and evaluate held-out platforms.
- **Which component of T1 helps most?** Perception and planning are added together. Separate perception-only and planning-only conditions could identify their effects.
- **Can stronger agents improve restoration without sacrificing measurement accuracy?** Restoration substantially reduces success after accurate results (§4.3). Compare baseline agents with explicit change tracking and restoration planning under matched budgets.
- **Does the diagnostic pattern persist on real hardware?** Evaluation is simulated. Reproduce selected experiments physically and compare success, reading errors, and first decisive failure causes.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported benchmark design and descriptive results; **medium** for broader causal interpretations.

Material missing information includes explicit run counts and seed allocation, statistical uncertainty, numerical wall-clock limits, detailed reference-calibration coverage, failure-attribution procedures and agreement, real-hardware validation, and independent verification of the claimed code release.
