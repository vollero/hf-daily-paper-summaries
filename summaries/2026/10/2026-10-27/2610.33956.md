# Continual Learning Exam: Benchmarking Long-Horizon RSI and Experiential Learning in LLM Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.33956) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33956)


## One-sentence takeaway

CLE provides connected professional task ladders where feedback plus revision improves completion, but its reported experiments do not isolate the benefit of accumulated knowledge or demonstrate recursive self-improvement (§4.2).

## Problem and motivation

The authors argue that agents deployed over time should retain experience, reuse effective procedures, and avoid repeated mistakes. Existing evaluations often measure isolated task success or historical information retrieval, leaving a gap between persistent state and demonstrated improvement through experience (§1).

CLE targets this gap with successive professional assignments that share information, artefacts, procedures, and contextual requirements.

## Approach and contributions

- **Connected benchmark:** CLE contains 120 expert-authored, human-verified tasks, each with five gated levels, covering 89 professional roles across 12 domains. Assignments connect through tools and skills, data dependencies, strategy, user context, and domain knowledge (§§1, 3.1).
- **Executable environment and grading:** The benchmark supplies 23,981 input files and 19 stateful mock applications exposing 1,024 APIs. A separate evaluation agent checks submissions against 16,125 human-authored criteria; every criterion must pass (§§3.1–3.5).
- **Controlled submission protocols and baseline evaluation:** The authors compare one submission without diagnostic feedback against up to two submissions with feedback after initial failure, across 17 configurations. Backbone parameters remain frozen while harness state persists across the fixed task stream (§4.1).
- **Flexible evaluation blocks:** Contiguous ladder segments can start at later levels using manually prepared predecessor artefacts, supporting future controlled evaluations (§3.1).

The primary contribution is an evaluation asset and protocol; the paper does not introduce a new learning algorithm.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Best evaluated system remains below half of levels completed | Claude Code with Opus 5 clears 42.17% of 600 levels with one submission and 47.67% with feedback plus one revision; the latter completes all levels in 28/120 tasks, or 23.33%. | Table 1; §4.2 |
| Feedback plus revision usually improves completion | Across 17 configurations, 16 improve; median overall completion gain is 5.33 percentage points between complete benchmark runs. | §4.2; Fig. 9 |
| Initial success does not imply sustained ladder completion | Under feedback plus revision, median cumulative pass rates across configurations are 62.50% at the first level and 8.33% at the fifth, both measured over all 120 tasks. | Table 1; §4.2 |
| Harness choice affects observed performance | Across six shared backbones, Hermes-Agent exceeds OpenClaw in overall completion under both budgets; mean feedback-plus-revision completion is 28.17% versus 16.75%. | §4.2; Appendix F.3 |
| Automated grading broadly matches human item labels | DeepSeek V4 Flash evaluator achieves 96.86% agreement on 732 rubric items from 60 submissions covering all five levels of 12 tasks. | Appendix D; Table 4 |
| Grading is repeatable under fixed conditions | Across three fresh grading sessions, 553/556 items receive identical labels throughout, yielding 99.46% consistency. | Appendix D; Table 5 |
| Later levels impose more simultaneous requirements | Mean checklist size rises from 18.74 items at the first level to 36.83 at the fifth; every item must pass. | Appendix E; Table 6 |

These results establish benchmark difficulty and an observed benefit from the **combined** feedback-and-revision intervention. They do not separate feedback quality from additional attempts or computation. The authors explicitly identify a matched harness-reset comparison as necessary to measure cross-task knowledge retention (§4.2). Completion scores alone also do not establish improved learning procedures, the paper’s definition of RSI (§2.3).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-acknowledged attribution gap:** No reported matched reset comparison isolates retained cross-task knowledge (§4.2).
- **Author-acknowledged selection effects:** Later conditional pass rates concern progressively selected successful tasks and retained experience; small final-transition samples limit interpretation (Appendix F.1).
- **Author-acknowledged resource comparability:** Configurations reach different levels and perform different revisions, so total resource use does not establish efficiency on matched workloads (Appendix F.3).
- **Analyst inference—measurement conflation:** Gating, increasing rubric size, base task competence, and experience reuse all affect completion. Falling cumulative pass rates cannot independently diagnose learning failure (§3.1; Appendix E).
- **Analyst inference—grading uncertainty:** Item-level agreement does not establish level-level accuracy under an all-items-must-pass rule; level-level human agreement is not reported (§3.2; Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference—scope and uncertainty:** Fixed-order evaluation with frozen parameters does not test order robustness or parameter-learning methods. Repeated actor runs and confidence intervals are not reported in the supplied text (§4.1; Table 1).

## Open research questions

1. **How much does retained knowledge improve subsequent tasks?** The reset control is proposed but unreported (§4.2). Next experiment: compare persistent and per-task-reset harnesses with identical models, task order, feedback, and budgets.
2. **How much gain comes from diagnostic feedback itself?** The trials change both feedback and submission allowance (§4.1). Next experiment: compare two-attempt runs with category feedback versus verdict-only feedback under matched execution budgets.
3. **Can agents improve their learning procedures rather than only retain task information?** RSI is defined but not demonstrated (§2.3). Next experiment: compare agents allowed to revise memory-management or verification procedures with agents restricted to storing task knowledge, then evaluate transfer to held-out ladders.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** in the reported benchmark structure and descriptive results; **low** in causal conclusions about continual learning or RSI.

Material missing information includes exact harness versions and retention policies, concrete execution-budget limits, actor-run replication and uncertainty estimates, task-order sensitivity, level-level grading accuracy, and controlled measurements of knowledge retention, forgetting, or learning-procedure improvement.
