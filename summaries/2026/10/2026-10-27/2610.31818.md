# Nested Sparsity for Scalable Pretraining and Continual Learning

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.31818) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31818)


## One-sentence takeaway
NESTOR jointly routes attention and experts through nested columns, achieving lower language-model loss than matched MoE baselines with estimated compute savings and modestly reduced forgetting in the tested settings (§§3–4).

## Problem and motivation
Conventional MoE transformers sparsify MLP computation while sharing attention parameters across tokens. The authors ask whether conditional computation can also select attention parameters and context, then compose hierarchically with expert routing. This matters especially for long contexts, where attention becomes expensive, and continual pretraining, where shared parameters can contribute to forgetting (§1).

The identified gap is that existing attention-routing approaches generally route attention separately from MLPs and repeat routing at each layer (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Nested architecture:** A router assigns each token to several columns for an entire block of layers. Each column has separate attention parameters, attends only among its routed tokens, and contains its own routed experts. This couples attention-parameter, attention-map, and expert sparsity (§2; Figure 1).
- **Empirical evaluation:** The authors compare pretraining scaling, independent domain adaptation, and context extension against dense, MoE, and sliding-window baselines. Ablations support a benefit from nesting beyond column-routed attention or changed expert configuration alone (§§3–4; Appendices C.2–C.3).
- **Theoretical account:** A stylized hierarchical regression model explains potential sample-efficiency, attention-compute, and retention benefits under explicit structural assumptions; it does not prove these properties for the full transformer (§5; Appendix F).

The tested architecture increases active columns with depth and uses global attention in its final block, shared experts, and attention residuals (§2; Appendix B).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Lower pretraining loss across tested scales | At 100M–1B active-parameter scales, trained on DCLM-baseline at context 4,096 and 50 tokens per active parameter, fitted curves estimate MoE requires **1.13–1.29×** and dense models **3.1–4.1×** NESTOR’s compute to match its loss. | §3; Figure 2; Table 3 |
| Largest-scale direct comparison | At the 1B scale with 50B training tokens, held-out cross-entropy is **2.571 nats** for NESTOR versus **2.592** for MoE; stored parameters are **5.53B versus 5.49B**. | Table 3 |
| Nesting receives ablation support | At 500M active parameters and 25B tokens, full NESTOR scores **2.758 nats**, MoE **2.793**, and a single-column variant with NESTOR-style experts **2.794**. | Table 4 |
| Modest retention benefit without replay | Following separate 0.5B-token adaptations at 32k context, mean target-loss reduction is **0.732 versus 0.734 nats**, and mean DCLM loss increase is **0.254 versus 0.261**, for 500M NESTOR versus 500M MoE. | §4.1; Table 6 |
| Retention ordering persists with replay | With approximately 25% replay within the same total adaptation budget, both sparse models average **0.687 nats** target-loss reduction; forgetting is **0.073** for NESTOR versus **0.076** for MoE. | Table 6 |
| Extra context-extension training improves equal-FLOP results | At 1B scale and 64k context, held-out loss is **2.632** for MoE, **2.607** for equal-token NESTOR, **2.599** with repeated documents at equal estimated FLOPs, and **2.574** with fresh documents at equal estimated FLOPs. | Tables 7–8 |
| Theory establishes conditional advantages | Under Model 1, nested prediction has fewer effective estimation dimensions and scores fewer context tokens; untouched coarse-label columns exhibit zero forgetting in the specified gradient-flow setting. | Theorem 4; Proposition 5 |

The direct losses establish an advantage in these experiments. The compute multipliers are fitted estimates, not measured baseline runs trained until matching loss. Equal-FLOP context extension also changes training duration and learning-rate scheduling (Appendix C.5). Neither these experiments nor the stylized theory establish downstream-task or wall-clock benefits.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Models reach only 1B active parameters, evaluation uses held-out loss, and downstream performance after post-training remains untested. Columns use only full causal attention (§6).
- **Analyst inference—statistical uncertainty:** Most scales have one seed; domain adaptation has one run per model, domain, and replay condition. This limits confidence in the small retention differences (Tables 3–6; Appendices C.4–C.5).
- **Analyst inference—compute accounting:** Estimates omit routers, attention residuals, and the output head; adaptation estimates also omit normalization and assume balanced routing. Practical speedups therefore remain unestablished (Appendices C.1, C.4).
- **Analyst inference—theory-to-model gap:** Theory uses supplied labels, one-layer linear attention, squared loss, and asymptotic fitting. Its zero-forgetting result does not extend directly to learned, overlapping columns with shared components. Freezing the flat predictor’s redundant value map also removes its cross-label forgetting (§5; Appendix F.2).
- **Analyst inference—comparison scope:** Sliding-window baselines are competitive at some pretraining scales, so the headline advantage over full-attention MoE does not establish superiority over all efficient attention designs (Appendix C.2; Figures 11–13).

## Open research questions
- **Do gains survive larger models and downstream evaluation?** The authors leave both unresolved. Next experiment: larger matched-resource pretraining followed by identical post-training and task benchmarks (§6).
- **Is the retention advantage reproducible across sequential shifts?** Current adaptations are independent, single-seed runs. Next experiment: multiple seeds and ordered domain sequences, measuring retention after each shift (Appendix C.4).
- **Do FLOP savings translate into practical throughput?** Reported accounting excludes several operations. Next experiment: matched-hardware measurements of training throughput, memory, and inference latency across context lengths (Appendix C.1).
- **What hierarchy do columns actually learn?** Column choices contain little corpus identity information, despite theory using coarse labels. Next experiment: controlled corpora with known hierarchical structure and tests of whether routing recovers it (§5; Table 9).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported experimental description; **medium** in broader efficiency and retention generalization.

Material missing information includes multi-seed uncertainty for most results, measured wall-clock and inference performance, downstream benchmarks, larger-scale validation, and an explicit repository URL in the supplied text.
