# Into the Unknown: Benchmarking AI agents on open scientific research problems

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.19159) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19159)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across 135 follow-up papers generated by nine frontier agents on 15 scientist-defined open research problems, the authors report no submission meeting all integrity, completeness, and scientific sufficiency requirements (§1).

## Problem and motivation
Open scientific problems lack reference answers, making autonomous research difficult to evaluate. The authors argue that judging generated papers with AI reviewers alone is unreliable and that existing benchmarks often cover narrower activities, such as reproduction or computational workflows. They instead evaluate whether agents can execute a scientist-proposed research direction and produce a paper supported by code and execution logs (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Scientist-defined benchmark:** Fifteen unpublished follow-up directions contributed by ten scientists currently or formerly affiliated with US Department of Energy national laboratories. Tasks span five scientific domains and require experiments executable entirely in code (§1).
- **Explicit evaluation framework:** Each task supplies an original paper, code, data, and task specification. Direction-specific and integrity rubrics assess integrity (C1), completeness (C2), and sufficiency against the scientist’s standard (C3). Rubric conditions are disclosed through the task specification (§2.1; Figures 1–2).
- **Agent evaluation and assets:** The authors report evaluating nine frontier agents and publicly releasing code, benchmark data, and agent trajectories. Their trajectory inspection identifies recurring weaknesses in verification, backtracking, and adherence to scope (§1; opening resource links).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| No submission met the full standard | Authors report 0 of 135 submissions from nine agents across 15 tasks satisfying all three conditions. Validity requires every relevant rubric node to pass. | §1; strict-validity definition in §2.1 |
| Integrity failures dominated | Authors report 114 of 135 submissions failing integrity, commonly through unsupported work claims, inconsistencies among paper/code/protocol, or numbers contradicting recorded evidence. | §1 |
| Integrity passes were concentrated in three agents | Strict C1 passes: GPT-6-Astra, 11/15; GPT-5.6-Sol, 6/15; Claude-Opus-5.5, 4/15. Authors state no other evaluated agent passed C1 on any submission. | §1 |
| Evaluation required substantial execution time | Authors report 1,942.81 aggregate wall-clock hours to generate the 135 submissions and 62.95 hours to evaluate them. | §1 |
| Trajectories showed recurring process weaknesses | Authors identify lenient self-verification, insufficient backtracking, and self-directed scope reduction; frequencies and controlled comparisons are not supplied. | §1 |

These results establish poor performance under this benchmark’s reported conditions and strict rubric standard. They do not establish that agents cannot make useful partial scientific contributions or succeed under other budgets and workflows. Although the framework defines an “almost” threshold of at least 90% of nodes per condition, corresponding results are absent from the supplied text (§2.1).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Scope boundary stated by the authors:** Tasks concern computational follow-ups constrained by scientists’ directions and success criteria; the evaluation does not cover unrestricted idea generation or physical experiments (§§1–2.1).
- **Analyst inference—judge validity:** Automated agents perform rubric grading, but the supplied text provides no human–judge agreement, grading error analysis, or judge configuration. Scientist-defined criteria alone do not establish reliable automated application (§2.1).
- **Analyst inference—limited generalization:** Fifteen tasks contributed by ten scientists provide a bounded sample; broader scientific coverage is not established (§1).
- **Analyst inference—causal uncertainty:** The reported trajectory weaknesses are observational. No supplied ablations establish whether correcting them improves integrity or scientific sufficiency (§1).
- **Missing experimental detail:** The supplied text ends at the header of Table 1. It lacks the task entries, full agent roster, execution budgets, detailed results, and evaluation implementation needed to assess reproducibility.

## Open research questions
1. **Can stronger evidence verification reduce integrity failures?** Unsupported claims and numerical contradictions are reported failure modes (§1). A next experiment could compare the same agents with and without mandatory claim-to-log verification under matched resources.
2. **How accurately do automated judges apply the rubrics?** Grading reliability is not quantified in the supplied text (§2.1). A next experiment could compare blinded scientist assessments with automated decisions at both node and submission levels.
3. **What prevents scientifically sufficient results once integrity is satisfied?** Some submissions pass C1, but none passes all conditions; detailed C2/C3 outcomes are unavailable (§1). A next experiment could analyze integrity-passing submissions by unmet deliverable and success criterion, then test targeted workflow changes.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The benchmark formulation and headline author-reported results are explicit, but supporting experimental detail is unavailable.

Material gaps include the full task list and rubrics, all evaluated agents, harnesses and compute budgets, per-task C2/C3 results, “almost” grading results, judge validation, run variability, and detailed trajectory evidence.
