# Embeddings Are Overpriced? A Frozen 16 MB Vocabulary for Small and Ternary Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.22428) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22428)


## One-sentence takeaway
In a controlled, single-seed experiment, a frozen vocabulary derived from pretrained embeddings substantially reduces trainable parameters while preserving several benchmark scores, at a reported 0.20-nat validation-loss penalty versus a learned table (§§3–4).

## Problem and motivation
Vocabulary embeddings can dominate small language models: the paper reports that Gemma 3 270M allocates 168M of 268M parameters to its table. Compressing the transformer body makes this vocabulary cost more prominent (§1).

The authors ask whether a large trainable input/output table earns its storage and training cost. Their earlier frozen-vocabulary models changed multiple components simultaneously; this study supplies a matched comparison of learned and frozen vocabularies (§§1, 7).

## Approach and contributions
- **Frozen shared vocabulary:** Center Gemma’s pretrained embedding matrix, project onto its top 512 principal components, and retain projection signs. The resulting vocabulary occupies 16.8 MB. Small trained input/output projections connect it to the transformer, and a unigram-frequency prior adjusts output scores (§2).
- **Controlled comparison:** Train identical Gemma-shaped bodies on identical batches: A uses a learned tied table; B uses frozen codes and a fixed prior. C combines frozen codes with a ternary BitNet body, a different schedule, and a trained prior (§3).
- **Deployment and vocabulary-extension evidence:** Evaluate a custom CPU implementation and add biomedical tokens without further training (§§4–5). The frozen-code concept and kernel lineage predate this paper; the principal new contribution is their controlled evaluation (§7).
- **Reported release:** Training data, checkpoints, code, logs, codes, and per-question evaluation outputs are provided under non-commercial research conditions, with additional Gemma terms for derived assets (§8).

## Evidence and main results
Here, A is the learned-table float model, B the frozen-table float model, and C the frozen-table ternary model.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Fewer trainable parameters, higher validation loss | A: 268.1M parameters; B: 101.0M. After the same 2.05B training tokens, B has 0.20 nats/token higher validation loss. | §§3–4; Table 2 discussion |
| Mixed benchmark effects | Zero-shot, full test sets: B exceeds A by 1.6 accuracy points on LAMBADA, ties on HellaSwag and PIQA, and trails by 3.4 points on ARC-Easy. “Tied” means the paired 95% bootstrap interval includes zero. | §§3–4; Tables 2–3 discussion |
| Frozen codes help rare targets | For validation targets seen 10–99 times in training, B and C have respectively 2.3 and 1.7 nats/token lower loss than A; evaluation uses 512 held-out sequences. | §4; Table 4 discussion |
| Ternary deployment retains some benchmark performance | C’s deployed file is 46.2 MB. It ties A on LAMBADA and PIQA, trails by 0.9 accuracy points on HellaSwag and 4.9 on ARC-Easy, and has 0.28 nats/token higher validation loss. | §4; Tables 2–3 discussion |
| Large reported CPU generation advantage | C generates 615–740 tokens/s using 62 MB memory; versus A’s Q8_0 GGUF, the reported advantage is 5.3–6.4× throughput and 7.6× lower memory. Setting: Xeon W-11955M, eight threads, greedy decoding, 128-token context, 16-bit KV cache. | Abstract; §4; Table 5 caption and discussion |
| Vocabulary extension is feasible but degrades quality | Adding 10,000 biomedical words without training incurs a reported approximately 3% quality cost for frozen vocabularies and 43% degradation for A. The evidence supports a storage–quality tradeoff at this architecture and training budget. It does not establish general superiority over learned embeddings or isolate the cause of C’s performance. Frozen codes import information from a pretrained model, whereas A’s table starts randomly initialized (§§2–3). The supplied text contains table captions and narrative summaries, but omits the actual table cells and plotted figures, limiting inspection of absolute scores and uncertainty intervals.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ limitation—training variability and budget:** Each arm has one seed; bootstrap intervals cover evaluation questions, not training runs. The authors acknowledge that A’s larger table is undertrained at this budget and may benefit more from additional data (§6).
- **Authors’ limitation—confounded ternary comparison:** C changes the body, schedule, and prior together. A partial trained-prior ablation exists, but the schedule effect is not isolated (§6).
- **Authors’ limitation—pretrained dependency:** Codes inherit Gemma’s strengths, weaknesses, and licensing terms. Constructing the source embedding through a separate small-model training stage remains untested (§6).
- **Authors’ limitation—extension costs:** Adding tokens worsens quality in every tested setting where the tokenizer already covers the words (§§5–6).
- **Authors’ limitation—deployment scope:** CPU results use one machine and different implementations; B has no kernel, and prefill performance is not compared (§6).
- **Analyst inference:** A–B measures the practical replacement package—pretrained binary codes, interface maps, and a fixed frequency prior—not freezing alone. The supplied text reports no complete ablation separating these ingredients (§§2–3).

## Open research questions
- **Does the tradeoff persist with more training data?** The authors expect A’s advantage to grow, but provide no longer run (§6). Next experiment: repeat matched A–B training at larger token budgets with multiple seeds.
- **Which frozen-vocabulary ingredients matter most?** Pretrained semantics, binary coding, and the prior are not independently isolated. Next experiment: compare semantic and random codes, vary code width, and ablate the prior under a fixed training setup.
- **What explains C’s quality gap?** Quantization, schedule, and prior change together (§6). Next experiment: run a factorial comparison of these components.
- **Can added tokens avoid quality loss?** Tested code and prior constructions all incur a cost (§5). Next experiment: evaluate alternative constructions or brief adaptation against a learned-table baseline initialized from constituent-token embeddings.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied methods and narrative results support the main tradeoff, but important numerical evidence is unavailable.

Missing information includes actual table cells, most absolute benchmark scores and confidence intervals, figure data, repeated-seed results, a complete component ablation, and measurements across additional architectures, training budgets, and CPU platforms. Released artifacts are described by the authors but were not inspected.
