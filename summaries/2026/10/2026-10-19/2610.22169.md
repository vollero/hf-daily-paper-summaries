# Lexical Reference Frames: Interpreting LM Residual Streams in Native Semantics

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.22169) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22169)


## One-sentence takeaway
LexFrame reads intermediate language-model states through approximations of the model’s remaining computation, achieving substantially better output agreement and intervention fidelity than tested lenses on two model families, but without demonstrated inference savings (§§4.1–4.4; Appendix B).

## Problem and motivation
Intermediate residual-stream states lack the direct vocabulary interpretation available at a model’s input and output. Fixed lenses apply one map per layer regardless of context; point-cloud statistics describe state distributions rather than how remaining layers use individual states. The authors argue that faithful interpretation requires directions that adapt to both layer and context, which matters for reading predictions, attributing component effects, and steering outputs (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Native reference frame:** Define token readouts as gradients of centred output logits through the remaining layers, and lexical writing directions through a pseudoinverse of the transport Jacobian. Derive a coherence bound on how closely a fixed direction can match context-dependent readouts (§3.2; Appendices C.1–C.2).
- **Controlled approximations:** LexFrame[n] retains the remaining layers but permits only a specified number of attention exchanges between positions. Withheld attention is replaced by an input-dependent ridge-regression estimate calibrated on unlabelled text. LexFrame[1] allows direct prefix access while withholding subsequent exchanges within that prefix (§3.3).
- **Empirical applications:** Evaluate readings, state substitution, lexical writing, steering, attribution, and training checkpoints. The method avoids fitting to final outputs, although its attention approximation is fitted in closed form (§4; Appendix A.1).

## Evidence and main results

Paired values below are **SmolLM3-3B / OLMo3-7B**. Unless specified otherwise, evaluation uses WikiText-103 test sequences of 128 tokens; layer averages exclude embedding and output layers (Appendix A.1).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Context adaptation improves readout alignment | At mid-depth, LexFrame[1] achieves cosine **0.97 / 0.97** with native lexical readouts, versus fixed-direction coherence bounds **0.30 / 0.35**; direction evaluation covers 64 frequency-spanning labels. | §4.1; Appendix C.2 |
| One attention order closes most of the output gap | Layer-averaged KL(model output ∥ reading): LexFrame[0] **0.87 / 0.93**, LexFrame[1] **0.16 / 0.15**, LexFrame[2] **0.06 / 0.05 nats**. LexFrame[1] top-token agreement is **0.86 / 0.86**. | §4.1; Figure 4; Table 1 |
| Reading distances predict substitution effects | Across **7,961 state pairs per layer**, LexFrame[1] reading divergence has layer-averaged rank correlation **0.94 / 0.95** with output changes after substitution; cloud distances and lenses reach at most **0.28**. | §4.2; Table 2 |
| Lexical bases write tokens more selectively | Moving states by **0.1 of their norm**, the target token gains most in **0.84 / 0.97** of cases using the LexFrame[1] basis, versus **0.54 / 0.73** using its readout; evaluation uses 64 states per layer and 64 labels. | §4.3; Table 3 |
| Selective steering survives approximation | For runner-up-versus-top-token steering, the LexFrame[1] basis flips **0.71 / 0.82** of predictions while changing other tokens by less than **0.1 nats**, versus **0.73 / 0.85** for the native basis. | §4.4; Table 5 |
| Nonlinear coefficient differences improve attribution | Against measured single-position component-removal effects on 64 sequences, LexFrame[1] attribution correlation is **0.88 / 0.90**, versus **0.62 / 0.68** for native-Jacobian linear attribution patching. | §4.4; Table 6 |
| Early confidence does not establish speedup | A threshold selected on half the sequences for **0.95 model agreement** leaves **0.45 / 0.42** of layers unused on the other half, versus at most **0.04** for lenses; computing LexFrame[1] still executes later layers. | Appendix B, “Settling depth”; Table 7 |
| Longer-context readings remain close | On 64 sequences of **1,024 tokens**, LexFrame[1] KL rises to **0.25 / 0.18 nats**, with top-token agreement **0.84 / 0.86**. | Appendix B, “Context length” |

These results support fidelity to the model’s own predictions and specified local interventions. They do not establish semantic truth, broad task-level benefits, or practical acceleration. The native frame’s exact output agreement is true by construction; finite-order approximation quality is the empirical finding (§3.3).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated computational limit:** LexFrame[1] saves no computation in the reported early-exit analysis. Reading every layer requires repeated execution of remaining layers (Appendix B; Appendix C.3).
- **Author-stated locality:** Readouts describe local sensitivity; coefficients require nonlinear transport. Bases provide first-order writing directions rather than global semantic coordinates (§4.3; Appendix C.1).
- **Analyst inference—generalisability:** Evaluation covers two model families and one English corpus, with context tests at the reported lengths. Transfer to other architectures, domains, or substantially longer contexts remains unestablished.
- **Analyst inference—comparison scope:** The tuned lens is explicitly omitted because it fits model outputs. The directional bound does not replace an empirical comparison of its prediction quality or cost (§4 setup).
- **Uncertainty and evidence availability:** Results come from one run with fixed sampling seeds; sequence-bootstrap intervals are described. The supplied text contains table captions and narrative results, but omits the actual table bodies and figure plots (Appendix A.1).
- Hallucination detection, knowledge editing, and compression are proposed applications, not evaluated outcomes (Appendix D).

## Open research questions
1. **Can faithful readings become computationally useful exits?** Remaining-layer transport prevents demonstrated savings. Test a cheaper approximation and measure end-to-end latency alongside output agreement.
2. **How robust are finite orders outside the evaluated distribution?** Evidence is confined to WikiText-103 and two families. Test new domains, architectures, and longer contexts while separating calibration-distribution changes from evaluation changes.
3. **Does token-level steering extend to sustained behaviour?** Experiments target the runner-up token at one state. Test multi-token targets and measure downstream generation quality and unintended changes.
4. **How competitive are trained lenses under matched budgets?** Tuned-lens readings are absent. Compare output fidelity, calibration, intervention performance, and total cost under matched data and compute budgets.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in this summary of the supplied evidence; broader applicability remains uncertain.

Material missing information includes complete numerical table entries and figure plots, per-condition uncertainty intervals, inference latency and memory measurements, tuned-lens comparisons, and evaluated downstream applications beyond the reported token-level settings.
