# SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.21082) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21082)


## One-sentence takeaway
On SciΠvot’s selected research-decision cases, four of five tested models score below chance with project records, while the best tested project condition reaches 63.5% accuracy versus 73.4% for fresh researchers (§4.2).

## Problem and motivation
Scientific agents can execute experiments continuously while choosing experiments that fail to resolve the research question. The authors identify **Principal Investigator judgment**—diagnosing anomalies, revising assumptions, allocating experimental effort, and maintaining scientific focus—as a recurring source of human intervention (§§1–2).

The evaluation gap is judging consequential next steps under uncertain, project-specific evidence. Existing benchmarks cover replication, executable workflows, and defined objectives; SciΠvot instead tests recognition of documented human corrections using the information available before intervention (§3.4).

## Approach and contributions
- **Correction-based benchmark:** 248 cases from 26 projects across six scientific fields, collected during roughly six months of supervised autonomous research. Each pairs an original agent plan with a human-corrected alternative reconstructed from the full history (§§1, 3.1–3.2).
- **Decision-point evaluation:** Evaluators choose between the plans using a frozen pre-intervention record. Later outcomes inform benchmark construction but are withheld during evaluation. This isolates plan recognition from plan generation (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Judgment taxonomy and diagnostic experiments:** Six overlapping categories describe the decisions; experiments compare five models, project access, requested reasoning effort, reconsideration, and fresh researchers (§§3.3–4; Appendix C).

Cases were selected using model-response variation across conditions. Consequently, this is a diagnostic challenge set rather than a representative sample of research decisions (§3.2).

## Evidence and main results

Accuracy means selecting the human-corrected reference plan; model scores average both option orders within each case.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Most tested models reject corrections more often than they recognize them | On 248 cases with project records at medium effort, four of five models score below 50%; upper bounds of both case and project-bootstrap intervals are below chance. Astra scores 59.5%; five-model mean is 39.2%. | §4.2; Table 4 caption |
| Fresh researchers outperform the best observed model condition | Highest tested project-condition accuracy: Astra, 63.5% at xhigh effort. Fresh researchers: 182/248 correct, or 73.4%, with an agent assistant available. | §4.2; Appendix D |
| Project access lowers accuracy in this protocol | At medium effort on the same cases, five-model mean accuracy is 39.2% with records versus 55.8% with options only. Every model declines; paired 95% intervals exclude zero for four models, but not Astra. | §4.4; Figure 4 |
| Judgment categories reveal uneven performance | Astra’s medium-effort project accuracy is 72.2% on anomaly awareness, 56.1% on planning, and 54.9% on focus; categories overlap. | §4.3; Figure 3 |
| Reconsideration provides an initial gain that partly reverses | Across five models and both orders, mean project accuracy progresses from 39.2% to 44.0% to 42.3%. First review repairs 422 errors and introduces 302; second repairs 264 and introduces 305, each over 2,480 transitions without correctness feedback. | §4.5; Figure 5; Appendix A.4 |
| Requested effort has inconsistent effects | In the project-condition effort comparison, Sol declines from 36.1% at low to 29.2% at max, while four models improve from low to max. Effort labels do not represent matched compute budgets. | §4.5; Limitations |

These results establish difficulty recognizing the designated corrections on this selected set. They do not establish general research failure rates or autonomous research productivity. The project-versus-options comparison combines retrieval, interpretation, and client-interface effects; it does not isolate why context hurts. The authors interpret preference for original agent plans as an on-policy/off-policy asymmetry, but the reported comparisons do not independently establish that causal mechanism (§§4.2, 4.4).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ scope limits:** Evaluation covers theoretical and computational work, excluding wet-lab execution and specialized commercial software workflows. It tests recognition, not generating plans or conducting research (Limitations).
- **Selection and dependence:** Model responses helped select cases. Several cases share projects or interventions, and project sizes vary; the authors report cluster-bootstrap intervals but caution against population-wide conclusions (§3.2; Appendices A.2–A.3, C.2).
- **Protocol comparability:** Reading tools differ across clients, and requested effort settings have unequal, unspecified compute budgets. Reconsideration lacks external feedback (Limitations; Appendix A.1).
- **Analyst inference—reference validity:** Retrospective reconstruction can introduce hindsight or goal-change ambiguity. The authors explicitly distinguish decision-time evidence from later outcomes, but the supplied text does not quantify independent agreement on reference-plan validity (§3.2; Appendix C.2).
- **Analyst inference—writer sensitivity:** A small controlled writer pilot shows that wording changes absolute difficulty. Its lack of a writer-model advantage does not establish general robustness to alternative reconstructions (Appendix A.5).
- **Reproducibility:** Individual response logs are excluded from release, and frozen project records are described as awaiting repository hosting (Data availability).

## Open research questions
1. **Can correction-based training improve autonomous research?** Preference optimization and reward learning are proposed, not evaluated (§5). Train on project-separated cases, then measure judgment accuracy and required human interventions on new projects.
2. **Which parts of project context cause errors?** The current comparison bundles records, retrieval, and interfaces (§4.4). Hold the interface fixed and compare raw records, primary evidence only, and records with inherited agent interpretations removed.
3. **Can feedback prevent reconsideration from undoing corrections?** Reviews here receive no correctness feedback (§4.5). Compare unguided review with code-grounded checks or independent verification under matched compute budgets.
4. **How stable are labels and scores under independent reconstruction?** One model writes the released set, and the writer pilot is limited (Appendix A.5). Have independent researchers reconstruct and adjudicate held-out cases, then compare agreement and model rankings.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied prose clearly supports the core protocol and headline findings, but many tables appear only as captions.

Material gaps include complete per-model results and numerical confidence intervals, the full project/domain distribution, human time budgets and assignment details, actual inference compute, independent reference-label agreement, and individual response logs. The text also leaves frozen-record availability unresolved.
