# BRACE: Adapting Whole-Body References for Force and Terrain Aware Humanoid Motion Tracking

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.07052) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07052)


## One-sentence takeaway
BRACE adapts unloaded, flat-ground motion references to terrain and hand wrenches, then distills force-exertion and compensation teachers into a proprioceptive humanoid controller that reduces simulated positional drift under load (§§III–IV).

## Problem and motivation
Human motion references typically specify body trajectories without the terrain geometry or interaction forces needed for physical work. Tracking them literally can conflict with the hand displacement and body lean required to push, lift, or resist loads.

The authors identify a gap between force-capable controllers without dense whole-body pose targets and terrain-adaptive trackers that reject loads without accepting commanded interaction wrenches. BRACE aims to combine these capabilities while letting a remote operator supply flat-ground motion (§§I–II).

## Approach and contributions
- **Joint reference adaptation:** Terrain adjustment changes foot heights, foot orientations, and root elevation. Wrench adjustment jointly limits arm effort, displaces hand targets through a compliance model, and shifts the center of mass for balance; whole-body inverse kinematics reconciles the targets (§III-A).
- **Complementary privileged teachers:** PPO trains an exertion teacher against a virtual reaction field and a compensation teacher under external hand loads. Both learn against terrain-adjusted targets, with teacher-specific effort budgets (§III-B).
- **One deployable student:** DAgger distills both teachers into a flow-matching controller conditioned on proprioceptive history, motion goals, interaction mode, and wrench commands. Deployment requires neither a height map nor measured external wrench (§III-C).

The contribution is their integration for whole-body tracking; PPO, DAgger, and flow matching are adopted components. The authors’ claim of being the first tracker combining these capabilities is not independently established by the supplied text (§I).

## Evidence and main results

All numerical comparisons below are author-reported. Figure 3 uses equal-weight means over PHUMA and held-out Bones-seed splits in G1 simulation; nominal terrain grades span 4.6–17.7° (§IV-A).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Better compensation on flat ground | At **20 N per hand**, success is **10.7 percentage points higher** and MPJPE-G **40% lower** than the best baseline reported for the comparison. | §IV-B1; Fig. 3a |
| Larger compensation advantage on slopes | At **20 N per hand**, success margin is **22.9 percentage points**, with MPJPE-G **43% lower** than the best baseline. | §IV-B2; Fig. 3b |
| Better flat-ground force exertion | At **9 N commanded per hand**, success exceeds HEFT by **5.7 percentage points**, MPJPE-G is **35% lower**, and force error is **34% lower** than the closest baseline. | §IV-C1; Fig. 3c |
| Largest success margin under combined demands | At **9 N commanded per hand on slopes**, success margin is **33.7 percentage points**, with global error **42% lower** and force error **47% lower** than the closest baseline. | §IV-C2; Fig. 3d |
| Lower torque error | Across commands of **0.5–2.5 N·m**, authors report lowest error at every command and reductions of **33% on flat ground** and **32% on slopes** versus the closest baseline; the reduction’s aggregation is unspecified. | §IV-D; Fig. 4 |
| Hardware application feasibility | Authors demonstrate teleoperation and autonomous execution after VLA fine-tuning; task-level success rates and comparative hardware force measurements are not supplied. | §IV-E; Figs. 1, 5 |

These results support improved tracking under the tested simulated loads, especially preservation of global position rather than tighter local pose imitation. They do not establish superiority over force-commanding controllers, which were excluded because their interfaces lack whole-body references (§§IV-A, IV-C2).

Force error compares **episode-average measured force against the feasibility-limited target**, only for completed exertion motions. It therefore does not establish instantaneous force accuracy or accuracy against the original uncapped command (§IV-A).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Explicit scope:** Terrain is restricted to conditions the authors consider manageable through proprioception, including slopes, surface irregularities, and small platforms (§I). Quantitative terrain results focus on slopes (§IV-A).
- **Explicit comparison gap:** Relative force accuracy against force-commanding controllers remains untested (§IV-C2).
- **Analyst inference — component attribution:** No component ablations are supplied, so gains cannot be isolated to reference adaptation, the two teachers, flow matching, or auxiliary losses.
- **Analyst inference — measurement limits:** Tracking errors stop at the first height/orientation violation, and force errors exclude incomplete motions. These summaries provide limited evidence about full-episode failures and transient contact behavior (§IV-A).
- **Analyst inference — reproducibility and uncertainty:** Test counts, seed variation, confidence intervals, exact split identifiers, and quantitative hardware repeatability are absent.
- **Textual ambiguity:** §III-B first specifies exertion error against the feasible wrench, then describes it against the commanded wrench. Figure 3 emphasizes SONIC/HEFT comparisons despite the broader baseline list; detailed results for all listed methods are unavailable.

## Open research questions
- **Which components drive the gains?** No ablations isolate them. A next experiment could remove terrain adaptation, hand leads, COM bracing, and flow matching separately under the same evaluation.
- **Does force accuracy transfer across real contacts?** Quantitative exertion uses a virtual spring–damper field. Test instrumented hardware contacts with varied stiffness, reporting transient error, overshoot, and completion.
- **How robust is proprioceptive adaptation beyond slopes?** Other terrain types are within the stated scope but lack comparable quantitative results. Evaluate held-out rough surfaces and platforms with controlled load changes.
- **How reliable are mode transitions?** Training includes ramped exertion/compensation switches, but transition-specific results are absent (§III-C). Measure tracking error and recovery during switches and sudden load release.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly describes the method and comparative simulation results, but lacks component ablations, statistical uncertainty, complete numerical curves, detailed baseline configurations, quantitative hardware validation, and confirmed artifact availability.
