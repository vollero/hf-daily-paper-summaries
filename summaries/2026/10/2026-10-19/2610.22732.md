# TokenEconomizer: Experience-Driven Black-Box Cost Control for Coding Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.22732) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22732)


## One-sentence takeaway
TokenEconomizer learns external cost-control policies from earlier coding-agent runs, reducing reported mean cache-aware cost by 18.4–30.9% across five SWE-Bench agent–model settings while improving Pass@1 in four and lowering it by 1.0 percentage point in the fifth (§4.2).

## Problem and motivation
Coding agents repeatedly pay for persistent instructions, tool schemas, and growing interaction histories. Isolated-task optimization misses recurring costs across work in the same environment: rediscovering operational facts, loading unused tools, and persisting in failure loops. The authors find that fixed compression and navigation strategies help some repositories but increase costs in others, motivating adaptation from completed traces (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Sequence-level cost accounting:** Optimize chronological task streams, charging all auxiliary controller calls to the task that triggers them and distinguishing uncached input, cached input, and output usage (§3; Appendix E).
- **Experience-grounded micro-skills:** Propose short operational facts from prior traces; use executable detectors, confirmations across non-overlapping edited files, need rates, and contradiction-driven retirement to control injection (§3.1).
- **Adaptive tool pruning:** Remove persistently unused or judged-irrelevant tools while protecting core tools and periodically restoring the full catalog for re-auditing (§3.2).
- **Failure-loop intervention:** Combine historical successful-run lengths, rule-based warning signals, and an LLM judge to redirect stalled runs before terminating persistent loops (§3.3).

The central contribution is their integration into a controller operating through exposed prompts, configuration, and execution events, without changing model weights or agent-loop code (§3; Appendix J).

These results support cost reductions across the tested workloads and harnesses. They measure **price-weighted, cache-aware usage**, rather than raw token-count reductions. “Comparable quality” remains the authors’ characterization: the supplied text lacks the main tables’ numerical bodies, preventing inspection of complete quality rates, variability, and any statistical equivalence claim.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated decision risks:** Skills can become stale, removed tools can become necessary, and productive difficult tasks can be mistaken for loops. Retirement, re-auditing, and redirection provide recovery mechanisms, but do not eliminate these errors (§6).
- **Author-stated scope limits:** Minimal harnesses are untested, and SWE-Bench streams contain independent fixes from fresh checkouts rather than actual interactive user sessions (§6; Appendix A).
- **Analyst inference—development overlap:** Hyperparameters were fixed using earlier development runs on the same six SWE-Bench repositories. This limits evidence for generalization to wholly unseen software-repair repositories, despite the additional benchmark evaluations (Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference—selection and accounting:** Web-Bench excludes projects where default execution fails early; cost is paired only over tasks both arms reach. Reported savings therefore do not establish performance across all projects or complete intended streams (Appendix D; Table 2 caption).
- **Analyst inference—feedback dependency:** Turn budgets use benchmark success labels. Performance with delayed, noisy, or unavailable user feedback remains untested (§3.3).
- **Observed detector weakness:** One micro-skill incorrectly treated attempted test commands as confirmations of validity and was injected before contradiction retired it, illustrating that executable detectors can still encode weak evidence (Appendix I.1(c)).

## Open research questions
- **Does adaptation transfer to unseen repair repositories?** Development used the evaluation repositories. Freeze the controller and evaluate chronologically on entirely held-out repositories (Appendix C).
- **How robust is control to imperfect success feedback?** Budgets use grader outcomes. Replace these with delayed or noisy feedback and measure savings alongside premature termination rates (§3.3).
- **How does performance change in interactive sessions?** SWE-Bench resets workspaces, while headless runs exclude human interaction. Test evolving developer sessions with changing tool needs and measure quality, cost, and recovery delays (§6; Appendix G).
- **Which safeguards are necessary?** Single-component ablations do not isolate confirmation, need gating, retirement, re-auditing, or redirect-before-stop. Ablate these safeguards separately and measure incorrect interventions and quality losses (§4.2; Appendix H).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly describes the method and headline results, but omits numerical bodies for Tables 1–5, including complete quality results, standard deviations, and controller configuration. Absolute costs, concrete pricing weights, statistical significance or equivalence tests, and a code/data release location are not established in the supplied text.
