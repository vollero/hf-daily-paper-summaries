# Game2Real-Bench: Do Toy Games Reveal and Transfer to Real-World Visual Reasoning?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.18834) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18834)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across 25 VLMs, toy-game and real-world scores correlate strongly, but randomly assigned one-shot game examples yield selective capability gains and a small average overall decline rather than reliable general transfer (§4.3; Appendices D.1–D.3).

## Problem and motivation
Procedural games offer scalable generation and verifiable answers, while natural scenes preserve real-world visual complexity. The authors identify a gap: existing evaluations do not directly establish whether game performance reflects real-world visual reasoning or whether solved game examples help real-world tasks. They address both questions through tasks organized by shared reasoning requirements rather than visual resemblance (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Aligned evaluation benchmark:** Game2Real-Bench combines 1,000 independently generated game questions with 400 curated real-world questions across fine-grained perception, spatial reasoning, constraint reasoning, and sequential planning. Alignment is at the reasoning-requirement level, without requiring paired samples (§3; Appendix B.3).
- **Verified generation resource:** Game2Real-Dataset contains 23,893 samples across 18 games and 65 QA families, supported by executable generators and task-specific verifiers. Separate seeds, layouts, semantic fingerprints, and image hashes distinguish the evaluation set from the dataset (§§3.2–3.4; Table 2).
- **Empirical analysis:** The authors evaluate 25 VLMs, test randomized one-shot transfer on four Qwen models, examine correlation robustness, and analyze inference configurations and representative failures (§4; Appendix D). The abstract states that the benchmark, dataset, and code are available.

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong aggregate correspondence | Across 25 model observations, separately computed scores on 1,000 game and 400 real-world samples give Pearson **r = 0.935** and Spearman **ρ = 0.935**. | Appendix D.1; Table 10 |
| Correspondence survives several robustness checks | With capability-balanced game scores across 22 open-weight models, Pearson r is **0.960**, with model-bootstrap 95% CI **[0.913, 0.981]**; excluding SpatiaLQA yields **0.946** under real-world sample averaging. | Appendix D.2; Table 11 |
| Benchmark remains challenging | Full-benchmark mean per-sample scores: GPT-5.6-Sol **69.8%**, Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview **59.6%**, Claude-Opus-4.8-thinking **53.7%**; best open-weight model Qwen3.8-27B **52.6%**. | Table 1; §4.2 |
| Random one-shot transfer is selective | On the same 400 real-world targets, averaging three demonstration assignments and four Qwen models gives **+4.89 percentage points** for constraint reasoning, **−4.92** for fine-grained perception, and **−1.34** overall. Demonstrations contain final answers without reasoning traces. | §4.3; Appendix D.3; Table 13 |
| Transfer varies by model and assignment | Qwen3.5-9B gains **1.49 points** overall on average but improves in only **2/3** assignments; Qwen3.5-4B loses **4.01 points**, with no improving assignment. | Table 14 |
| Main inference configurations outperform designated non-thinking configurations | On all 1,400 samples, gains are **6.23**, **5.95**, **11.09**, and **41.53 points** for Qwen3.5-2B, Step3-VL-10B, Gemma4-31B-it, and GPT-5.6-Sol, respectively. Configurations also differ in budgets and/or decoding controls. | Appendix D.4; Table 15 |

The evidence supports aggregate model-score correspondence and capability-dependent effects of this particular prompting policy. It does not establish shared internal reasoning, predictive validity for individual game–real links, or benefits from training on the released dataset. Overall scores combine binary correctness with normalized SpatiaLQA graph F1, so they are not uniformly accuracy measurements (Appendix C.4).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Four capabilities, 18 games, and 400 real-world samples do not cover natural visual reasoning broadly. Transfer experiments address only one-shot prompting; training and multi-shot transfer remain untested (§5).
- **Alignment limits:** Only 14 games have primary real-world annotations, although all 18 contribute to the main correlation. Capability labels overlap, and aggregate correlation does not validate each designated link (Appendices B.3 and D.1).
- **Analyst inference—transfer mechanism remains unresolved:** Random demonstrations can cross capability boundaries. The experiment measures their combined content, formatting, and context effects; it does not isolate reasoning transfer from generic prompting effects (Appendix D.3).
- **Analyst inference—thinking attribution is weak:** Appendix D.4 reports differing decoding settings and output budgets; Gemma’s recorded thinking flag is false in both configurations, and Step3’s main server template was not retained. These comparisons do not cleanly isolate thinking mode.
- **Analyst inference—verification is not fully independent:** Rolling Cube checking recomputes labels but shares rotation and layout functions with generation, leaving potential shared implementation errors (Appendix B.2).
- **Uncertainty limits:** Correlation robustness includes bootstrap intervals, but toy-question resampling is excluded. Transfer variability covers three assignments on fixed targets, rather than confidence intervals over new target populations (Appendices D.2–D.3).

## Open research questions
1. **Does matching demonstrations to reasoning requirements improve transfer?** Random assignment leaves this unresolved. Compare random, capability-matched, and task-matched selection under identical inference settings (Appendix D.3).
2. **Do game demonstrations help through reasoning content or presentation cues?** Their effects are bundled. Compare full demonstrations with format-only, text-only, and reasoning-trace variants using matched context budgets (Appendix D.3).
3. **Does aggregate correspondence extend to individual capability links?** Current robustness checks concern model-level aggregates. Test capability-specific correlations and generalization to newly curated sources with balanced coverage (Appendices B.3 and D.2).
4. **Can game training produce durable real-world gains?** The dataset is provided, but training transfer is untested. Compare game-based fine-tuning against matched non-game supervision on held-out real-world tasks (§5).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported benchmark construction and aggregate findings; **medium** in broader transfer and causal interpretations.

Material gaps include independently audited verifier correctness, transfer controls separating content from formatting, target-population uncertainty for transfer gains, fully recoverable inference configurations for every comparison, and evidence from actual game-based training. Some figure cross-references are inconsistent; tables and appendix descriptions provide clearer support.
