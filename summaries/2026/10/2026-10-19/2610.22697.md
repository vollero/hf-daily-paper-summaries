# What It Is, Not What It Says: Securing Agentic Browsing Interactions with Web Semantics

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.22697) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22697)


## One-sentence takeaway
Intact combines element provenance, isolated planning, and capability checks to contain injected instructions, reducing attack-induced plan steps to consent requests in its evaluation, while leaving end-to-end task completion untested.

## Problem and motivation
Web agents flatten webpages into text or screenshots, obscuring who supplied each element and what it can legitimately do. Injected text can then persuade an agent to act on unrelated elements using the user’s authority. The authors identify three gaps: unreliable attribution of third-party content, cross-element influence during planning, and insufficient enforcement outside the LLM (§§I–II).

## Approach and contributions
- **GenTree:** Combines DOM, accessibility information, script-level provenance, and element capabilities. Instrumentation tracks third-party creation and modification, including within first-party documents (§IV-A).
- **Virtual isolation:** Each planning call receives one element, the user request, and a structural log of earlier operations without their text or values. Code binds each decision to its source element (§IV-B).
- **Execution controls:** The harness checks element capabilities and requires consent before acting on third-party elements. The implementation uses stock Chromium, Playwright, and CDP (§IV).
- **Evaluation assets:** The authors report releasing source code and an injected-attack benchmark of captured real webpages (§I).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Instrumentation substantially expands third-party attribution | Across 1,270 captured landing pages, mean third-party element share rises from 10% under standard attribution to 22.7% with GenTree; standard attribution misses 58.7% of identified third-party elements overall. | §V-B1, Table I |
| Isolation reduces attack-induced plan steps | With gpt-6-luna instructed to simulate a persuaded agent, Vanilla follows attacks on 797/1,091 pages (73.1%); Intact produces attack-induced steps on 140/1,091 (12.8%), each at most a consent request under its harness. | §§V-C1–2, Table III |
| Full inbound data copying is absent, but partial guessing occurs | Vanilla copies target data in 345/546 inbound attacks (63.2%); Intact copies the full data in none. One Intact response guesses a matching first word, held for consent. | §V-C2; Appendix B-B |
| Benign permission demand is concentrated | Third-party steps constitute 10.1% of 3,466 benign-plan steps and occur on 112/1,091 tasks; the other 979 tasks would receive no third-party consent interruption. | §V-C2, Table IV |
| Utility loss is small under one judge-based measure | Only Vanilla’s plan is judged appropriate on 40/1,091 tasks (3.7%). Overall appropriateness is 56.2% for Intact and 59.3% for Vanilla; uncertainty is 33.8% and 35.7%, respectively. | §V-D1, Table V |
| Sequential planning adds substantial latency | Across 1,091 benign tasks, median planning time is 83 seconds for Intact versus 2.5 seconds for Vanilla; median costs are $0.0045 versus $0.0021. The slowest excluded pages are absent. | §§V-E–V-E1, Table VI |
| Parallel speedup remains an estimate | Recorded-decision replay estimates median latency falling from 83.2 to 12.5 seconds. The schedule is unimplemented and assumes repeated decisions and unchanged parallel-call latency. | §V-E1, Figure 7 |

These results support containment in the supplied single-page attack setting, rather than resistance to persuasion. Intact’s 12.8% permission appearance rate is not an executed-attack success rate. Likewise, the 3.7% utility figure measures selected-element appropriateness relative to Vanilla, not observed task completion: no evaluation steps were executed (§V-D).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Compromised first parties, multi-page attacks, and availability attacks are excluded. Decoys can still redirect chunk selection or halt progress (§§III, VI).
- **Author-stated attribution gaps:** JavaScript instrumentation assumes scripts do not tamper with it. Coverage excludes shadow roots, cookies, `postMessage` exchanges, and extension-injected content; third-party responses rendered by first-party scripts are not tainted (§§III, IV-A2, V-C3).
- **Author-stated functionality limits:** Isolated calls cannot compare values across elements. Custom elements without declared interactions may be unusable (§§IV-A1, V-D).
- **Evaluation qualifications:** Tasks are LLM-generated; planners and judge use the same model; the judge sees neither operations nor entered values. Its instructions were revised using a pilot retained in the results (§§IV-B1, V-D).
- **Analyst inference:** Landing-page sampling, benchmark exclusions, and omission of the slowest planning cases limit generalization to complex browsing workflows (§§V-A, V-C1, V-E).
- **Analyst inference:** Consent safety remains unestablished because permission counts do not measure whether users recognize and reject malicious requests (§V-C2).

## Open research questions
- **Does containment survive executed, multi-page workflows?** These attacks are outside scope and utility is assessed without execution. Next experiment: run instrumented workflows with injected attacks across navigation boundaries.
- **Can agents compare page values without restoring cross-element injection paths?** The current planner cannot perform such comparisons. Next experiment: test a constrained comparison interface on price-selection tasks with adversarial candidate content.
- **How robust is attribution under uncovered data flows?** Several channels and first-party rendering of third-party responses remain unsupported. Next experiment: construct known-provenance tests for these paths and measure attribution errors.
- **Does consent gating remain effective in use?** The paper measures potential interruptions, not user decisions. Next experiment: evaluate approval accuracy and task completion under mixed benign and malicious consent requests.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly describes the architecture and aggregate results, but supports narrower conclusions than demonstrated deployment security.

Material missing information includes full table cells and figure contents, complete planner and judge prompts, end-to-end execution outcomes, user-consent behavior, broader model comparisons, and implemented parallel-scheduling results. The reported code and benchmark release was not independently verified.
