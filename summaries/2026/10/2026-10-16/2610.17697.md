# LeanPRM: Lean-Grounded Process Supervision for Natural Language Proof Verification

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.17697) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17697)


## One-sentence takeaway
LeanPRM uses paired informal and Lean proofs to generate process-supervision data, improving a Qwen3-4B verifier over PRM800K training on four of five benchmarks, while retaining model-dependent label-quality risks (Table 2; §5).

## Problem and motivation
Open-ended mathematical proofs require checking intermediate reasoning rather than a short final answer. The authors identify a supervision gap: expert step annotations are expensive, continuation-based labels require checkable answers, and formalizing existing informal proofs demands expertise. They seek scalable training labels for verifiers that ultimately read only natural language (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Label-generation framework:** A model generates aligned natural-language and Lean steps without checker feedback. Lean checks the frozen attempt; a second model assesses prose–formal alignment and mathematical versus syntax failures, with one syntax-repair attempt. The first failing step and all subsequent steps receive incorrect labels (§2).
- **Corpus and benchmark:** After filtering formalization-side failures and lexical shortcuts, LeanPRM contains 10,634 proofs and 152,005 step labels without human annotation. LeanPRM-Bench provides 200 held-out proofs with labels, error classes, and formal proofs (§§2.1–2.2).
- **Verifier training and evaluation:** Qwen3 verifiers receive GRPO training using step-label and whole-proof rewards. Controlled comparisons hold the training recipe and update budget fixed while changing training data; separate experiments test model size and proof selection (§3).

The authors claim novelty in combining scalable Lean-grounded labels for natural-language proofs, mathematical error classes, and training a text-only verifier (§1; Table 3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Substantial automatically labeled corpus | 10,634 proofs: 6,370 correct and 4,264 incorrect; 152,005 step labels; no human annotation. | §2.1 |
| Better held-out exact labeling | Qwen3-4B: initial 48.2 ± 1.3%, PRM800K-trained 60.3 ± 1.6%, LeanPRM-trained 74.7 ± 1.8%. Metric requires every step label in a proof to match; 200 proofs. | Table 2; §2.2 |
| Transfer gains on three external benchmarks | LeanPRM versus PRM800K, both 4B and trained for 500 updates: Hard2Verify response balanced accuracy 72.8 versus 63.1%; PRMBench first-error accuracy 47.4 versus 43.3%; IMO-GradingBench binary balanced accuracy 66.4 versus 57.0%. | Table 2; §3; Appendix E |
| ProcessBench remains a weakness | Mean F1 across four subsets: LeanPRM 74.9 ± 0.4% versus PRM800K 83.0 ± 0.3%, using the same training budget. | Table 2 |
| Proof-selection improvement is modest | At N=16 on IMO-GradingBench, correct selected proofs: LeanPRM 35.9%, random selection 32.4%, PRM800K verifier 33.2%; averaged over 30 problems and three decoding seeds. | §3.1; Figure 4 |
| Benefits extend across tested sizes | After 500 updates, LeanPRM-Bench exact accuracy rises from 22.7 to 54.0% at 0.6B, 48.2 to 74.7% at 4B, and 45.7 to 75.0% at 8B. All sizes also improve on Hard2Verify and IMO-GradingBench. | Table 5; Figure 5 |
| Audits motivate filtering but do not certify labels | Blind model audit judged 23–28 of 30 flagged steps sound in each audited formalization-side class, versus 13 of 30 in each of the two audited mathematical classes. | Table 1; §2.1 |

Table 2 and Table 5 report means and population standard deviations across three evaluation runs. IMO-GradingBench uses 987 candidates after context-length exclusions and a binarized human grade; PRMBench uses first-error accuracy rather than its default metric (Appendix E).

The evidence supports better verification under the reported settings and transfer beyond the generation pipeline. It does not isolate the causal value of Lean labels from proof-style training data, establish perfect label validity, or demonstrate benefits from using the verifier to train a prover (§5).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors:** Mathematical-versus-formalization classification and prose alignment depend on models. A single model judge audits samples, leaving possible labeling errors (§5).
- **Authors:** No comparison trains on the same proofs labeled by an LLM judge, so label quality and training-distribution effects remain confounded (§5).
- **Authors:** Training and LeanPRM-Bench share a pipeline and Claude generators; generated negatives may cover a restricted error distribution (§5).
- **Formal-checking caveat:** One positive training proof contains an explicit `sorry` placeholder; transitive dependencies were not checked. No explicit placeholders were found in positive validation or benchmark proofs (Appendix B).
- **Unresolved explanation:** The authors attribute much of the ProcessBench deficit to labeling conventions, but explicitly state that this hypothesis was not directly tested (Appendix G).
- **Analyst inference:** Formatting affects measured capability because unparsable responses count as wrong; the paper documents size-dependent parsing failures (§3.1; Appendix E). Reported variability across decoding runs does not establish variability across independent training runs.
- **Comparison boundary:** The longer † run changes both training duration and data and has validation-selection overlap with some replacement benchmark items; it should not support the controlled main comparison (Appendices B and D).

## Open research questions
1. **How much benefit comes specifically from Lean-grounded labels?** The same-proof LLM-label baseline is absent (§5). Next experiment: train identical verifiers on identical proofs with Lean-derived versus independent model-derived labels.
2. **Do labels remain reliable after filtering?** Borderline mathematical errors and unchecked transitive dependencies remain (§5; Appendix B). Next experiment: conduct an expert audit of the final corpus and recheck accepted proofs with placeholder dependencies excluded.
3. **Does error diversity improve transfer?** Negatives originate from Claude mistakes (§5). Next experiment: add proofs from different generators and human sources, then evaluate on generator-disjoint tests.
4. **Can these verifiers improve proof generation?** Reward-model use is proposed rather than demonstrated (§5). Next experiment: train a prover with LeanPRM verifier rewards and evaluate on independently checked held-out proofs.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported comparisons; **medium** in label validity and broader generalization.
