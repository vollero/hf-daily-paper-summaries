# Mosaic: Multi-Session Agentic Reinforcement Learning

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.22174) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22174)


## One-sentence takeaway
Mosaic combines session-count-aware trajectory advantages with answer-likelihood-based session credit, improving Qwen3.5-4B’s BrowseComp accuracy over broadcasting while reducing average session use in both evaluated execution modes (Table 1).

## Problem and motivation
Context compaction and subagent forking break an agent’s execution into separate contexts. The authors argue that conventional single-session RL poorly matches these workflows, while broadcasting one trajectory advantage across every session cannot distinguish useful sessions or guide session allocation (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Multi-session formulation:** Represents a rollout as multiple training sessions while aggregating token losses at the trajectory level to preserve equal rollout weighting (§2.2).
- **Session-aware trajectory advantages:** Ranks session counts within outcome groups, favoring fewer sessions among successes and giving less negative feedback to longer failures. Bounded modulation preserves outcome ordering; fallback rules supply signals when all outcomes match (§3.1; Appendix A).
- **Session-level credit:** Measures changes in the current actor’s length-normalized ground-truth-answer log-likelihood across session boundaries. It normalizes gains by session type and adds them to trajectory advantages, excluding terminal sessions. Additional gates constrain conflicting corrections (§3.2; Appendix B).
- **Empirical analysis:** Tests compaction and subagent execution and examines session utility using positive information-gain frequency and positive gain per session (§4; Appendix D.3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Compaction improves over broadcasting | On BrowseComp with Qwen3.5-4B, accuracy rises from 35.31% to 38.86%; mean sessions fall from 3.17 to 2.35. | Table 1 |
| Subagent execution improves over broadcasting | Same benchmark/model: accuracy rises from 31.44% to 32.46%; mean sessions fall from 4.43 to 3.77. | Table 1 |
| Compaction session utility increases | Broadcasting → Mosaic: Positive-IG Rate 47.74% → 59.92%; IG/Session 0.86 → 0.89 on BrowseComp. | Table 2; metric definitions in Appendix D.3 |
| Subagent session utility increases | Broadcasting → Mosaic: Positive-IG Rate 41.15% → 51.04%; IG/Session 0.74 → 0.80 on BrowseComp. | Table 2; Appendix D.3 |
| Both components contribute | Authors report improvements from each component, with a clearer combined gain for compaction and a modest combined gain for subagents; exact ablation values are absent from the retrieved text. | §4.3; Figure 3(c–d) |
| Session scoring is reportedly cheaper than turn-level scoring | Authors report lower marginal-gain computation wall-time than IGPO, but numerical timings are unavailable. | §4.2; Figure 3(b) |

These results establish improvements in the reported benchmark/model settings. They do not establish statistical significance, broad model generality, or lower total execution cost: session count is a proxy, and the authors explicitly acknowledge that it need not track cost (§4.5). Information-gain metrics measure answer predictability under the actor, rather than independently verified causal session value (Appendix B.1).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Immediate marginal gain may misrepresent downstream contributions in branching subagent trajectories (§4.3).
- **Author-stated:** Session count is not inherently exploration intensity or execution cost; public training data are scarce, and asynchronous training remains untested (§§4.5–5).
- **Author-stated:** Outcome-group imbalance changes the absolute strength of multiplicative modulation; singleton outcome groups cannot support within-outcome ranking (Appendix A.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Evaluation on one base model and one benchmark limits evidence for transfer to other sizes, tasks, or harnesses (§4.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Utility diagnostics reuse the answer-likelihood quantity used for shaping, so they provide limited independent validation of the proposed mechanism (§3.2; Appendix D.3).
- **Reporting gaps:** No seed variability or uncertainty estimates are supplied. Several prompt/schema blocks and numerical figure contents are missing from the retrieved text, limiting replication and assessment of ablations (Appendix C; Figures 3–7).

## Open research questions
1. **Can credit assignment capture delayed effects in branching trajectories?** The authors identify a mismatch between immediate gain and downstream influence (§4.3). Compare Mosaic with descendant-aware credit on controlled branching tasks.
2. **Do fewer sessions translate into lower actual cost?** Session count is acknowledged as an imperfect proxy (§4.5). Measure accuracy against total tokens, tool calls, latency, and scoring overhead under matched budgets.
3. **Does the method generalize beyond this evaluation?** Only one model and benchmark are reported (§4.1). Repeat the broadcasting comparison across model sizes and additional long-horizon task domains.
4. **Does grouped optimization remain reliable asynchronously?** Experiments alternate rollout and training to avoid asynchrony (§5). Vary policy staleness and group-completion rules while measuring accuracy and trajectory-selection bias.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The method and central table results are explicit, but robustness and mechanism claims have weaker support. Missing information includes uncertainty estimates, exact ablation and timing values, complete prompt/schema text, full cost accounting, and confirmation of released code or checkpoints.
