# Copy-Paste Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.19247) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19247)


## One-sentence takeaway

Adding a learned pointer alongside the vocabulary head improves language-model pretraining and retrieval in the tested models, with ablations supporting the authors’ explanation that an additional gradient pathway drives much of the gain (§§3–4; Appendix H).

## Problem and motivation

Self-attention can retrieve earlier tokens, but standard language models must still predict them through a vocabulary projection whose hidden dimension is much smaller than the vocabulary. The authors argue that this head restricts both expressivity and the training signal reaching the backbone, making copying unnecessarily difficult (§§1–2.1).

The identified gap is whether explicit copying can improve modern decoder-only pretraining even when the backbone already has full-context access. The paper positions its contribution as demonstrating this benefit and investigating its optimization mechanism, rather than inventing pointer-based copying (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Copy-Paste head:** Learned queries and keys score past positions; probabilities for positions containing the same token are summed into a token distribution. An LM-head `<copy>` gate mixes this distribution with conventional vocabulary prediction, while a learned sink permits fallback. Training uses only next-token cross-entropy, without copy labels or auxiliary losses (§2.2, Eq. 7).
- **Pretraining evidence:** Matched-recipe comparisons cover full-attention, sliding-window, hybrid, and Gated DeltaNet backbones, followed by instruction tuning and long-context evaluation (§3; Appendix C.5).
- **Mechanistic evidence:** Gradient measurements, synthetic copying, stop-gradient interventions, and byte-level experiments support the proposed gradient-bottleneck explanation (§4.1; Appendix H).
- **Retrieval memory:** Independently encoded document copy keys support question answering without exposing document contents to backbone attention, enabling reusable document representations (§3.4).

## Evidence and main results

All findings below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Faster convergence and smaller context cache | At 1.7B, CPLMs reach vanilla final validation loss using 1.25× fewer training tokens for Full/Hybrid and 2× fewer for SW. At 4k context, SW CPLM matches Full LM with 12× less cache memory, including copy keys. | §3.1, Fig. 2; Fig. 1 |
| Modest base-model benchmark gains | Across the listed zero-shot benchmarks, 1.7B CPLMs gain 0.4–0.9 accuracy points overall; LAMBADA gains are 2.5–4.0 points versus matched vanilla models. | §3.1, Table 1 |
| Gains survive instruction tuning | After Tulu-3 SFT with contexts up to 16k, average benchmark scores improve by 1.2–5.1 points. Math averages improve by 3.9 points for Full and 7.9 for SW; Hybrid MBPP is an exception to benchmark-level improvement. | §3.2, Table 2; Appendix D |
| Strong single-needle retrieval for SW | For 1.7B SW-256 models, mean accuracy rises from 14.3% to 99.4% across eight insertion depths and context lengths of 1k–64k, with 200 examples per cell. Full improves only from 49.7% to 54.8%; both Full variants fail from 16k onward. | §3.3; Appendix E, Fig. 13 |
| Retrieval works through document copy keys | On 200 HotpotQA validation questions, document-hidden CPLMs retain 90–97% of document-visible accuracy with five distractors and achieve 34–38% EM with 500 distractors, using a cache reported as 50× smaller than Full-model caching. | §3.4, Fig. 5 |
| Backbone gradients account for much of the improvement | In a single-seed, 135M Full experiment, stopping CP gradients into the backbone reduces the validation improvement from 0.031 to 0.008 nats/token, or from 0.49 to 0.12 PPL. | Appendix H.1, Fig. 17, Table 15 |
| Byte-level models show no comparable gain | With vocabulary size 257 below hidden dimension 576, byte-level CPLMs finish within 0.002 nats/byte of vanilla after approximately 20B bytes. | Appendix H.2, Fig. 18, Table 16 |
| Runtime overhead is small in measured settings | On one B300 in bfloat16, 1.7B Full CPLM training throughput is 97% of vanilla at 4,096-token sequences. For 4k/16k prompts followed by 256 greedy tokens, total request time increases 0.8–1.7% across tested configurations. | Appendix B, Tables 5–6 |

These results support improved optimization and retrieval within the tested settings. The interventions strengthen the gradient-path explanation, but do not establish it as the exclusive mechanism. Near-perfect single-needle copying also does not establish general long-context reasoning: difficult multi-key tasks remain near the floor, and SW BABILong performance remains weak (Appendix F).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Trained experiments stop at 1.7B parameters, and the CP pathway directly supports only tokens available in its context (§6). The 9B/30B architectures are untrained timing probes (Appendix B).
- **Author-reported failures:** Full-attention length extrapolation still fails; downstream improvements vary by task and become less uniform after SFT (Appendices E–F).
- **Comparison limits:** Alternative pointer mixtures perform similarly; the authors report small margins and use the gate/sink mainly for convenience. Adapted partition baselines differ from their original implementation (Appendix C.6).
- **Analyst inference:** Mechanistic generalization needs stronger replication: Appendix H uses one seed and one small Full backbone. Byte-level experiments also change text coverage and effective context, so they provide supporting evidence rather than a fully isolated bottleneck intervention.
- **Author-stated risks:** Models lack safety alignment and may reproduce input-sensitive or copyrighted material more faithfully (Ethics statement).

## Open research questions

1. **Do quality gains persist above the trained scale?** Larger models receive timing measurements only. Next experiment: matched-token and matched-compute pretraining comparisons above 1.7B.
2. **How general is the gradient explanation?** Mechanistic interventions cover one small Full backbone. Next experiment: repeat stop-gradient tests across seeds, backbone types, and vocabulary-to-hidden-dimension ratios.
3. **Can copying support harder retrieval and reasoning?** Single-needle success coexists with weak multi-key and BABILong results. Next experiment: vary the number of required facts and intervening transformations while controlling context length.
4. **Can smaller copy keys preserve larger-model retrieval?** The copy-dimension sweep is limited to small-model perplexity. Next experiment: test reduced key dimensions in 1.7B HotpotQA and long-context evaluations (Appendix C.2).
