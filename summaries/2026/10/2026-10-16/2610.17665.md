# RoTA-Bench: Evaluating 3D Spatial Reasoning through Interactive Projection Alignment

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.17665) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17665)


## One-sentence takeaway
RoTA-Bench exposes a large human–model gap in interactive silhouette alignment, with evidence that successful control requires more than improving immediate image overlap (Table 1; §5.4).

## Problem and motivation
The authors ask whether vision-language models can infer spatial structure from images and use visual feedback to execute precise rotations. They identify a gap between static spatial question answering and interactive benchmarks dominated by planning, interface use, or pathfinding: neither directly isolates visual orientation reasoning and rotation control (§§1, 3.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Interactive benchmark:** A solver observes a target silhouette, current silhouette, and depth-shaded auxiliary image, then selects camera-fixed rotations. Success requires projection IoU ≥0.90; numerical IoU and internal poses remain hidden (§3.2; Table 4).
- **Solvable task generation:** Sphere-bounded extrusion converts icons into objects that preserve their canonical silhouette. Reversible initialization guarantees a feasible solution. The collection contains 674 tasks, including a frozen 100-task test set stratified by reference-path length (§3.3; Appendix I).
- **Behavioral diagnostics:** Human, model, agent, and privileged greedy comparisons separate rotation direction from angle selection, examine image–pose disagreement, and ablate auxiliary views and history (§§3.4–5.4). The reproducibility statement reports release of construction code, evaluation code, and the complete dataset.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Large human–model success gap | On 100 test tasks, GPT-5.6-Sol succeeds on 17/100, with 95% Wilson CI [10.89%, 25.55%]; humans succeed on all 400 runs across four passes by 20 participants. Final IoU averages 0.598 versus 0.923. | Tables 1, 5, 11 |
| Immediate overlap maximization is insufficient | Greedy policies enumerate all 24 simulator successors: IoU greedy achieves 39% success and 0.725 final IoU; pose greedy achieves 100% and 0.924 on the same test set. | §5.1; Table 1 |
| Both direction and angle selection differ | Human minus Sol effective-direction rate is 25.16 percentage points [22.79, 27.56]; angle gap is 3.50° smaller [2.42°, 4.60°], using paired task-bootstrap intervals. Angle metrics condition on effective directions. | Appendix K.4; Table 14 |
| History removal disrupts fine control | On the same 30 tasks, full input versus no history yields 5/30 versus 0/30 successes, final IoU 0.5656 versus 0.4822, and 30° action shares of 62.75% versus 96.76%. Paired success difference: 16.67 points [3.33, 30.00]. | Tables 2, 15–16 |
| Image progress can accompany pose regression | Across 211 matched participant–task–state pairs from 51 tasks, actions improving IoU while increasing pose error occur in 19.43% of Sol actions versus 10.90% of human actions. | Table 8; Appendix K.6 |
| Geometry affects observed performance | On two 30-task sets with identical reference-length distributions, Sol achieves final IoU 0.5656 on icon-derived objects versus 0.7179 on Poly Haven meshes; success is 5/30 versus 6/30. Tasks are not individually paired. | Appendix K.8; Table 18 |

The evidence establishes substantial behavioral differences under this protocol. It does not directly reveal internal 3D representations. Pose error is a diagnostic, whereas silhouette overlap defines success; distinct poses can satisfy the projection objective (§3.4). The authors also caution that observed model rankings require paired comparisons before reliable superiority claims can be made (Appendix K.1).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Icon extrusion limits geometric diversity; single-object targets and discrete rotations simplify interaction relative to continuous physical environments (§5.5).
- **Author-stated diagnostic limits:** Symmetries are numerically estimated. Pose alignment and projection success are not equivalent (Appendix D).
- **Author-stated comparison limits:** Shared-state matching controls current pose but not prior observations or actions; some Sol actions appear in multiple participant pairs (Appendix K.6).
- **Analyst inference:** The history ablation supports an input-dependence finding for Sol, but cannot identify whether earlier images, actions, or responses cause the effect. Only one model and a 30-task subset receive this ablation (Appendix K.5).
- **Analyst inference:** The cross-geometry comparison cannot isolate familiarity from other geometry differences because task sets are not individually paired (Appendix K.8).
- **Population boundary:** Human participants are undergraduates; broader participant evaluation remains future work (Appendix C).

## Open research questions
- **Which history components enable fine adjustments?** The ablation removes earlier images, responses, and actions together. Next experiment: remove each component separately on the same tasks (Appendix K.5).
- **Does geometric familiarity improve control independently of shape difficulty?** The mesh comparison changes geometry alongside potential familiarity. Next experiment: compare familiar and unfamiliar shapes matched on geometric properties and reference difficulty (Appendix K.8).
- **Do these findings extend to continuous control and richer scenes?** Current tasks isolate discrete single-object rotation. Next experiment: introduce continuous rotations, occlusions, and multiple objects separately (§5.5).
- **Can interactive training improve generalization?** Reinforcement learning is proposed but not evaluated. Next experiment: train on the reserved tasks and assess held-out icon and mesh geometries (§5.5; Appendix I).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported benchmark and behavioral results; **medium** for explanations involving underlying spatial understanding.

Material gaps include an explicit release URL, independent verification of released assets, repeated model-run variability, human reasoning records, and experiments isolating history components or demonstrating training gains.
