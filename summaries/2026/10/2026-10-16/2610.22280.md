# LEASH: Uncertainty-Aware Weak-to-Strong Escalation and Oversight for Computer-Use Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.22280) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22280)


## One-sentence takeaway
LEASH uses a weak computer-use agent’s hidden-state failure signals to guide escalation and subsequent oversight, achieving 73.66% OSWorld success at an estimated $0.4117 per task versus 72.90% at $0.5094 for always-on Opus 5 in its strongest configuration (Table 1).

## Problem and motivation
Long computer-use tasks create a cost–accuracy trade-off: weak agents often fail, while using a strong model throughout is expensive. The authors identify two gaps: inexpensive decisions about when to escalate, and continued monitoring after control passes to a strong black-box model. Existing monitoring approaches can require extra inference, annotated training data, or repeated sampling (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Reference-based uncertainty:** Collect successful and failed weak-agent trajectories, then fit shared-covariance Gaussian statistics to final-layer query and action representations. The score measures relative Mahalanobis distance to success versus failure references; no gradient-based monitor training is required (§3.2).
- **Initial and runtime escalation:** Quantile-calibrated gates route difficult tasks immediately or transfer control during execution. A strong-model Manager supplies subgoal planning, verification, and reference labels; after handoff, the strong executor retains control (§§3.1–3.3).
- **Shadow oversight:** After handoff, the weak model generates unexecuted candidate actions every three strong-agent steps. High uncertainty triggers reassessment with increased strong-model reasoning effort (§3.4; Appendix A.4).
- **Conditional theoretical justification:** Under shared-covariance Gaussian assumptions, the initial gate optimally captures weak-agent failures at a fixed escalation rate. Maximizing task success additionally requires escalation benefit to increase with the score; the proof does not cover runtime escalation (§5).

The evidence supports useful selective allocation within this OSWorld workflow. It does not establish statistically significant superiority over always-on Opus, causal benefits from uncertainty-selected Shadow interventions, or generalization beyond computer use. Costs include workflow inference calls but represent token-price estimates, rather than measured local hardware costs (Appendix B.1).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Evaluation covers only OSWorld. Weak-agent failure risk can poorly predict escalation benefit when both models struggle. Shadow inputs differ from reference weak-agent trajectories, and its intervention effect is not causally isolated (Limitations).
- **Comparison boundary:** Always-on strong-model references lack the Manager, whereas LEASH includes one. Thus, their comparison measures complete workflows rather than routing alone (Appendix B.1).
- **Calibration boundary:** A 50% target produces realized escalation rates of approximately 43–64%; finite reference data, refitting, and distribution differences affect budget control (Appendices A.3 and B.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Table 4 supports the bundled Shadow procedure, but cannot distinguish uncertainty-based checkpoint selection from the effects of reflection or additional reasoning computation.
- **Reporting gap:** Main success results lack reported confidence intervals or repeated-execution variability; random-allocation variability does not resolve that uncertainty.

## Open research questions
- **Does predicted escalation benefit outperform weak-agent failure risk?** The authors explicitly distinguish them. Next experiment: collect paired weak/strong outcomes and compare benefit-based routing at matched total cost.
- **Does Shadow uncertainty select better interventions?** Its causal contribution remains unresolved. Next experiment: compare uncertainty-triggered, random, and periodic checkpoints with matched intervention counts and reasoning budgets.
- **When does the initial gate help?** The reported no-Shadow ablation favors runtime-only execution. Next experiment: vary the initial/runtime budget split while holding oversight and total cost fixed.
- **How robust is calibration under task shift?** Realized escalation rates depart from targets. Next experiment: calibrate on one application group and evaluate success, cost, and escalation-rate drift on another.
