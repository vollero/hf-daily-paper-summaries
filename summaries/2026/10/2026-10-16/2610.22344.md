# MLExpert: Towards a Generalist Agentic Model for Autonomous Machine Learning Engineering

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.22344) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22344)


## One-sentence takeaway
MLExpert combines outcome-filtered supervised training with execution-window reinforcement learning to raise its base model’s MLE-Bench Lite medal rate from 37.88% to 72.73%, with additional gains on scientific and program-optimization tasks (Table 9; §§5.2–5.5).

## Problem and motivation
Autonomous machine learning engineering requires sustained experimentation: agents must build pipelines, interpret execution feedback, revise solutions, and select submissions within a time budget. The authors identify a training gap between this process and methods that train isolated decisions or operate inside externally controlled workflows. Supervision can contain deteriorating experiment sequences, while costly experiments provide delayed feedback that must be used before a complete run finishes (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Executable tasks and interaction environment:** Kaggle-MLE Factory converts historical competitions into standardized tasks with hidden test labels; 154 tasks survive manual review. The harness maintains an experiment tree and lets the model choose revisions, execution, backtracking, and submissions (§3; Appendix A).
- **Hierarchical agentic SFT:** General coding/tool-use training precedes MLE and scientific demonstrations. Outcome-based filtering discards weak trajectories and truncates substantially degraded tails while retaining earlier exploration (§4.1; Appendix C.1).
- **Windowed Interaction RL (WIRL):** Decisions between successive experiment results share a reward combining execution validity, medal tier, and local progress. Asynchronous collection enables updates during continuing trajectories; token-level correction handles rollout-policy mismatch (§4.2).
- **MLExpert model:** These components train a 35B MoE model initialized from Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. The paper evaluates both MLE performance and transfer to scientific experimentation, agent scaffolds, and algorithm components (§§5.1–5.5).

These are the authors’ proposed contributions; the supplied evidence does not independently establish priority over all related methods.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Substantial MLE improvement | On 22-task MLE-Bench Lite under the MLExpert Harness, Any Medal rises from **37.88% ± 5.46 SEM** to **72.73% ± 2.62**, over three evaluations. Budget: 12 hours and up to 3,000 steps per task, with one H200. | §5.1; Table 9 |
| Frontier-comparable medal rate | MLExpert’s 72.73% mean matches single-evaluation rates for GLM-5.3 and Kimi-K3; GPT-5.6 Sol reaches 77.27%. | Table 9 |
| Improvement transfers across harnesses | Under Claude Code, Any Medal is **69.70% ± 1.52 SEM** for MLExpert versus **42.42% ± 4.01** for Base, each over three evaluations of MLE-Bench Lite. | Appendix D.1 |
| Both SFT and RL contribute | Successive MLE-Bench Lite Any Medal rates are **37.9 → 47.0 → 62.1 → 72.7%** for Base, general SFT, MLE SFT, and WIRL. | Table 3; Appendix D.1 |
| WIRL outperforms tested RL alternatives | From the same MLE-SFT initialization, Any Medal is **72.73% ± 2.62 SEM**, versus GRPO **56.06% ± 5.46**, PPO-style **60.61% ± 4.01**, no trend reward **66.67% ± 3.03**, and no policy correction **65.15% ± 5.46**; three evaluations each. | §5.3; Table 10 |
| Lower learner waiting overhead | Under the same training setup, mean per-update waiting fraction is **39.3%** for WIRL versus **87.6%** for GRPO. This measures waiting relative to waiting plus training time. | Appendix D.1; Figure 5 |
| Scientific-task gains | On ten NatureBench Lite tasks with Claude Code and a four-hour task budget, MLExpert matches SOTA on **7/10** and surpasses it on **5/10**, versus Base’s **4/10** and **0/10**. Surpassing requires normalized relative gap **>0.1**; MLExpert has one evaluation. | Table 2; Appendix D.2 |
| Scaffold transfer includes increased solver use | Frozen GPT-4.1-nano scaffolds achieve **43.7%** held-out AIME accuracy for MLExpert versus **23.3%** for Base, using **8.40 versus 5.74** calls per problem. Results average three held-out years, each evaluated three times. | Table 11; Appendix D.3 |
| Algorithm-component gains vary by task | With equal five-hour search budgets on 12 selected MLS-Bench-Lite tasks, mean hidden-verifier reward rises from **0.321543 to 0.425198**; MLExpert wins ten tasks, ties one, and loses one. | Table 12; Appendix D.4 |

The results support improved optimization performance over the base model in the evaluated settings. Ablations support the usefulness of WIRL’s trend reward and policy correction. They do not establish universal superiority, statistically equivalent frontier performance, or recursive self-improvement. Reduced learner waiting also does not establish lower total training cost.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-reported task dependence:** Both models score zero on scene densification, and MLExpert performs worse on time-series forecasting (Appendix D.4).
- **Analyst inference—limited precision:** MLE comparisons mix three-run averages with single frontier-model evaluations. NatureBench reports one MLExpert evaluation on a small subset; external reference results retain their original conditions (Appendices D.1–D.2).
- **Analyst inference—scaffold uncertainty:** Each optimizer produces one frozen scaffold. Repeated solver evaluations measure its performance, but not variation across independent scaffold searches. Higher MLExpert accuracy also accompanies more solver calls (Appendix D.3).
- **Analyst inference—unisolated components:** No reported ablation isolates outcome-guided SFT filtering, scientific-data inclusion, or window boundaries independently of other RL changes (§5.3).
- **Reproducibility gaps:** Hyperparameters and SFT corpus statistics are provided, but the supplied text does not specify the RL task pool, total RL updates/windows, total compute, or general-SFT corpus size (Appendix C).

## Open research questions
- **Does windowed credit assignment itself drive the RL gain?** Current comparisons change several training properties together. A next experiment could hold reward, collection, correction, and compute fixed while varying only window versus terminal credit assignment.
- **How much does outcome-filtered SFT help?** Its contribution is not isolated. Compare unfiltered, quality-filtered, and tail-truncated demonstrations with matched training tokens.
- **How reliable is cross-task transfer?** NatureBench has one reported MLExpert run, and scaffold search produces one candidate per optimizer. Repeat independent searches and evaluations across broader task selections.
- **Does WIRL improve efficiency at equal cost?** Waiting overhead falls, but total compute is unavailable and scaffold calls increase. Compare performance under matched GPU-hours, tokens, and solver-call budgets.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the reported benchmark gains and method description, but broader generalization remains uncertain.

Material missing information includes total training cost, RL training volume and task composition, independent-search variability, controlled component ablations, and verified contents of the linked code/model releases.
