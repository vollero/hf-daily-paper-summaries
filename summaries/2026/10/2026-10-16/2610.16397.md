# Recursive Environment Scaling for General Long-Horizon Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.16397) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16397)


## One-sentence takeaway
RES recursively adds validated access and execution dependencies to existing agent environments, reducing success under fixed evaluation budgets while producing trajectories that improve supervised fine-tuning (§§4–5).

## Problem and motivation
Agent difficulty depends on information access, resource relationships, and action prerequisites as well as task objectives. The authors identify a gap between expanding tasks or constructing new environments and systematically making existing environments harder while preserving their objectives and completion requirements (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Environment scaling:** Transform information access, resource relationships, and operational prerequisites while preserving underlying facts and required outputs; instructions change to explain revised access procedures (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Validated recursive construction:** A proposer composes transformations; independent replay and public-interface execution check consistency and recoverability. Accepted children become parents for subsequent rounds, with repairs for failed candidates (§§3.3–3.4).
- **Evaluation and training evidence:** Paired original/scaled environments measure difficulty, while collected trajectories support matched-count SFT comparisons. Ablations examine dependency composition, inheritance, depth, and operator breadth (§§4–5).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Scaled environments reduce success across models | All 18 comparisons across six models and three benchmarks decline; 294 paired tasks, one 50-turn attempt per condition. DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash Claw Pass@1 falls from 82.86% to 5.71%, using native score ≥0.80. | §§4.1–4.2; Table 1 caption |
| RES trajectories improve SFT | All nine backbone–benchmark comparisons improve. For Qwen3.5-35B-A3B-Base, gains over original-trajectory SFT are 12.38 points in Claw Pass@1, 14.00 in Workspace TCR@80, and 14.61 in Terminal Pass@1. Each condition uses 8,428 trajectories and identical optimization; evaluation uses original environments with one 50-turn attempt. | §4.3; Table 2 caption; Appendix B |
| Dependencies account for substantial difficulty | On 15 Claw tasks, removing composed dependencies and prerequisite work raises DeepSeek Pass@1 from 0.00% to 100.00% and mean native score from 7.73 to 92.93/100; one 50-turn attempt, success threshold 0.80. | §5.1; Table 3A |
| Inheritance and library breadth matter | On the same 15-task rebuilt comparison, full RES yields 6.67% Pass@1 versus 93.33% without inheritance and 80.00% with the Basic library; matched planned depths and nominal proposer budgets, one 50-turn DeepSeek attempt. | Table 3B; Appendix D.1 |
| Additional depth increases difficulty, but not monotonically | Across 105 retained Claw lineages, DeepSeek Pass@1 is 82.86% at depth zero, 60.00% at cap one, 3.81% at cap five, and 4.76% at cap ten; one 50-turn attempt, threshold 0.80. | Table 3C; Appendix C.3 |
| Historical interaction demand rises | Across 105 Claw seeds, historical means from budget zero to ten increase from 3.55 to 43.22 turns and 8.05 to 206.02 tool calls, including failed attempts and retained early-stopped states. | §5.2; Figure 4; Appendix C.1 |
| Sampled validity review finds no listed issues | Three researchers reviewed 20 original/scaled pairs per benchmark, checking objective preservation, information availability, scoring consistency, leakage, and bypasses; none was found in that sample. | Appendix A |

The evidence supports increased difficulty under the specified execution budgets and improved original-benchmark performance after RES-based SFT. It does not establish that scaled tasks require better planning independently of additional access work, or that training gains persist under equal token budgets. Historical averages include earlier environments and must be distinguished from endpoint results; Appendix C explicitly separates these statistics.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Disclosed scope:** Component ablations use a selected subset of Claw tasks and one evaluator. Dependency removal also removes prerequisite work, so it does not isolate dependency structure from workload (Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference—budget dependence:** Frequent turn-limit failures make results sensitive to execution budgets. Larger-budget evaluation is needed to distinguish eventual solvability from practical completion difficulty (§§5.2, 5.4).
- **Analyst inference—training control:** Equal trajectory counts do not establish equal supervised-token or compute budgets. The supplied text reports original-data token totals but omits corresponding RES totals (Appendix B).
- **Disclosed measurement limits:** Construction costs exclude unsuccessful branches, target solving, grading, and orchestration. Matched proposer opportunities also do not imply matched token expenditure (§5.3; Appendices A, D.1).
- **Analyst inference—uncertainty and validity:** Independent comparisons use single attempts; confidence intervals are not supplied. Manual validation covers a sample, and training-overlap checks address recorded representations rather than proving all semantic overlap absent (Appendices A–B).

## Open research questions
- **Does RES expose planning failures beyond budget exhaustion?** Many rollouts reach the turn limit. Reevaluate frozen pairs at increasing turn and time budgets, measuring completion and where failures occur.
- **Do SFT gains survive token-matched training?** Trajectory counts are controlled, but RES token totals are unavailable. Compare conditions with equal supervised tokens and training compute.
- **How transferable is construction across proposer and stopping models?** The reported construction uses a fixed proposer and scaling-stage target (§4.1). Cross these choices and evaluate resulting environments with held-out models.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text provides substantial protocols, appendices, and explicit findings, but omits the numerical bodies of Tables 1–2 and most appendix tables. Material gaps include complete per-model results, RES training token and length distributions, detailed optimization-table values, statistical uncertainty, full construction costs, and confirmed release contents beyond the linked repository.
