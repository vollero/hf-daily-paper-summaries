# EVA-Cap: Optimizing Audiovisual Video Captioning via Event-Centric Alignment

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.22186) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22186)


## One-sentence takeaway
EVA-Cap combines event-verified training captions with progressive event-level reinforcement learning to improve audiovisual synchronization and speaker attribution over tested open-source captioners, while retaining hallucination and complex-scene failures (Tables 1–4; §A.5).

## Problem and motivation
Audiovisual captions must describe what happens, identify who acts or speaks, and preserve temporal relationships between sound and imagery. The authors argue that holistic caption supervision overlooks these relationships, while dialogue-only or attribute-specific supervision covers too little of the problem (§§1–2).

Their identified gap is both representational and practical: caption training lacks explicit event structure and sufficiently reliable event-level supervision (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **EVA-Data:** An automatic pipeline initializes captions with AVoCaDO, refines them with Gemini-2.5-Pro, and checks timestamped event coverage and alignment with GPT-4.1. Filtering produces 63K audiovisual video–caption pairs (§3.1).
- **Event-centric rewards:** Captions are represented through modality, event type, character, and content. Separate rewards measure reference-event recall, correct character attribution, and audio-to-visual synchronization through retrieval over candidate visual events (§3.2.2).
- **Progressive post-training:** Qwen2.5-Omni-7B receives SFT, followed by GRPO that first optimizes completeness and synchronization, then adds attribution. Appendix experiments also apply the reward framework to ASID-Captioner and ASID-1M (§3.2; §§A.2–A.3).

The contribution is this particular data-and-reward framework; the authors acknowledge closely related event-binding approaches and leave direct comparisons of alternative binding designs open (§A.4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Better direct caption scores, with a hallucination trade-off | On video-SALMONN-2, EVA-Cap has 34.6% total error versus DiaDem’s 36.5%, but 17.0% hallucination versus 15.5%. UGC-VideoCap average scores are 76.9 versus 75.6; judges are GPT-4.1 and GPT-4o, respectively. | Table 1 |
| Stronger caption-conditioned QA among listed open-source models | With Gemini-2.5-Pro answering from captions, Daily-Omni accuracy is 56.1% versus TimeChat-Captioner’s 52.8%; WorldSense is 29.0% versus AVoCaDO’s 25.7%. Both differences are 3.3 percentage points. | Table 2; §4.1 |
| Improved event alignment over DiaDem | On 1,066 ChronusAV videos under 200 seconds, V2A/A2V accuracy is 55.4%/56.2% versus 45.9%/47.7%. On 422 AV-SpeakerBench recognition questions, accuracy is 40.1% versus 32.7%. Evaluation is caption-conditioned QA, with additional Seed-1.6 scoring for ChronusAV. | Table 3; §4.1 |
| SFT and subsequent GRPO each contribute | V2A/A2V/speaker accuracy progresses from 8.4%/11.9%/3.1% for the backbone to 44.8%/46.1%/31.8% after SFT, then 55.4%/56.2%/40.1% for final EVA-Cap. | Table 4 |
| Attribution introduces a measurable trade-off | Relative to continuing completeness-plus-synchronization training, adding attribution in GRPO round two raises speaker accuracy from 37.0% to 40.1% and A2V from 55.5% to 56.2%, while V2A falls from 56.5% to 55.4%. | Table 4 |
| Data and rewards have supporting separate tests | Under the appendix protocol using Qwen3.5-27B evaluation and reduced GRPO data, EVA-Data SFT reaches 35.6% A2V versus 30.7% for same-size ASID-1M SFT; ASID-Captioner improves from 30.4% to 39.0% A2V after the proposed GRPO stages. | Tables 5–6; §A.3 |
| Long-output hallucinations remain problematic | In the longest video-SALMONN-2 output-length partition, EVA-Cap’s hallucination rate is 17.0% versus DiaDem’s 14.9%; EVA-Cap performs better in the shorter two partitions. | Table 8; §A.3.3 |

These results support improved performance under the reported evaluation pipelines and provide ablation evidence for both data and rewards. They do not establish universal superiority: Gemini-2.5-Pro remains ahead on several reported benchmarks, and hallucination does not consistently improve. Appendix event scores use a different evaluator and should not be directly compared with main-table scores (§A.3).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated failures:** Rapid scene changes still cause omissions and audiovisual misalignment; off-screen speech in multi-character scenes causes incorrect speaker binding (§A.5; Fig. 6).
- **Author-stated annotation risk:** Proprietary teachers can introduce biases and residual errors despite automatic verification (§A.5).
- **Author-stated unresolved comparison:** Decoupled audio/visual rewards are not experimentally compared with unified audiovisual-event binding used by related work (§A.4).
- **Analyst inference—evaluation dependence:** Model-mediated annotation, rewards, QA, and scoring leave uncertainty about agreement with human judgments. The reward-judge checks provide partial evidence, but model agreement is not human validation (§§4.1, A.3.2).
- **Analyst inference—scope:** Duration restrictions and filtering for synchronization-rich, discriminative GRPO samples limit evidence for longer videos or sparse-event settings (§§3.1.2, A.2).
- **Analyst inference—faithfulness coverage:** Recall and reference-character matching do not directly penalize every unsupported generated event. The precision-reward experiment exposes a completeness–hallucination trade-off rather than resolving it (§3.2.2; §A.3.4).

## Open research questions
1. **Do gains persist under human evaluation?** Current headline results rely heavily on model-mediated assessment. A blinded human study could separately score coverage, attribution, synchronization, and unsupported details (§4.1).
2. **Which event-binding formulation works best?** The paper leaves decoupled versus unified audiovisual rewards untested. Compare them with matched data, backbone, training budget, and judges (§A.4).
3. **Can grounding reduce complex-scene failures?** Rapid cuts and off-screen speakers remain failure modes. Test instance- and timestamp-grounded supervision on targeted scene-transition and speaker-attribution subsets (§A.5).
4. **Can faithfulness improve without sacrificing coverage?** Precision rewards shorten captions but increase omissions. Sweep reward weights under controlled output lengths and measure both event precision and recall (§§A.3.3–A.3.4).
