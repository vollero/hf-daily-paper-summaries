# A team of Gemini 4 Argon agents beats Minecraft

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.22319) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22319)


## One-sentence takeaway
Four Gemini 4 Argon coding agents autonomously defeated Minecraft’s Ender Dragon in one random-world, Easy-difficulty run lasting 27 hours 14 minutes, demonstrating sustained coordination through a privileged code interface without establishing reliability across runs (§§2, 3, 8).

## Problem and motivation
The paper tests whether general-purpose coding agents can sustain a shared goal across thousands of dependent actions, failures, and context compactions. The authors contrast their setup with Minecraft systems using trained gameplay policies or game-specific planners, curricula, and skill libraries (§§1, 7). They motivate Minecraft as a controlled setting for studying coordination and recovery relevant to long-running software and enterprise work; transfer to those applications is not evaluated.

## Approach and contributions
- **Autonomous completion demonstration:** Four agents independently wrote and debugged JavaScript programs using standard Mineflayer and its pathfinding plugin. They received a goal and general working instructions, without supplied game strategies, assigned roles, or additional game-specific helpers (§§2, 7).
- **Observed team practices:** Chat and shared memory supported specialization, reusable helper functions, knowledge sharing, item recovery, and correction of erroneous reports. These are documented behaviors, rather than experimentally isolated mechanisms (§4).
- **Recorded verification:** Programs, outputs, chat, and server events were recorded; a non-playing Observer monitored the session, and the server log recorded the winning advancement (§§2.3, 5). Public release of these records is not established.

The contribution is principally an empirical demonstration and case study; the paper does not introduce a new training algorithm. Its priority claim is qualified by its specific tool and setup restrictions, while §7 acknowledges other reported dragon kills.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Verified completion | Four agents in a random Easy survival world won after 27 h 14 min and 2,783 programs; server log recorded “Free the End.” | §§2, 3, 5 |
| Substantial failure burden | Before victory: 605 deaths; 22% of programs ended in an error; the End fight took nearly 14 hours. | §8 |
| Coordination and tool reuse | 229 pre-win messages; a crafting fix appeared in 655 programs across all four agents; 42 persistent helper functions were retained. | §§3–4 |
| Quantitative reasoning example | Two Eye-of-Ender trajectories sampled every 50 ms produced an estimate 43 m from the actual portal room. | §3.3, Fig. 7 |
| Large inference expenditure | Players processed approximately 1.35 billion input tokens, 91% cached, and generated approximately 16 million output tokens over roughly 24,000 calls before victory; estimated API cost was $520 introductory or $1,040 standard. | §6 |
| Infrastructure interruptions | Six server restarts paused the world for approximately 20 minutes, included in completion time. | §3 |

The evidence supports successful completion and sustained collaborative activity in this particular configuration. It does not establish a success rate, a benefit from multiple agents versus one agent, or causal benefits from memory and communication. Human completion times and community systems discussed in §§3 and 7 use different settings and are contextual comparisons rather than controlled baselines.

## Limitations and validity risks
**Author-stated:** Mineflayer exposes exact surrounding blocks, including those behind rock, and internal dragon state; its plugin handles pathfinding. Easy difficulty reduces survival demands. The model already knows Minecraft and Mineflayer. Frequent deaths, program errors, server interruptions, and evaluation on only one run limit the result (§8).

**Analyst inference:** Without matched ablations, the contribution of team size, shared memory, chat, and pathfinding remains unresolved. Recorded logs strengthen traceability, but the supplied text does not establish their public availability or provide complete runnable configuration. Claims about real-world task relevance remain motivational rather than demonstrated.

## Open research questions
- **How reliable is completion?** One run cannot establish reliability (§8). Repeat across independently generated seeds under fixed time and token budgets, reporting success rates and completion-time distributions.
- **What does collaboration contribute?** Teamwork is observed without a single-agent control (§4). Compare one versus four agents under matched aggregate inference budgets, then ablate chat and shared memory.
- **How much does privileged access matter?** Structured state and pathfinding are acknowledged advantages (§8). Compare full access with restricted visible-state access and a pixel-based control condition.
- **Can failure and cost be reduced?** The successful run still incurred substantial errors and resource use (§§6, 8). Compare the current loop with stronger action verification and reusable-tool validation across matched seeds.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in summarizing the reported run; **low** in generalizing its reliability or attributing success to particular components.

Missing material information includes repeated-run outcomes, controlled baselines and ablations, complete agent prompts and inference settings, exact seed and full execution configuration, and confirmed public access to programs, logs, and world saves.
