# JevAgentHarness: Benchmarking, Deploying and Training Decision Models That Share Control with LLMs

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.21412) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21412)


## One-sentence takeaway
Decision models trained on agent states can substantially reduce agent costs, but isolated accuracy and confidence thresholds do not reliably predict success under distribution shift (§§7.1–7.5; App. G.9).

## Problem and motivation
Agent harnesses repeatedly call expensive LLMs even when the immediate output is a choice among listed tools or actions. The authors ask which choices smaller decision models can take over while preserving task success.

Their identified gap is that isolated question benchmarks miss consequential errors: a wrong action changes later states, and an irreversible mistake can fail an entire task despite high average decision accuracy (§§1, 3).

## Approach and contributions
- **AgentDecisionBench:** evaluates isolated questions, recorded agent states, and live task control. Its new DecisionGym environment supplies acceptable next actions at reachable solvable states, including after detours (§3).
- **JevAgentHarness:** expresses choices as typed questions and delegates answers above thresholds fitted separately for each decision point on shadow-mode agent trajectories; otherwise the LLM decides (§§2.1, 4).
- **AgentDecision-4B:** post-trains Qwen3-4B using LoRA on visited states, including scripted mistakes, with answer-key labels and Jev probabilities. SFT uses cross-entropy and a calibration term; subsequent in-loop GRPO adds no measurable gain in the reported runs (§§5, 7.4).
- **Deployment study:** tests individual delegations before combining passing configurations on fresh tasks, with registered non-inferiority tests and multiple-testing correction (§6).

The authors promise release of models, data, recipes, benchmark, and harness; the supplied text does not establish release completion.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Isolated rankings can reverse in live control | On 1,114 BFCL tool questions, StartLux-9B scored 92.2% versus AgentDecision-4B’s 89.3%; on 200 shifted DecisionGym tasks with unrestricted tool delegation, success was 69.5% versus 91.5%; Jev achieved 82.0%. | §7.1; Fig. 4; App. G.5 |
| Thresholding matters, but delegation depends on the agent | With Flash on 500 Stage-1 DecisionGym tasks, the reported cascade saved 14.2% without changing task outcomes; unrestricted Jev tool selection lost 19.0 percentage points. Flash-Lite’s fitted tool threshold delegated nothing. | §7.2 |
| Trained model passed the fresh-task combined test | On 500 fresh DecisionGym tasks, AgentDecision-4B **v1** achieved 100.0% success versus Flash’s 99.4%, saving 81.1% of cost. The success difference was +0.6 points, 95% CI [0.0, 1.4]. | Tables 27, 29 |
| Combining delegations with Jev failed non-inferiority | On those 500 tasks, success fell from 99.4% to 97.2%; difference −2.2 points, 95% CI [−3.8, −0.8], despite 40.7% cost savings. | Tables 27, 29 |
| Speed gains require suitable serving | Dedicated-GPU DecisionGym deployment was reported as 86.3% cheaper and 2.5× faster, with less than a three-point success loss. The registered v1 deployment instead took 10.21 seconds per task versus 6.83 seconds for Flash. | §7.3; Table 29 |
| Household-game deployment also benefited | On 140 ALFWorld games, a separately trained 4B action cascade achieved 95.0% success versus 90.7%, saved 89.0% of cost, and reduced mean time from 37.0 to 18.2 seconds. | Table 1 |
| Additional training did not uniformly help | Public tool-use training raised isolated accuracy from 78.8% to 85.1%, while unrestricted shifted-task success fell from 96.0% to 91.5%. GRPO added no measurable gain over SFT. | §7.4 |
| New rules can cause severe failure | On hard tasks with ten customers and an unseen rule, AgentDecision-4B achieved 3.0% task success versus the LLM’s 88.0%. Under shifted tasks with in-domain thresholds, none of the three initially tested models established non-inferiority. | §7.5; App. G.9 |

These author-reported results support substantial savings in fitted environments, rather than universal transfer or equal success. Registered results and later exploratory experiments have different evidential status (§6; Table 1). The frozen **v1** checkpoint and the later public-data-trained model also have different results and should not be conflated.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** English tasks, mostly Gemini agents, benign observations, and agent-specific calibration/training. Adversarial observations and policy injection were not tested.
- **Transfer failures:** unseen rules and shifted task structures can defeat confident delegation; thresholds require refitting (§7.5; App. G.9).
- **Comparison gaps:** no same-task comparison with learned routers such as RouteLLM; GRPO showed no measurable benefit (Limitations).
- **Statistical boundaries:** non-inferiority permits a prespecified loss and does not establish equivalence. Most experiments use one temperature-zero run per task and arm (Limitations; Compute, software and runs).
- **Analyst inference:** near-ceiling DecisionGym results provide limited evidence about harder workflows; its scripted plans and strict failure rules also constrain generalization (§7; App. B.1).
- **Theory boundaries:** the optimality proposition assumes additive error losses and a common error cost; its simplified threshold additionally assumes calibration and confidence-independent LLM accuracy (App. A).
- **Coverage limitation:** the source is truncated, omitting substantial appendix material and leaving several tables available only as captions.

## Open research questions
- **Can deferral remain reliable under shift?** In-domain thresholds failed to establish non-inferiority on shifted tasks. Test drift detection and sequential recalibration against fixed thresholds on changing rules and tool names (App. G.9).
- **How much does post-error training contribute independently?** The recipe includes error-following states, but the supplied text does not establish their isolated causal contribution. Compare matched SFT datasets with and without those states (§5).
- **Does this outperform learned routing on the same workload?** That comparison is absent. Evaluate both approaches on identical tasks with matched success margins and full serving costs (Limitations).
- **When does in-loop RL add value beyond SFT?** GRPO produced no measurable improvement here. Test matched training budgets on longer tasks with more recoverable errors (§7.4).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the central findings and important counterexamples, but coverage is incomplete.

Missing information includes omitted appendix methods and ablations, full contents of several result tables, detailed uncertainty for some headline deployment gains, and confirmation that promised artifacts were released.
