# One Bias Score, Two Factors: How Mitigations Affect Option Bias in Multiple-Choice Evaluation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.20993) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20993)


## One-sentence takeaway
Under balanced option orderings, pooled selection bias exactly equals flip rate times positional skew, revealing that prior correction and reversal aggregation improve different behaviors while aggregation can also stabilize wrong answers (§§3, 6–7).

## Problem and motivation
Reordering multiple-choice options can change language-model answers and model rankings. Pooled bias scores measure uneven letter selection, while consistency metrics count answer changes; either alone can hide what a mitigation actually changes. The authors identify the missing link as an exact decomposition connecting these measurements under balanced orderings (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Exact decomposition:** Define flips by changes in chosen **content**, rather than answer letter. When every content visits every position equally often, pooled selection-rate SD equals **flip rate × positional skew among flipping items**. Both factors require no gold labels. Balanced RStd has a related decomposition whose second factor requires labels (§§3.1–3.3).
- **Generalization beyond balanced designs:** Derive a label-free correction for stable items’ positional imbalance, restoring the identity for arbitrary ordering designs (Appendix B.1).
- **Mitigation analysis:** Compare existing prior corrections, reversal probability products, and their compositions on stored probabilities. The contribution is explaining their distinct effects, including cancellation of particular letter-preference patterns, rather than introducing those mitigation methods (§§4, 6; Appendix D).
- **Empirical characterization:** Study 41 open-weight checkpoints from seven families across 12 benchmarks, linking flips to accuracy and output uncertainty, and skew to estimated option-symbol priors (§§4–5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Pooled bias separates exactly into two factors | Under position-balanced orderings, pooled SD = FL × std(g), where g is the chosen-position distribution among flipping items; this is an arithmetic identity. | §3.2, Eq. 6; Appendix B.1 |
| Flip rate tracks accuracy much more closely than skew | Across first-token checkpoint–benchmark cells, Spearman correlations with cyclic-order-averaged accuracy are −0.947 for flip rate and −0.333 for skew. | Appendix C.2 |
| Output uncertainty distinguishes flipping items | A smaller canonical top-two option-probability gap identifies flipping versus stable items with median AUC 0.85 across eligible cells; controls retain an association among always-wrong items. | §5.1; Appendix C.1 |
| Flipping answers align with estimated letter priors | On four-option cells, median centered cosine is 0.87 for the held-out estimated prior versus −0.10 for its letter-permuted control. | §5.2 |
| Similar pooled gains conceal different changes | PriDe and reversal product both reduce balanced RStd from 0.064 to 0.036 in the mitigation comparison, but reversal product reduces flips much more. Comparisons use shared held-out items in cells with at least three options. | §6; §7, Table 2 caption |
| Composition improves both factors | Correction followed by reversal product retains the flip reduction, further reduces skew, and narrows placement range most; rank reversals remain at the product’s level. Numerical table entries are absent from the supplied extraction. | §7; Table 2 caption |
| Aggregation can stabilize mistakes | Reversal product increases stable-wrong share more than accuracy; about two in five resolved flips end on a wrong option in the eligible mitigation cohort. | §7; Appendix D.4, Table 14 caption |
| Cyclic sweeps miss additional order sensitivity | In the four-option all-ordering cohort, cyclic flip rate understates the full-ordering rate by 0.095 on average, while ranking cells nearly identically. | Appendix E.1 |

The mathematics establishes a measurement decomposition, not a causal account of model behavior. Experiments support distinct effects of the tested mitigation operations, but neither a lower flip rate nor a lower skew guarantees correctness or ranking stability. The supplied text includes many table captions without their numerical bodies, limiting independent assessment of effect sizes and uncertainty intervals.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope:** Mostly English multiple-choice benchmarks, deterministic readouts, and specific mitigation configurations; internal causes of flipping and letter preferences remain unresolved (Limitations).
- **Ordering coverage:** Stability on cyclic rotations does not imply stability across all permutations; the appendix directly measures missed flips (Appendix E).
- **Causal ambiguity:** Probability-margin associations and output-logit interventions do not establish how internal uncertainty or positional preferences arise (§5.1; Appendix D.3).
- **Residual correction errors:** The study does not separate prior-estimation error, item-specific preferences, and violations of PriDe’s probability model as explanations for remaining flips (§6).
- **Statistical scope:** Main checkpoint-block intervals hold retained items fixed and do not account for item sampling; mitigation contrasts are not adjusted for multiple comparisons (Appendix A.5).
- **Analyst inference:** Treating flips as a pure order effect is unsafe for nondeterministic systems without repeated-prompt controls. The closed-model check explicitly finds substantial repeat disagreement (Appendix C.5).

## Open research questions
- **What internally causes flips?** Output uncertainty correlates with flipping, but causality remains open. A next experiment could intervene on internal representations while tracking probability margins, flip rate, skew, and accuracy (Limitations; §5.1).
- **Why does prior correction leave flips unresolved?** Several explanations remain conflated. Vary calibration size and compare shared versus item-specific prior estimates under controlled ordering designs (§6).
- **Which aggregation pairings improve correctness as well as stability?** Reversal cancels only particular preference components and can stabilize distractors. Compare alternative pairings at equal query budgets, measuring stable-correct and stable-wrong transitions (Appendices D.2–D.4).
- **How should order sensitivity be measured under sampling?** Closed-model flips mix reorder effects with repeat variability. Use repeated samples for each ordering to estimate these sources separately (Appendix C.5).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the decomposition and qualitative findings; **medium** in quantitative mitigation comparisons.
