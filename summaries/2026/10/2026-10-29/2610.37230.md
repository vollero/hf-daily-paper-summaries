# TAO: Tool-Aware Joint Scheduling for LLM-Based Agentic Serving

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-29.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.37230) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37230)


## One-sentence takeaway
TAO combines decaying cache credit, predicted tool readiness, and joint GPU admission and placement to improve agent-serving responsiveness, with substantial gains in several evaluated settings but workload-dependent regressions.

## Problem and motivation
Agent sessions alternate between GPU inference and external CPU tools, leaving opportunities to release GPU capacity while tools execute. Returning sessions may reuse cached context, making both tool timing and node choice consequential.

The authors identify two gaps: existing schedulers inadequately represent near-term tool returns, and per-session admission and routing heuristics miss better combinations of sessions competing for shared capacity (§§1–2). TAO targets “Clustered GPUs with Distributed CPUs,” where external executors manage tool resources independently of GPU admission.

## Approach and contributions
- **Tool-aware evaluation:** Each session–node assignment receives a lexicographic triplet: decayed node-specific cache credit, binary tool-return readiness, and token footprint. Cache credit takes strict precedence; readiness distinguishes plans tied on cache credit (§§3.2–3.4).
- **Joint admission and placement:** A capacity-state dynamic program selects waiting sessions and assigns nodes together. It is exact for the frozen, discretized problem; exhausted searches fall back to best-fit-decreasing (§3.5; Appendix B).
- **Asynchronous execution with revalidation:** A worker computes plans from snapshots; live-state checks discard changed plans or apply a feasible prefix. A conditional bound characterizes loss relative to a fresh-snapshot objective optimum, without guaranteeing end-to-end latency (§3.5; Appendices B–C).

The contribution is the integration of these evaluation signals with joint scheduling and live execution. Dynamic programming and multiple-knapsack optimization are established machinery, as the authors acknowledge (§5; Appendix J).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|

| Larger-model showcase reports strong gains | GLM-4.7 SWE-verified, one eight-H100 node, 96–160 workers: latency reductions of 16.2–18.9%, TTFT reductions of 15.7–38.4%, and decode increases of 22.8–24.7% versus ThunderAgent. Task-count reporting is inconsistent. | Table 2; §4.2 |
| Readiness helps in a matched component comparison | DeepSeek SWE-Lite at 32 workers: enabling readiness increases decode by 4.03% and reduces tool-end-to-first-token delay by 10.88%; duration-prediction MAE is 0.410 s over 10,967 completed tools. | §4.3; Figure 6 |
| Joint placement contributes in the component study | DeepSeek SWE-Lite, two four-H100 nodes, 32 workers: replacing DP with BFD lowers decode from 1,417.97 to 1,233.72 tok/s and raises mean TTFT from 0.154 to 0.195 s. | Table 4 |
| Mixed-workload overload exposes a substantial regression | AutomationBench at 128 workers on DeepSeek: TAO versus ThunderAgent has 563.114 versus 523.676 s latency and 1,013.157 versus 1,140.862 tok/s decode; TTFT is 19.123 s versus Dynamo’s 3.166 s. | Table 3 |
| Reported planner work is small but incomplete | Qwen at 160 workers: completed solves average 1.59 ms; preparation plus completed solving totals 0.11236% of runtime. BFD fallback occurs in 8.98% of submissions, with exhausted searches and fallback execution untimed. | Appendix I; Table 9 |

These results support improvements across several tested deployments and component-level benefits from readiness and joint placement. They do not establish universal dominance or that maximizing TAO’s surrogate objective minimizes task latency.

There are reporting discrepancies: §4.2 claims Qwen SWE-Lite decode leadership at every worker count, whereas Table 1 shows ThunderAgent ahead at 96 workers. Table 2 states 300 tasks per run, while the accompanying SWE-verified discussion states 500 tasks.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-qualified optimality:** Exactness applies to frozen, discretized scheduling instances. Quantization can exclude feasible assignments; fallback, stale state, and partial application introduce additional losses (§3.5; Appendices B–C).
- **Author-qualified theory:** The asynchronous bound compares scheduling objectives on the same realized state. It does not bound task latency or regret against a globally optimal online policy (Appendix C).
- **Author-qualified measurement:** Scheduler overhead excludes exhausted searches and BFD execution. The duration estimator is a heuristic without calibrated individual-call coverage (Appendices A, I).
- **Analyst inference—limited isolation:** The longer-delay study changes readiness, cache decay, and shared-token refresh together against its historical reference. Its runtime gains cannot be attributed solely to readiness; each current cell is a single exploratory run with live generation (Appendix G).
- **Analyst inference—generalization remains uncertain:** Evaluations concentrate on coding/terminal workloads and H100 deployments with at most four replicas. Main performance tables provide no repeated-run uncertainty estimates. Natural-live trajectories can differ across runs (Tables 1–3, 9; Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference—objective sensitivity:** Fixed cache-first precedence can suppress readiness unless aggregate cache values tie. The supplied text does not establish whether this ordering, the cache half-life, or the readiness horizon is robust across workloads (§§3.2–3.4).

## Open research questions
1. **When should readiness outrank cache reuse?** The fixed precedence lacks a reported ordering sensitivity study. Compare alternative orderings and constrained objectives on matched traces, measuring post-tool delay, task latency, and starvation.
2. **How does joint planning scale beyond four replicas?** Current overhead evidence covers small replica counts and omits fallback computation. Sweep replica count and waiting-pool size while timing all search and fallback work (Appendix I).
3. **What causes high-concurrency AutomationBench regressions?** Table 3 establishes the regression without isolating its cause. Repeat that setting with matched workloads and readiness, cache, and planner ablations.
4. **How robust is prediction under changing tool behavior?** Historical prediction works in the reported studies, but individual-call coverage is uncalibrated. Introduce controlled duration shifts and cold-start tools, comparing historical, hint-only, and oracle timing (Appendices A, G).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text provides detailed algorithms, comparisons, and caveats, but contains inconsistent result descriptions.

Material missing information includes code/artifact release availability, repeated-run variability for main results, task-success or output-quality comparisons, sensitivity to key scheduling parameters, complete planner-cost accounting, and consistent GLM SWE-verified task counts.
