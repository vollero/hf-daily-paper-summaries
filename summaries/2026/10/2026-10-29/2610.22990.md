# PersonaCUABench: Benchmarking Computer-Use Agents Serving Diverse Personas

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-29.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.22990) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22990)


## One-sentence takeaway

PersonaCUABench finds that persona-conditioned requests reduce original-specification task completion across nine browser-agent backbones, while showing that this metric can penalize agents for respecting user constraints (§§4.2, 5.2).

## Problem and motivation

Fixed-instruction benchmarks measure whether agents complete predefined tasks, but users may omit details, introduce constraints, or require approval before acting. The authors identify a gap between completing the original specification and serving the person making the request. Their evaluation varies the simulated requester while retaining the same underlying tasks, measuring both completion and satisfaction (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Persona-conditioned benchmark:** 74 tasks—38 information tasks from Odysseys and 36 action tasks from REAL—paired with four domain-specific personas and an unchanged original request. The benchmark contains 44 constructed personas across 11 domains (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Interactive evaluation protocol:** A persona agent rewrites instructions and answers clarification questions. The tested agent receives only dialogue, without the profile or original instruction, and operates through text-based browser tools (§§2.1–2.2; Appendix B.1).
- **Complementary scoring:** Original-specification completion, simulated satisfaction, and requirement-level “raised, engaged, met” labels distinguish omitted requirements, ignored requirements, and unsuccessful attempts (§2.3).
- **Comparative diagnosis and interventions:** Nine backbones share one harness; profile disclosure and mandatory clarification are separately tested on GPT-6 Astra (§§4.1, 5.3).

These are benchmark and evaluation contributions; the paper does not introduce a trained agent.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Persona requests reduce original-specification completion | Across 74 tasks and nine backbones, mean completion falls from 0.689 to 0.441: −0.248, task-level bootstrap 95% CI [−0.295, −0.202], or −36% relative. | Table 14 |
| Loss varies across constructed groups | Mean completion: expert 0.504, senior 0.402, young 0.414, practical 0.445, versus 0.689 for original requests; all nine backbones lose in each group. | Table 14 |
| Most loss cells involve unsuccessful engagement | Among 1,230 persona cells scoring below their paired original-request cell, 59.9% are classified as engaged-but-unmet, 17.1% as solely unraised requirements, and 11.2% as user-requested stops. | §5.2; Figure 5 |
| Full completion does not ensure simulated satisfaction | Among 646 fully successful persona cells, 62.7% receive 5/5 and 25.9% receive ≤3; corresponding shares for fully successful original requests are 84.1% and 9.5%. | §5.1; Appendix C.3 |
| Verification changes perceived action success | Among 800 failed persona action cells, 56% are judged done at delivery versus 27% after site-state verification. | §5.1 |
| Profile disclosure does not recover completion | GPT-6 Astra, 296 matched persona cells: information completion changes by −0.027, 95% CI [−0.051, −0.001]; action completion by +0.028, [−0.007, +0.069]. | §5.3; Appendix C.6 |
| Mandatory clarification does not recover completion | GPT-6 Astra, matched persona requests: completion changes from 0.434 to 0.421, Δ=−0.013, 95% CI [−0.042, +0.018]. | Appendix C.6 |

The results establish a consistent completion gap within this simulated, text-browser setting. They do **not** establish an equivalent decline in service to real users: persona requests can change the desired outcome, and correct pauses can fail the original checker. The 59.9% figure counts loss cells equally, rather than measuring the proportion of lost completion points (Appendix C.4).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Website tasks only; desktop applications, coding, standalone chat, persistent memory, and repeated user interactions are excluded. Interventions cover one backbone (Limitations).
- **Simulated populations:** Constructed personas may encode stereotypes; demographic performance differences cannot be generalized to real populations, and simulated satisfaction cannot replace real-user feedback (Ethical Statement).
- **Incomplete persona expression:** Only 15.4% of assessed profile dimensions are clearly expressed on average. Excluding profile contradictions barely changes the completion gap, but this does not establish realistic user behavior (Appendix A.4).
- **Measurement uncertainty:** Raised/engaged labels have human–judge κ of 0.20–0.55, and primary loss causes are not independently re-annotated. The human validation excludes raised/engaged labels on 315 REAL outputs (Appendices B.4, C.4).
- **Analyst inference—coupled evaluation:** The same model family simulates users and supplies automated judgments. Satisfaction also receives the hidden original instruction; after-verification ratings can consequently penalize requested stops (Appendices B.2, C.3).
- **Checker validity:** One illustrated REAL checker passes search criteria while missing the original task’s delivery requirement (Figure 13). Thus, passing the checker need not establish complete task fulfillment.

## Open research questions

1. **Do real users reproduce these gaps?** Simulated satisfaction and personas lack direct population validation. Next experiment: compare matched tasks with recruited users and simulated counterparts, measuring completion, constraint adherence, and satisfaction.
2. **Can execution support preserve expressed requirements?** Most loss cells are labeled engaged-but-unmet, but engagement includes misreading. Next experiment: separately annotate interpretation and execution failures, then test a persistent requirement checklist.
3. **Would targeted clarification help?** Mandatory questions do not recover completion, and simulated replies can introduce conflicting details. Next experiment: compare omission-targeted questions with generic questions using replies checked for consistency with task facts.
4. **Can scoring distinguish authorized stopping from failure?** Original checkers and verified satisfaction penalize some requested pauses. Next experiment: evaluate a user-request rubric alongside the original checker, with humans judging authorization boundaries.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the principal aggregate findings and their caveats, but real-user validity remains unestablished.

Material missing information includes the bodies of many referenced tables, the full persona prompt and profile specifications, complete task-level results, repeated-run variability, and verified repository contents. The text also leaves the initial task-candidate selection process only partly specified.
