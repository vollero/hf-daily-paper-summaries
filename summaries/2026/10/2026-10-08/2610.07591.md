# Recurrent Looped Transformer

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-08.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.07591) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07591)


## One-sentence takeaway
On synthetic algorithmic tasks, feeding the previous token’s final decoder state into the next update substantially improves length generalization for parity and swap-based permutation tracking, with task-dependent benefits and increased sequential execution (§§3.2–3.4).

## Problem and motivation
State tracking requires successive updates, while a conventional Transformer applies a fixed number of layers at each position. The authors identify a gap between fixed-depth processing and computation paths that grow with sequence length, particularly for permutation composition (§1). They investigate whether explicit recurrence can improve extrapolation beyond training lengths while retaining parallel causal encoding.

## Approach and contributions
- **Architecture:** RLT separates a parallel causal encoder from a recurrent Transformer decoder. A gated merge combines the current encoder representation with the preceding final decoder output; decoder blocks use sliding-window attention and cross-attention to encoder-prefix memory (§§2.2–2.5).
- **Depth allocation:** At fixed total layer count, the encoder–decoder split controls recurrent update depth. Experiments show different preferred splits for parity and swap-based permutation tracking (§3.2).
- **Feedback interval:** RLT-1 updates feedback every token; RLT-2 shares feedback across a chunk; RLT-0 removes the feedback merge. These variants expose an accuracy–parallelism tradeoff (§§2.7, 3.3).
- **Execution specification:** Appendices define full-history training, cache continuation, and current-policy replay. RL procedures are specified but not experimentally evaluated (§2.8; Appendices D–I).

The authors position the contribution as this particular combination and its design axes; temporal feedback itself appears in the related work (§4).

## Evidence and main results
Unless noted otherwise, accuracy values below are mean ± sample SD over three initialization seeds, using 1,024 shared examples per formal-task test length and checkpoints selected by minimum in-distribution validation loss (§3.1).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong parity extrapolation | Training: 3–40 bits, 2,000 steps. At 256 bits, RLT-1 splits 5+3 and 7+1 achieve **100 ± 0%** final-answer accuracy versus **50.07 ± 1.63%** for Transformer 8. | §3.2; Figure 6 |
| Strong swap-based tracking, with longer-length degradation | Training: 32 operations, 2,000 steps. RLT-1 4+4 final-state accuracy is **97.30 ± 2.76%** at 256 operations and **55.70 ± 25.78%** at 512; Transformer 8 scores **0.85 ± 0.30%** at both lengths. | §3.2; Appendix J.1.5 |
| Intermediate-length arithmetic gains | After 5,000 steps, flat mod-5 at length 63: RLT-1 6+2 **93.36 ± 5.69%**, Transformer **33.20 ± 2.33%**. Bracketed mod-5 at length 64: RLT-1 5+3 **67.97 ± 2.91%**, Transformer **46.71 ± 1.21%**. Training lengths: 3–39 flat, 3–40 bracketed. | §§3.1–3.2; Figure 6 |
| Feedback frequency matters differently across tasks | At split 4+4 after 2,000 steps, 64-bit parity: RLT-1 **100 ± 0%**, chunk4 **98.99 ± 1.66%**, RLT-0 **50.23 ± 3.80%**. At 64 swap operations: RLT-1 **100 ± 0%**, chunk4 **19.60 ± 6.54%**. | §3.3; Figure 8 |
| Benefits are not universal | After 2,000 steps, teacher-forced addition token accuracy at 32-digit operands has model means of **14.89–16.84%** on 256 shared pairs; training used 1–8 digits. Standard S₅ remains near the uniform reference across feedback variants. | §3.4; Appendix J.1.5 |
| Chunking improves measured training speed | Seed-42 CPU timing, split 4+4, two mod-5 tasks, steps 1,001–2,000: chunk4 steps run **2.27×** as fast as RLT-1; RLT-0 **4.17–4.30×**. Validation, checkpointing, and logging excluded. | Appendix J.10 |

These results support task-specific length-generalization gains and a role for feedback in the tested settings. They do not establish general language-model improvements, arbitrary-length correctness, or a compute-matched advantage. “Fixed per-token cost” refers to block count: attention work still grows with context length (Appendix B.2).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated comparison limits:** Equal layer counts do not match parameters or compute; the Transformer comparison also changes attention structure. RLT-0 removes merge parameters as well as feedback (§3.1; Appendices J.1.6, J.9).
- **Author-reported failures and instability:** Standard S₅ remains difficult, addition extrapolation is weak, arithmetic accuracy declines at longer lengths, and some runs show substantial initialization variability or temporary regressions (§§3.2–3.4; Appendix J.1.6).
- **Author-stated scope:** RL is unevaluated, and the proposed large-to-small chunk training schedule was not tested (§§2.7, 3.4). Addition extrapolation for RLT-0 and RLT-2 was also not evaluated (Appendix J.9).
- **Analyst inference:** Three initializations on one fixed data stream provide limited evidence about variability across training datasets. Single-seed CPU timing does not establish accelerator throughput or serving latency.
- **Execution boundary:** Serving equivalence assumes exact arithmetic and aligned execution; different floating-point kernels can change outputs (Appendices B.1, I.3).

## Open research questions
- **Does the advantage persist under matched resources?** Current comparisons change parameters and compute. Next experiment: match parameter count and training compute across RLT and Transformer controls.
- **Can staged chunk sizes recover per-token accuracy efficiently?** The proposed schedule remains untested (§2.7). Next experiment: compare fixed intervals with large-to-small schedules under equal training budgets.
- **Why does recurrence help swaps but fail on standard S₅ and addition?** These failures remain unresolved (§3.4). Next experiment: vary operation distributions and training lengths while measuring intermediate-state errors.
- **Do the gains transfer to language modeling or RL?** Only supervised synthetic tasks are evaluated. Next experiment: test language modeling and an RL task with verified rollout–replay agreement.
