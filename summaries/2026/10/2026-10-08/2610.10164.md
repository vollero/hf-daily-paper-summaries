# UniSkill: Learning Actor-Aligned Skill Proposals for an Evolving Policy

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-08.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.10164) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10164)


## One-sentence takeaway
UniSkill jointly trains an LLM actor and skill proposer using recorded-trajectory likelihood contrasts instead of additional proposal-evaluation rollouts, reporting strong benchmark performance while acknowledging that its alignment proxy sometimes selects harmful skills (§§3–5).

## Problem and motivation
Reusable textual skills can help agents across tasks, but evaluating those skills becomes difficult when the actor also learns: later success may reflect actor improvement rather than skill utility. Testing each candidate before updating the actor avoids this confound but requires additional environment rollouts. The authors seek cheaper feedback that measures a proposed skill’s usefulness for the current actor (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Shared-policy co-evolution:** One policy executes tasks with retrieved skills and proposes Add, Update, or No Edit operations from paired successful and failed trajectories. Actor training uses GRPO; proposer training uses REINFORCE++ (§§3.1–3.3).
- **Contrastive action feedback:** Holding the pre-update actor and recorded histories fixed, UniSkill measures how substituting a candidate skill changes the action log-likelihood gap between separate successful and failed reference trajectories. This supplies proposal feedback without new evaluation rollouts (§3.3).
- **Edit exploration and admission controls:** A probability-floor regularizer discourages suppression of available edit operations. A frozen critic checks appropriateness and grounded, reusable content; committed edits must also increase successful-reference likelihood and the contrastive gap (§§3.3–3.4).

These are the authors’ claimed contributions; GRPO, REINFORCE++, embedding retrieval, and the benchmark environments are components rather than newly introduced methods.

The evidence supports benchmark effectiveness and an informative aggregate alignment signal. It does not establish that every admitted skill improves execution, statistically significant superiority over close baselines, or quantified end-to-end compute savings. Proposal scoring avoids additional rollouts, but training still requires actor rollouts, likelihood computation, and critic calls.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-observed proxy failures:** Positive alignment can accompany reduced rollout success because the actor inconsistently follows guidance or the skill omits action-order constraints. The additional admission check removes some failures, but others remain (Appendix E.2.2).
- **Analyst inference—proposal coverage:** Eligible groups need successful and failed references beyond the proposer’s input pair. Uniformly successful or failed groups cannot provide this feedback; coverage as training progresses is not reported (§3.3).
- **Analyst inference—limited validation scope:** Proxy validation covers two ALFWorld checkpoints and source training queries. Broader calibration across tasks, later checkpoints, and WebShop is not established (§5, Q2; Appendix D.3).
- **Comparison uncertainty:** Most baselines are quoted rather than rerun; Complementary RL’s purchase-completion metric is explicitly excluded from ranking. Alternative critics receive only single-run evaluation (§4.1; Table 1; Appendix D.2).
- **Resource and reporting limits:** Smaller-backbone experiments shrink the shared policy while retaining external components. Hardware, exact timing values, and several table bodies and prompts are unavailable in the supplied text (Appendices A–B; §4.3).

## Open research questions

1. **Can admission better predict closed-loop improvement?** Harmful proposals survive both current checks. Next experiment: compare alternative admission thresholds against fixed-actor rollout gains on held-out queries (Appendix E.2.2).
2. **How should proposal learning work when outcomes lack diversity?** Current reference construction excludes such groups. Next experiment: measure eligible-group coverage throughout training and compare reference-buffer strategies (§3.3).
3. **Does the alignment proxy generalize beyond ALFWorld source tasks?** Direct utility validation is restricted to those tasks and two checkpoints. Next experiment: repeat the matched-rollout analysis on WebShop and unseen ALFWorld tasks (Appendix D.3).
4. **How robust are exploration settings?** The probability floor is heuristic and the feedback magnitude comes from a preliminary ALFWorld run. Next experiment: sweep both settings across benchmarks and multiple seeds (Appendix B).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly specifies the method and main results, but omits prompt contents, several table bodies, exact timing and hardware details, evaluation episode counts, and full numerical smaller-model results. Figure references conflict for timing versus model-size plots, and implementation availability is inconsistent between the abstract and Reproducibility Statement.
