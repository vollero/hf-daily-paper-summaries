# CoDance: Learning Reactive and Compliant Human-Humanoid Interaction from Video

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-08.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.05324) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.05324)


## One-sentence takeaway
CoDance learns partner-conditioned humanoid dancing from one paired-human video by augmenting motion references with synthetic two-hand forces, demonstrating compliant wrist responses in simulation and sustained dancing on a physical robot.

## Problem and motivation
Partnered dancing requires a humanoid to coordinate footsteps with a moving person while accommodating continuous hand contact and maintaining balance. Conventional motion imitation penalizes departures from a reference, although compliant interaction requires such departures; video demonstrations also lack force measurements. The authors address the combined gap of partner-responsive locomotion and compliant two-hand interaction (§I).

## Approach and contributions
- **Video-to-interaction pipeline:** Reconstruct and separately retarget both dancers, using one as the robot reference and the other as a simulated partner. Temporal reversal, force-free samples, and clip chaining expand the demonstration (§III-A–B).
- **Partner-informed multi-link augmentation:** Extend the SoftMimic compliance procedure with coupled wrist displacement intents, partner-guided force directions, and balance-aware force scaling. Whole-body inverse kinematics generates adapted references and force schedules (§III-A, Algorithm 1).
- **Combined learned control:** Train PPO policies with partner-following and compliance rewards, plus either adversarial locomotion imitation or lower-body reference tracking. The actor receives partner pelvis/knee histories, proprioception, reference information, and commanded stiffness, but no applied-force measurements (§III-B).
- **Physical demonstration:** Deploy the decoupled policy on a Unitree G1 using Vicon partner tracking and direct human hand contact (§III-C, §IV-G).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Partner observations affect following, but reference mismatch remains difficult | Decoupled policy: observed partner **0.057 m** foot error at **1 s**, **99.6%** clip success; reversed partner **0.267 m**, **28.6%** success. Whole-body policy: **0.072 m**, **99.7%**, versus **0.255 m**, **24.3%** under reversal. | §IV-C, Table II |
| Force-aware training produces greater wrist adaptation | On seen simulated clips, both compliant policies reproduce approximately **80%** of adapted-reference wrist displacement, with **2.6 cm** mean displacement error; stiff tracker reproduces approximately **20%**, with more than twice the error. On unseen schedules, compliant-policy error increases by less than **1 cm**. Measurements use the final **0.2 s** of force holds. | §IV-B–D, Fig. 3 |
| The locomotion objective preserves demonstrated lower-body motion | On unseen clips with force events, removing the adversarial term raises lower-body rotation error from **0.225 to 0.587 rad**, while foot error remains **0.072 m**. Whole-body tracking yields **0.197 rad** rotation error and **0.074 m** foot error. | §IV-E, Table III |
| Training with clip chaining improves direction-transition success | On two unseen force-event clips, one per direction, decoupled success is **97.6%** with chaining training versus **48.0%** without; foot errors are **0.073 m** and **0.076 m**, respectively. Whole-body success is **96.2%**. | §IV-F, Table IV |
| Hardware dancing is feasible in the reported trials | All **three** runs maintain two-hand contact without falling: one lasts **12.8 s**, two last **38.9 s** each. Root distance stays within **0.1 m** of the **0.5 m** target for **93%, 83%, and 73%** of the respective runs. | §IV-G, Fig. 5 |

Simulation results average three evaluation seeds with 400 trials per seed (§IV-A). Together, the ablations support the usefulness of partner observations, force-aware references, locomotion objectives, and chaining within this setting. “Unseen” clips contain newly sampled augmentation events from the same underlying dance, so these results establish schedule generalization rather than generalization to new dances (§IV-A). Hardware evidence demonstrates feasibility, but does not quantitatively validate wrist compliance against the simulated metric.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Only one video and one dance are studied. The simulated partner replays fixed motion with synthetic interaction forces; reference dependence limits opposite-direction following. A fixed directional offset excludes dances with changing relative placement (§V).
- **Analyst inference:** The reported experiments do not separately ablate coupled wrist intents, partner-guided directions, or balance scaling, leaving their individual contributions unresolved (§IV).
- **Analyst inference:** Three hardware runs provide limited evidence about reliability across people, body geometries, or interaction styles. Hardware hand-force measurements and numerical compliance comparisons are not reported (§III-C, §IV-G).
- **Comparison caveat:** The no-foot-constraint baseline also removes foot-distance termination, so its high success rate is not directly comparable under identical failure criteria (§IV-A).
- **Reproducibility:** Architecture, rewards, randomization, and training settings are supplied, but code, checkpoints, and dataset release availability are not established (§III, Appendix).

## Open research questions
- **Can following become less reference-dependent?** Reversed-partner success remains low (§IV-C, §V). A next experiment could vary partner speed, pauses, and direction independently of the reference.
- **Which augmentation extensions matter most?** Their individual effects are not isolated (§IV). A next experiment could separately remove coupled intents, partner guidance, and balance scaling while measuring compliance and falls.
- **Does the method generalize across dances and partners?** The study uses one dance and a narrow hardware evaluation (§IV-G, §V). A next experiment could hold out dances and participants, including turning and changing relative placement.
- **Does simulated compliance predict physical contact behavior?** Hardware compliance is qualitative (§IV-G, Fig. 6). A next experiment could instrument both hands and compare displacement, force error, and stability under controlled pulls.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported method and within-setting results; **low** for broader generalization.

Material missing information includes public asset availability, exact numerical values underlying Fig. 3 beyond the prose summary, independent training-run variability, hardware force/compliance measurements, and evaluations across additional dances and human partners.
