# Agent Plasticity: Measuring Self-Improvement Through Experience

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-08.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.08902) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08902)


## One-sentence takeaway
Under a fixed-weight, persistent-artifact protocol, agents differ substantially in held-out improvement per learning dollar, and the highest endpoint performer is not necessarily the most efficient learner (§§3.2–3.4).

## Problem and motivation
Static agent evaluations measure current capability but miss whether experience produces lasting improvements, whether those improvements generalize, and how much learning costs. The authors address this measurement gap rather than introduce a new self-improvement algorithm: endpoint scores alone cannot distinguish strong initial capability from efficient capability acquisition (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled evaluation protocol:** Frozen models act in fresh contexts and inherit only self-constructed Python tools, skills, strategies, and memory. Reflection receives training evidence; board-game held-out trajectories and scores remain inaccessible. Proposed updates undergo structural and executable checks (§2.1).
- **Agent plasticity metric:** Held-out score gain divided by cumulative training and reflection cost, including rejected updates. The headline measure estimates efficiency to saturation using a fitted learning curve; dollar cost is a proxy, not physical computation (§§2.1–2.2).
- **Comparative measurement and diagnostics:** Learning trajectories across Chess, Go, Hex, and NetHack distinguish endpoint capability, acquisition efficiency, and transfer. Offline artifact analysis separates missing coverage, missed reuse, and failures despite reuse (§§3.2–3.5).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Persistent chess gains vary substantially | Hard-chess held-out ID score, defined as \(100(W+0.5D)/N\): Claude Fable 5 increases from 37.5% at checkpoint 0 to 73.3% averaged over checkpoints 16–20; Claude Opus 5 increases from 25.0% to 66.4%. | §3.2; Fig. 3 |
| Endpoint capability and learning efficiency diverge | Combined held-out ID curve across chess, Go, and Hex: estimated saturation plasticity is 298 for GPT-5.6 Sol, 233 for Claude Opus 5, 57 for Claude Fable 5, and 14 for Claude Opus 4.8, in percentage points per $1,000 of mean per-game learning cost. Fable has the highest fitted endpoint. | §3.4; Fig. 1 |
| Transfer can be substantial in particular tasks | Between checkpoints 0 and 20, Fable’s Go score rises from 20% to 80% on held-out ID and 0% to 50% on OOD; Sol’s Hex score rises from 40% to 77.5% on ID and 28.1% to 78.1% on OOD. Scores use \(100(W+0.5D)/N\). | §3.3; Fig. 9 |
| Board-game efficiency does not predict NetHack improvement | NetHack mean raw score across early checkpoints 0–2 versus late checkpoints 36–40: Opus 5.5 rises from 2,019 to 5,831; Sol declines from about 570 to 420. Each checkpoint uses ten fresh games. | §3.3; Appendix C |
| High reuse is insufficient for strong performance | Fable, Opus 5, Opus 4.8, and Sol reuse artifacts at 94–98% of relevant chess decisions, pooled across Hard/Easy conditions and checkpoints 1–20, yet achieve different game scores. | §3.5; Figs. 4, 29 |

These results support persistent adaptation beyond reflection-visible board-game interactions and distinguish efficiency from endpoint capability. They do not establish that feedback itself causes the gains, that artifact reuse causes improvement, or that model rankings transfer across tasks. NetHack evaluates fresh games before using their trajectories for reflection, rather than maintaining the board games’ separate held-out splits (Appendix C).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ limitations:** Experience feedback and additional artifact-development computation are not disentangled; budget-matched reflection without feedback is missing (§4).
- **Authors’ limitations:** OOD means stronger opponents, not arbitrary new tasks. Separately trained lineages do not demonstrate cross-task artifact transfer (§§2.1, 4).
- **Authors’ limitations:** Finite evaluations introduce noise; plasticity depends on score headroom, horizon, provider pricing, and saturation assumptions. The monotone fit summarizes rises while suppressing individual regressions (§§2.2, 4).
- **Authors’ limitations:** Reuse diagnostics are observational and use Gemini 3.1 Pro semantic judgments alongside execution logs; they cannot distinguish artifact inadequacy from poor application (§2.3; Appendix G).
- **Additional supplied caveats:** NetHack has one lineage per model, changing game difficulty across checkpoints, and earlier harness versions for other lineages (Appendices B–C; Table 3 caption).
- **Analyst inference:** Position memorization can contribute to held-out gains: Figure 15 explicitly shows a fix useful only when a particular position recurs, limiting interpretation as broad strategic learning.

## Open research questions
- **How much improvement requires environmental feedback?** The paper does not isolate feedback from extra computation (§4). Compare genuine-feedback reflection with budget-matched no-feedback and shuffled-feedback conditions.
- **Does forced reuse resolve weak improvement?** Reuse correlates with gains but is not causal (§3.5). Randomize forced invocation, artifact removal, and artifact replacement at evaluation time.
- **Do artifacts or improvement strategies transfer across tasks?** Independent lineages leave this unanswered (§4). Compare transferred inventories against empty inventories under matched learning budgets.
- **Can plasticity itself be trained?** The study measures rather than optimizes it (§4). Compare post-training objectives rewarding immediate performance versus future held-out gains, then evaluate on unseen tasks.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported protocol and descriptive findings; **medium** for robustness of efficiency rankings and bottleneck interpretations.

The supplied text lacks the bodies of setup and analyzer tables, numerical bootstrap intervals, detailed pricing and fitted parameters, independent-run variability for board games, human validation of diagnostic labels, and an explicit code/data release statement.
