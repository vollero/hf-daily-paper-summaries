# SheetSage2: Coherent Lead-Sheet Transcription with Synthetic Supervision

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-08.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.05336) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.05336)


## One-sentence takeaway
SheetSage2 combines MIDI-derived supervision, structured decoding, and autoregressive distillation to improve lead-sheet transcription across the evaluated benchmarks while reducing tempo-level and octave inconsistency, within a restricted popular-music setting (§§3–5).

## Problem and motivation
Lead-sheet transcription must recover rhythm, harmony, melody, and form from polyphonic audio and organize them into readable notation. Independent task predictions can disagree about beat grids, melodic intervals, and harmonic context. The authors identify two obstacles: scarce complete audio–score annotations and local predictions that remain musically inconsistent despite reasonable task accuracy (§1).

## Approach and contributions
1. **Scalable synthetic supervision.** Symbolic models learn from rule-derived or metadata-derived seed labels, annotate MIDI collections, and transfer those labels to rendered audio. Synthetic and partially annotated real recordings jointly supervise the acoustic models (§§2.3–2.4; Appendix B.2).
2. **Structured transcription teacher.** Two MERT2-FS-based Prober models predict musical attributes. Sequential decoders impose a shared tempo reference and rhythm grid, then decode meter, key, sections, chords, and interval-dependent melody (§§2.4–2.7).
3. **Autoregressive distillation.** A student learns exclusively from the teacher’s decoded event sequences. Grammar-constrained greedy generation produces unified events that can be exported as ABC notation or MIDI, without task-specific dynamic programming at inference (§§2.1–2.2; Appendix A).

These contributions extend existing MIDI labeling, acoustic encoders, and sequence-model decoding; the authors do not present those underlying ingredients as entirely new (§4).

The evidence supports stronger task performance and specific consistency benefits on the evaluated cohorts. It does not establish universally superior transcription: several metrics favor specialists or Prober. Octave consistency permits a uniformly wrong register, and the main melody metric does not test note durations or absolute octaves. No aggregate human assessment of score readability is reported.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Sixteenth-note quantization approximates triplets and finer subdivisions; one tempo anchor limits large tempo changes; meter and chord inventories are restricted. The model assumes 12-tone equal temperament and targets steady-beat popular music rather than classical or free-rhythm music (§5).
- **Evaluation boundaries:** Hooktheory consistency diagnostics overlap training data. Consistency cohorts require assessable predictions or sufficient note matches across systems, restricting which failures they capture (Appendices E.1–E.2).
- **Comparison caveats:** Madmom’s chord training overlaps Chords1217; ChordFormer uses cross-validation there and ensembles on other chord datasets. Key scoring evaluates only the longest predicted key segment (Appendix D.1).
- **Analyst inference:** The rhythm comparison changes several decoder ingredients together, so it does not isolate the tempo anchor’s individual contribution (§3.3; Appendix C.4).
- **Reproducibility boundary:** Weights and inference code are reported released, but private/licensed training data constrain independent training reproduction. The supplied text provides no uncertainty estimates for the main comparisons.

## Open research questions
1. **Which rhythm constraint drives consistency?** The P-DBN comparison bundles different observations and decoder settings. Next experiment: remove the global anchor, relative-tempo evidence, and meter flexibility separately while holding other settings fixed.
2. **Can richer synthetic audio improve transfer?** Synthetic-only vocal transcription remains weaker, and the selected example has poor onsets and lengths (Appendix D.4). Next experiment: compare matched MIDI supervision rendered with different timbres and vocal synthesis.
3. **Does consistency improve practical score usability?** Existing metrics omit duration accuracy, spelling quality, or absolute register. Next experiment: add expert readability/editing assessments and duration-aware scoring on held-out complete lead sheets.
4. **Can the representation support broader music?** The grid, tuning, harmony, and tempo assumptions explicitly restrict coverage (§5). Next experiment: extend those representations and evaluate separately on triplets, large tempo changes, and underrepresented musical traditions.
