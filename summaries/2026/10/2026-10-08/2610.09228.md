# Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-08.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.09228) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09228)


## One-sentence takeaway
Robo-COP couples deployment-derived policy fine-tuning with candidate verification and orchestrator memory revision, improving mean held-out success over a frozen-policy harness on ten simulated and three physical manipulation tasks (§§4.2–4.3).

## Problem and motivation
An adaptive robot orchestrator can route around a frozen vision-language-action (VLA) policy’s failures, but cannot repair its execution capabilities. Updating the policy introduces another problem: the orchestrator’s accumulated advice may describe an obsolete checkpoint. Deployment also supplies limited, partly failed demonstrations, and an unsuccessful update can consume task attempts. The authors address joint adaptation without a separate exploration phase (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Coupled deployment loop:** A vision-language model orchestrates scripted motions and VLA calls, reflects on episodes, and revises policy-dependent memory after candidate adoption or rejection (§§3.1, 3.3).
- **Skill-level demonstration curation:** A unified action stream records both scripted and policy actions. Text and video judges retain successful or useful partial skills, including segments from failed episodes (§3.2; Appendix C).
- **Selective training and verification:** A controller requests training when accumulated demonstrations plausibly address recurring failures. Candidates initialize from the pretrained policy and undergo fine-tuning; adoption requires task safeguards, checks on sufficiently observed previously admitted skills, and semantic review of target skills (§§3.2–3.3; Appendices D–G).

The contribution is this integrated deployment process; LoRA, the underlying VLA, perception tools, and simulator are existing components.

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Higher simulated held-out success | Equally weighted mean across ten tasks, with 50 shared held-out initializations per task: Robo-COP **73.8%**, frozen harness **64.8%**, fixed schedule **65.8%**, adapted ASPIRE **54.0%**, standalone VLA **28.0%**. | §4.2; Fig. 5; Table 6 |
| Benefits vary by task regime | Held-out means: VLA-sufficient tasks, Robo-COP **80.0%** versus frozen **89.3%**; primitive-sufficient tasks, **72.0%** versus frozen **55.0%** and ASPIRE **73.5%**; neither-sufficient tasks, **70.0%** versus frozen **53.3%**. | Table 1 |
| Higher average deployment success | Across 100 deployment trials per simulated task, including collection and verification: Robo-COP **73.2%**, frozen **68.7%**, fixed schedule **70.3%**. | §4.2; Table 6 |
| Selective candidate adoption | Controller trained on seven simulated tasks and waited on three; verification rejected **5 of 12** candidates, and six tasks ended with an updated policy. | §4.2; Table 4 |
| Fewer grasp/drop failures | Across 500 simulated held-out trials, total failures fell from **176** for frozen to **131** for Robo-COP; grasp/drop failures fell from **68** to **40**. | §4.2, pp. 7–8 |
| Physical gains concentrated in one task | Three tasks, each with 50 learning trials and 20 held-out tests: mean held-out success **50.0%** versus frozen **38.3%**. The adopted-update task improved from **35% to 70%**; the other two tied at **40%**. | §4.3; Table 2 |
| Forced training could hurt | On the physical mustard-and-carrots task, a separate forced, unverified update achieved **20%** held-out success versus **40%** for both main systems. | §4.3, p. 9 |

These results support improvement of the complete system within the tested settings, including better average success during learning. They do not establish universal gains: Robo-COP trails the frozen harness in the VLA-sufficient regime. The failure analysis is consistent with improved grasping, but does not independently isolate the effect of training on segments from failed episodes.

## Limitations and validity risks
**Author-stated:** Learning requires skills the existing policy or scripted tools can demonstrate at least partially. Physical trials need human resets, updates add curation/training/evaluation costs, and deployment pauses for training. Scripted skills, judges, and orchestrator reasoning remain fixed. Larger-scale validation is still needed (§§3.2, 5).

**Analyst inferences:**

- **Component attribution remains incomplete:** Fixed-schedule training changes both scheduling and verification together. Separate ablations of the controller, verifier, memory revision, and failed-episode curation are not reported in the supplied text (§4.1; Appendix H).
- **Learning-run uncertainty is limited:** Evaluation uses one learning run per method and task. Figure 5 intervals concern the fixed task set, rather than variability across independently repeated learning runs (§4.1; Appendix H).
- **Verification offers bounded protection:** Numerical safeguards tolerate declines and check prior skills only with sufficient calls; small groups cannot block adoption. Semantic review supplements these checks but has no reported independent accuracy audit (Appendices E–G).
- **Memory history affects comparisons:** On stack sorting, frozen-harness success falls from **61% during deployment to 6% held-out**, following late deployment decline. This makes the final memory state consequential to the reported comparison (Appendix H, pp. 18–19).

## Open research questions
- **Which components produce the gains?** Their effects are bundled in the comparisons. Next experiment: matched runs independently removing selective scheduling, verification, memory revision, and failed-episode segments.
- **Does learning transfer across tasks?** Pooled multi-task training is proposed as future work. Next experiment: train on accumulated skills from several tasks and evaluate unseen tasks plus retention of earlier skills (§5).
- **How reliable are the automated judges?** Independent curation and adoption accuracy is not reported. Next experiment: compare decisions with human-annotated trajectories and measure downstream policy effects (Appendices C, G).
- **What is the benefit per deployment hour?** Updates pause task execution, while results emphasize success per trial. Next experiment: include training, judging, evaluation, and reset time in an end-to-end throughput comparison (§§3.2, 5).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported protocol and aggregate results; **medium** in component-level causal attribution and broader generalization.

Material missing information includes repeated independent learning runs, isolated component ablations, independent judge accuracy, end-to-end compute/time costs, and cross-task or longer-term retention experiments. Code and videos are claimed available, but their contents and reproducibility were not verified from the supplied text.
