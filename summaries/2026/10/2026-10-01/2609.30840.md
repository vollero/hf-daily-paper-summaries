# Aligning One-Step Generative Models with Reward-Weighted Transport Distillation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-01.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.30840) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30840)


## One-sentence takeaway
RWTD aligns one-step image generators through reward-weighted transport between current and reference samples, improving SANA Sprint’s GenEval score from 0.73 to 0.80 while offering a measured reward–diversity trade-off (Table 1; Table 7).

## Problem and motivation
General one-step generators lack tractable likelihoods and multi-step denoising trajectories, while useful rewards may be non-differentiable. The authors identify a gap in post-training methods that can improve reward using scalar feedback while preserving one-step inference, reference quality, diversity, and conditional fidelity (§§1, 3.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Adaptive alignment target:** RWTD separately normalizes exponential reward weights for current and frozen-reference samples, then mixes their distributions. Reference mass \(ρ\) controls anchoring versus adaptation. The authors claim novelty for this particular mixed target (§4.1).
- **Transport and distillation:** A frozen visual encoder supplies feature distances for entropic optimal transport. The generator regresses toward detached, partially interpolated transport destinations. Rewards require no gradients, but generator updates differentiate through the frozen encoder (§§4.2–4.3).
- **Fixed-point analysis:** Under idealized assumptions, mixed tilting adds a reward-dependent rational amplification beyond ordinary reference tilting; exact partial transport preserves the fixed-point set. These results characterize equilibria rather than guarantee practical training convergence (§5; Appendix A).
- **Empirical validation:** Experiments evaluate compositional alignment on two backbones and preference alignment on SANA Sprint, with target-assignment, hyperparameter, diversity, and early-stopping analyses (§6; Appendix C).

These results support improved compositional correctness and broader automated metric gains in the tested settings. The preference and GenEval optimization experiments are separate: the 0.80 result does not describe the HPSv2-trained model. The evidence does not establish universal superiority, human preference gains, or full preservation of diversity. The authors’ state-of-the-art claim is supported here by the listed comparisons, without independent verification.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated theoretical boundary:** Practical training uses finite minibatches, entropic couplings, barycentric projections, and neural regression; the population results provide intuition rather than strict guarantees (§5.2). Fixed-point existence also requires additional conditions (§A.2).
- **Author-reported trade-offs:** Every aligned model in Table 7 has lower LPIPS diversity than the base. Increasing reward temperature strengthens some preference metrics while reducing CLIP (§C.1).
- **Analyst inference—limited generality:** Experiments cover two visual backbones, with preference alignment reported on SANA Sprint. Applicability to other generator families or modalities remains untested (§6).
- **Analyst inference—measurement uncertainty:** Tables report point estimates without confidence intervals or repeated-training variability. Consequently, the reliability of small preference-metric differences is unclear.
- **Analyst inference—encoder dependence:** MAE and DINOv2 are paired with different backbones, so these experiments do not isolate the effect of feature geometry (§B.3).
- **Comparison qualification:** SANA DrPO and DRaFT were reimplemented and tuned on held-out validation prompts. Early-stopping analysis examines the earliest saved checkpoint, so it does not exhaust all possible stopping choices (§B.4; §C.2).

## Open research questions
1. **How general is the alignment interface?** The experiments remain confined to visual backbones. A next experiment could apply RWTD to a differently trained one-step generator using the same reward and evaluation protocol.
2. **Can diversity be retained more fully?** Reference mixing reduces collapse but remains below base LPIPS. A next experiment could sweep or schedule reference mass and measure reward, diversity, and compositional fidelity jointly.
3. **How much does encoder choice determine outcomes?** Backbone and encoder changes are confounded. A next experiment could compare MAE and DINOv2 on the same backbone with fixed particle counts and training budget.
4. **Do automated gains translate into reliable human preference gains?** The supplied results contain no direct human evaluation or training-run uncertainty. A next experiment could combine blinded human comparisons with repeated training seeds.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported method and table-level findings; broader generalization remains uncertain.
