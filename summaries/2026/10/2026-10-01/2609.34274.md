# BIABench: Evaluating AI agents on real-world bioimage analysis tasks

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-01.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.34274) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34274)


## One-sentence takeaway
BIABench evaluates agents on 16 study-derived bioimage workflows and finds that strong routine-task performance coexists with poor complex-task accuracy, substantial run variability, and process scores that weakly predict correctness (§§3, 5).

## Problem and motivation
Bioimage analysis requires turning raw microscopy data into measurements through multiple steps, specialized software, and quality control. The authors identify a gap between benchmarks for individual image-analysis steps and evaluations of autonomous workflows that choose their own methods and reproduce a study’s biological readout. Large volumes and time series also require agents to inspect data through code, tools, and rendered views rather than directly consuming all images as context (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Study-to-task construction:** Reconstruct published analyses as raw inputs, a biological measurement request, and reference outputs, while withholding the source study from agents (§3.1).
- **Benchmark and execution interface:** Provide 16 tasks covering eleven subtasks, brief and detailed instructions, explicit output contracts, and wrappers for six coding, Python tool-use, and Fiji/ImageJ GUI agents (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Separate outcome and process evaluation:** Rank submissions using task-specific outcome metrics; separately assess saved artifacts with a severity-weighted VLM rubric validated against expert review (§3.3).
- **Empirical analysis:** Compare harnesses, models, instruction detail, costs, and repeated-run reliability (§§4–5). The contribution is an evaluation framework and study, rather than a new image-analysis algorithm.

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Routine tasks perform substantially better than the hardest workflows | With GPT-5.6 Sol and three runs per agent–task pair, best mean outcomes on NF-κB, HeLa segmentation, and cell counting were 0.85–0.96; every agent reached at least 0.56 on these tasks | §5.1; Fig. 2a |
| Complex-task failures persist across configurations | No tested model–instruction combination exceeded mean outcome 0.25 on nuclear-pore kinetics or 3D puncta quantification; their composites assess onset/curve agreement and per-nucleus count error, respectively | §5.2; Table A2 |
| Biological specialization shows no overall advantage in this setting | General-purpose agents achieved or tied the highest task mean on 14 of 16 tasks under the shared GPT-5.6 Sol model | §5.1 |
| Detailed protocols redistribute accuracy | DeepSeek Harness mean outcome changed by +0.01 with V4-Flash and <0.01 with GPT-5.6 Sol; V4-Flash bacterial tracking rose from 0.32 to 0.94, while cell counting fell from 0.83 to 0.42 | §§5.2; Fig. 2c |
| Repeated-run instability exceeds the agent effect | In the GPT-5.6 Sol comparison, 40% of agent–task pairs had outcome ranges >0.2; variance shares were task 63%, run-to-run 21%, task–agent interaction 13%, and agent 3% | Fig. 3 |
| Process scoring weakly predicts outcome | Across 282 scored GPT-5.6 Sol runs, Pearson r was 0.29, falling to 0.09 after excluding outcomes ≤0.05; expert process scores on 20 runs gave r = −0.03 | §5.3; Figs. 2e, 5; Appendix E |
| Judge agreement is useful but imperfect | On 922 jointly decided items from 20 reviewed runs, accuracy was 87%, κ = 0.67 (95% CI 0.61–0.73); the judge passed 27% of expert-failed items | §3.3; Table E2 |
| Longer execution does not reliably identify better runs | The longest run scored best in 20/70 pairs whose outcomes differed by >0.05; outcomes <0.1 took median 21 minutes versus 10 minutes for outcomes ≥0.5 | Fig. 6 |

These results support a reliability gap within the tested suite and configurations. They do not isolate dimensionality as a causal factor or establish that all possible process-based checks are ineffective. Outcome scores are heterogeneous task metrics, so their aggregate is a benchmark summary rather than one uniform measure of biological accuracy.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Some specialized agents primarily support human interaction; autonomous evaluation may underrepresent their intended use (§5.1). Human near-perfect performance is assumed from study endpoints, rather than measured through a human benchmark (§6).
- **Author-documented comparison constraints:** Most model-exchange sessions used shorter budgets. Incompatible harness–model pairings were omitted, and provider safety refusals were excluded from outcome means (§4; Appendix C).
- **Author-documented scoring limits:** NF-κB uses dose–response properties without per-well ground truth; zebrafish and vascular morphometric tables are requested but not scored (Appendices A, D).
- **Analyst inference:** Selected subsets and the synthetic microtubule task limit generalization to broader laboratory workloads. Dimensionality, input size, and pipeline complexity vary together, preventing a clean causal attribution (Table A1; Appendix A).
- **Analyst inference:** Process validation uses one author-expert, omits raw inputs from that review, and excludes undecidable items. Unequal artifact preservation and capped judge evidence may affect comparability; one puncta evaluator also supplies outcome metrics to the judge (Appendix E).

## Open research questions
1. **What drives complex-task failure?** Size, dimensionality, and pipeline length are not independently varied. A matched experiment could vary these factors separately while holding the biological readout fixed.
2. **Can agents select their own successful reruns?** Best-of-three outcomes improve, but process scores and runtime provide weak selection signals (§5.3). Test image-grounded checks and table–report consistency checks on held-out runs.
3. **When does expert guidance help?** Detailed instructions produce large opposing task changes (§5.2). Ablate tool recommendations, parameter guidance, and output conventions separately.
4. **How much does human intervention help specialized agents?** Their autonomous results do not evaluate their intended interactive use (§5.1). Compare autonomous execution with a fixed allowance of expert corrections.

## Confidence and missing information
**High** confidence in the reported benchmark design and descriptive findings; **medium** confidence in broader generalization.

The supplied text does not provide measured human task performance, independent multi-expert validation, uncertainty intervals for aggregate agent rankings, or controlled ablations separating data size, dimensionality, and workflow complexity. Released code, data, and archived runs are described but were not independently inspected.
