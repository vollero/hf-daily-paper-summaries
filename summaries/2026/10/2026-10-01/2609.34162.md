# SlideDP: Scaling Host-Resident LLM Fine-Tuning Across Multiple GPUs

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-01.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.34162) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34162)


## One-sentence takeaway
SlideDP coordinates shared CPU-resident training states, GPU communication, and layer-wise updates to improve synchronous full-parameter LLM fine-tuning throughput and capacity on single-node multi-GPU systems.

## Problem and motivation
Layer streaming keeps persistent model and optimizer states in host memory, allowing fine-tuning beyond GPU memory capacity. Extending it directly to data parallelism replicates transfers and gradient returns across GPUs competing for fixed CPU, DRAM, and PCIe resources. Strong scaling also shortens the GPU computation available to hide host work (§§1–2.4).

The authors identify a scheduling gap: reducing redundant state movement alone does not coordinate transfers, CPU updates, buffer reuse, and parameter versions within a bounded GPU working window (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Shared-host runtime:** One authoritative copy of FP32 master weights and Adam states serves all ranks. Replicated or sharded parameter delivery is independent of ownership; GPU reduction can return one aggregated gradient per layer. Layer and chunk pipelines overlap transfers and updates while enforcing synchronous parameter-version dependencies (§§3.2–3.3).
- **Scaling model:** Resource-service demands and execution dependencies describe when host work becomes exposed and why communication choices depend on topology and workload (§3.4).
- **Measured policy selection:** Elastic Checkpointing exposes retention, offload, and recomputation choices. AutoPolicy probes routes and chunks, calibrates activation candidates in a reduced pipeline, allocates layer policies through a MILP, and validates memory feasibility (§§3.5–3.6).

The claimed contribution is their coordination in a shared-host synchronous runtime; the paper explicitly builds on existing offloading, checkpointing, and layer-wise update mechanisms (§2).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Matched-batch throughput gains | Qwen3-8B, four RTX 4090s, batches 4–32/GPU: geometric-mean speedups of **1.83×** over ZeRO-Offload and **1.48×** over MegaTrain. Qwen3-14B, four H100s: **1.46×** over ZeRO-Offload at batches 16–64 and **1.59×** over MegaTrain at 16–128. Default sequence length: 1024. | §4.2, Figs. 6–7 |
| Larger-batch peak exceeds measured FSDP2 peak | Qwen3-14B on four H100s: **23.2K tokens/s**, **1,048,576 tokens/step**, without gradient accumulation at batch 256/GPU; **11.2%** above FSDP2 at batch 32/GPU. This comparison uses different batches. | §4.2, Figs. 7, 10 |
| Expanded model and sequence capacity | Qwen2.5-72B on four RTX 4090s: **240 TFLOPS**, **1.56×** SlideFormer; other three offloading baselines marked OOM. Separately, Qwen3-14B on four H100s completes **256K-token** sequences at one sequence/GPU and **1.86 PFLOPS**. | §4.2, Figs. 9, 11 |
| Near-linear weak scaling | Qwen3-32B, 64 sequences/GPU: **95–98%** parallel efficiency across two, four, and eight A800s; eight-GPU throughput **7,963 tokens/s**. | §4.5, Fig. 14 |
| Route preference reverses with topology | Qwen3-14B, four A800s, batches 8/16: sharded delivery improves throughput **16.8–32.7%** with NVLink bridges; replicated delivery improves it **19.2–26.0%** after bridge removal. | §4.5, Fig. 15 |
| Policy gains require amortization | H100/Qwen3-14B batch 32 ablation: **12.4%** throughput gain, GPU memory **15.0→76.6 GiB**. A separate cached-profile selection run costs **1,218 s**, saves **0.970 s/step**, and estimates break-even at **1,257 steps**. | Table 3; §4.6 |
| Dense-model loss agreement | Qwen3-14B, four H100s, batch 16/GPU, sequence 1024, **200 steps**, matched C4 samples and optimizer settings: maximum absolute mean-loss differences versus FSDP2 are **5.2×10⁻⁴** for fixed checkpointing and **6.3×10⁻⁴** for the selected layout. | §4.7 |

These author-reported results support improved throughput and feasible workload capacity in the tested settings. Largest-feasible-batch comparisons combine capacity and speed benefits; they do not isolate equal-batch execution efficiency. Projected compute references and sequence capacities are estimates, while the loss comparison supports short-run numerical agreement rather than general convergence equivalence (§§4.1–4.3, 4.7).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Explicit scope:** The runtime targets one shared-host node. Multi-node scaling is not evaluated (§3.1).
- **Explicit modeling qualification:** AutoPolicy optimizes a calibrated cost surrogate; selected layouts require full-runtime validation and may fall back to a conservative feasible plan (§3.6).
- **Analyst inference—limited correctness coverage:** Dense correctness evidence covers one model and a short training run. MoE results report throughput without an equivalent loss comparison (§4.7).
- **Analyst inference—policy attribution:** Table 3 changes chunks and activation layouts together, limiting attribution of gains to individual policy components (§4.6).
- **Analyst inference—measurement uncertainty:** Batch-scaling points normally average two runs over short steady-state windows; the supplied text gives no confidence intervals (§4.1).
- **Analyst inference—deployment cost:** The reported selector timing reuses cached activation profiling, leaving cold-start selection cost unavailable (§4.6).

## Open research questions
- **Does numerical agreement persist through longer training and MoE routing?** The correctness study is limited to one dense-model run. Next experiment: matched long-duration dense and MoE runs against FSDP2, comparing loss and final task metrics.
- **How robust is policy selection across changing workloads?** Profiles are reusable only with matching hardware, workload, and implementation identities (§3.6). Next experiment: vary sequence lengths and batch composition during training and measure policy degradation and retuning cost.
- **Which policy components deliver the gains?** The main policy ablation combines chunk and activation changes. Next experiment: factorial ablations of chunking, activation retention, and checkpoint granularity at matched routes and memory budgets.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the summary of the supplied text; empirical claims remain author-reported.

Material missing information includes verified repository contents and exact software revisions, confidence intervals, cold-start selector cost, training data for throughput runs, longer-term convergence results, MoE correctness comparisons, and multi-node evaluation.
