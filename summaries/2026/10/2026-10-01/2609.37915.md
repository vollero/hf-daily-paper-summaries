# Overcoming Scaling Limits in On-Policy Self-Distillation for LLM Reasoning

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-01.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.37915) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37915)


## One-sentence takeaway
OASIS replaces written teacher references with self-generated attempts and concentrates distillation on answer-verified trajectories, improving mean competition-math Avg@12 over OPSD across three tested Qwen3 sizes at substantially higher generation cost (Table 2; Figure 6).

## Problem and motivation
Standard on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) supervises student-generated trajectories using a teacher that additionally sees a worked solution. The authors identify two problems: this requires written solutions, and supervision often falls on unsuccessful trajectories where the teacher benefits from information unavailable to the student.

Their central question is whether **where supervision is applied** matters more than the teacher’s reference context. They find that privileged references help continuation recovery much more on failed prefixes than on verified ones, while OPSD’s downstream gains diminish across the tested model sizes (§§1, 3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Separate scaffold from context.** Signal-replay and truncated-continuation probes distinguish the trajectory receiving supervision from the information conditioning the teacher (§3; Appendix A.2).
- **Analyze two objective-level issues.** Proposition 1 derives a context-mixture optimum and residual information-dependent loss for unclipped forward KL. Proposition 2 shows that per-entry clipping can reverse a clipped token’s logit gradient; this does not necessarily decrease its probability (§3.5; Appendices A.1–A.3).
- **Introduce OASIS.** Sample multiple on-policy attempts, select the shortest answer-verified trajectory as the scaffold, and condition the frozen teacher on a distinct same-problem attempt—preferably the shortest unverified one. Retain OPSD’s clipped token-level objective and require final-answer labels rather than worked solutions. Problems without verified attempts are masked, with a rare unverified fallback (§4; Appendix A.5).
- **Evaluate performance and trade-offs.** Compare three Qwen3 sizes on competition mathematics and test scaffold length and rollout count in limited ablations (§5; Appendix A.4).

These results establish an empirical advantage under the reported training settings, not a general scaling law or compute-efficiency advantage. The authors explicitly consider the small 1.7B difference inconclusive by itself (§5.2). Repeated evaluation measures sampling variability, not variability across independent training runs. The recovery probes support the proposed mechanism but do not directly measure the conditional mutual information in Proposition 1.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** competition mathematics, one model family, higher generation cost, and single training runs at 4B and 8B (§6).
- **Author-stated theoretical caveat:** Proposition 1 concerns unclipped KL and inaccessible context information; it is not an exact characterization of implemented OPSD. References are deterministically associated with problems, so a sufficiently capable student could potentially infer their information (Appendix A.1).
- **Analyst inference—budget confounding:** Table 2 matches supervised-example budgets rather than generation compute. Table 4 expands baseline training but also uses a different configuration; neither comparison establishes superiority at matched wall-clock cost.
- **Analyst inference—mechanism isolation:** the supplied text announces a factorial analysis but does not show a complete trained scaffold-by-context factorial comparison. Context evidence is primarily diagnostic; reported training ablations cover scaffold length and rollout count only at 1.7B on AIME24.
- **Reporting ambiguities:** Table 1 specifies cut fractions from 0.1 through 0.9, whereas Appendix A.2 lists five fractions. Table 5 reports fully verified selected OASIS scaffolds, while Appendix A.5 documents unverified fallbacks. These inconsistencies limit exact interpretation.
- **Verification boundary:** answer verification does not establish intermediate reasoning correctness, and the authors acknowledge verifier imperfections (§4.3).

## Open research questions
- **Does OASIS retain its advantage at matched compute?** Generation budgets differ substantially. Next experiment: compare methods at equal generated tokens or GPU-hours, including independent training seeds.
- **Which scaffold/context change causes the gains?** Full training-level factorial evidence is unavailable. Next experiment: cross verified versus unverified scaffolds with gold versus self-generated contexts while holding other settings fixed.
- **Can changing clipping reduce late-training drift?** The paper provides a gradient analysis and observations, but no training ablation of clipping alternatives. Next experiment: compare larger thresholds and per-token clipping under a shared evaluation protocol.
- **Does the method transfer beyond mathematics?** Only one domain and model family are tested. Next experiment: evaluate answer-verifiable code tasks with unit tests and another model family, as suggested in §6.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the reported method and benchmark comparisons, but broad scaling and causal interpretations remain limited.

Material missing information includes independent training-seed results, matched-compute comparisons, wall-clock costs, verifier error rates, a complete trained factorial analysis, an explicit checkpoint-selection criterion, and an OASIS code or checkpoint release location.
