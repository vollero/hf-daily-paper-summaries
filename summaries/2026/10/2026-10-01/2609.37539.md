# SkillGym: Training Skill-Use Agents with Automatic Verifiable Environment Generation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-01.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.37539) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37539)


## One-sentence takeaway
SkillGym converts public skill packages into executable training tasks whose verified trajectories improve agent performance and skill consultation, with weaker gains on tasks requiring domain methods or bundled tools.

## Problem and motivation
External skills provide agents with knowledge, procedures, and scripts, but using them requires discovering relevant guidance and translating it into actions. The authors identify a training-data gap: community skills generally lack accompanying tasks, environments, and success checks, while prior training approaches concentrate on skills derived from experience in limited environments (§1).

The challenge is to construct tasks where skill-specific knowledge provides a meaningful advantage and verifiers accept valid alternative solutions while rejecting plausible but incorrect outputs.

## Approach and contributions
- **Skill-driven environment generation:** Curate complete English-language skill packages that support offline, noninteractive execution without GPUs; build dependency environments through builder–reviewer iteration (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Structured task construction:** Generate procedural, abductive, constraint-satisfaction, and partial-order tasks. Each package includes instructions, an initial workspace, a reference solution, and an executable verifier. Execution checks require the initial state to fail and the reference solution to pass; reviewers inspect leakage and overly strict or weak checks (§3.2).
- **Training assets and evidence:** Report 6,772 tasks associated with 3,494 skills and 19,070 successful trajectories, followed by supervised finetuning across six backbones. Analyses examine unseen-skill transfer, consultation behavior, task structures, and reviewer approval (§§4–5; Tables 7–9).

The distinctive combination is external-skill-driven synthesis, explicit reasoning profiles, and verifier quality review; skill-driven synthesis itself already appears in the paper’s SKT comparison (§2).

These results support better consultation and task performance within the evaluated settings, including transfer to excluded skills. They do not establish reliable execution of all skill methods, universal skill necessity, or superior performance from reasoning-profile diversity. The larger-model comparison is also benchmark-specific, and the small SkillEval difference has overlapping reported standard deviations.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated limits:** Gains on method- and tool-centric SkillsBench tasks are weak. Training uses SFT only; reinforcement learning remains future work (§6).
- **Authors’ ablation caveat:** Small-subset models often exhaust output budgets through repeated reasoning and underperform the base model on SkillEval. The authors recommend interpreting ablations within matched pairs (§5.3).
- **Analyst inference—restricted transfer evidence:** English, offline, noninteractive skill selection and Bash-only evaluation limit conclusions about online services, interactive workflows, other languages, and deployment harnesses (Table 4; §4.1).
- **Analyst inference—verification remains imperfectly established:** Automated reviewer checks provide stronger evidence than reference-solution execution alone, but no independent task-verifier error audit is reported. Figure 6 labels some tasks’ skills “not essential.”
- **Reporting inconsistencies:** Table 8’s total excludes partial-order construction runs despite the headline four-profile description, and includes unresolved reviewer cases. Table 9 lists three harnesses, while §3.3 names four. These leave the exact released and training-data composition unclear.
- **Comparison and analysis limits:** SKT scores come from another paper using a different harness (§4.2). Structural labels rely on an LLM annotator; the reported check-count validation does not establish accuracy of every semantic label (Appendix G).

## Open research questions
- **How can training improve method execution beyond consultation?** Compliance and method/tool transfer remain weak. A next experiment could add verified method- and tool-centric trajectories while matching training tokens and task difficulty.
- **Does profile diversity help at larger training scales?** The small matched-budget ablation shows no average advantage. Repeat it across larger budgets, evaluating each reasoning structure separately.
- **Can verifier-based reinforcement learning improve outcomes?** Only SFT is tested. Compare SFT with SFT-plus-RL under matched compute, including audits for verifier exploitation.
- **How reliably do generated verifiers grade alternative solutions?** Reviewer approval helps, but independent grading accuracy is unavailable. Audit sampled tasks using human review and deliberately varied correct and incorrect solutions.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** Main performance and consultation findings have direct table/figure support, but dataset accounting and some evaluation details remain unclear.

Material missing information includes independent verifier-error rates; exact partial-order and unresolved-task inclusion in training; OpenCode trajectory counts; human validation of structural labels; hardware underlying the reported training GPU hours; and full construction costs, which exclude environment building, annotation, and evaluation.
