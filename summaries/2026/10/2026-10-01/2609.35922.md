# Almost Human, Except When It Matters: VoxParity and the Decisions a Voice Should Change

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-01.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.35922) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35922)


## One-sentence takeaway
VoxParity finds that only 11 of 23 tested voice systems pass its words-only null test, while exploratory analyses suggest that leading systems often recognize actionable audio cues without choosing the required protective action (§§6–7).

## Problem and motivation
Voice agents execute transactions and service decisions where vocal delivery, background sounds, or another speaker can change the appropriate action despite unchanged caller words. The author identifies a gap between measuring cue recognition and measuring whether those cues change executable tool calls beyond what a transcript-only pipeline explains (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Counterfactual action benchmark:** 183 scenarios across 14 sectors hold caller transcripts fixed while changing audio and the correct typed tool call. Seven cue types cover delivery, sarcasm, second voices, environmental sounds, masked words, speaker age, and disfluency/silence; scoring deterministically matches tools and arguments (§2).
- **Words-only null test:** Compare each system’s audio-minus-exact-transcript credit with the same change for a Whisper-large-v3-turbo → gpt-oss-120b cascade. Positive differences must survive Holm correction; comparisons use identical cells and item-clustered bootstraps (§§3.1, 3.4).
- **Failure analysis and intervention probes:** Evaluate 28 systems, collect volunteer action selections, and explore error direction, recognition versus action, stated policies, and supplied descriptions of delivery. These analyses are exploratory rather than confirmatory (§§3–7, 11).
- **Reusable evaluation infrastructure:** The author reports releasing agent drivers, a deterministic scorer, regeneration scripts, and a public development split; most items remain held out (§12).

## Evidence and main results
All intervals below are reported 95% intervals.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Audio-dependent action is uneven | On 206 cue-bearing cells, 11/23 systems with transcript paths pass the Holm-corrected null test; 1/7 eligible production realtime agents passes. | §6.2; Figure 3; Table A2 |
| Errors favor routine execution | Every tested system has higher unsafe execution on protective calls than over-triggering on clean calls; pooled across 28 systems: 41% [36%, 46%] versus 12% [9%, 15%]. | §4.1; Figure A4 |
| Hearing reduces some unsafe actions | Across 23 systems with transcript paths, audio reduces unsafe execution relative to their own transcripts by 0.12 [0.09, 0.15]; over-triggering changes by +0.00 [−0.01, +0.01]. | §4.3 |
| Recognition can coexist with failure | All four leading systems identify the medical monitor behind a utility-disconnection request; three schedule disconnection and none applies the prescribed vulnerability hold. | §4.2; Table 3 |
| Stated rules correlate with emotional-cue gains | For the four leading systems, gain over the null on emotional cells is +0.33 [+0.19, +0.49] with stated rules, versus +0.00 [−0.11, +0.11] without them; this split is observational. | §5.1 |
| Heard quiet states are frequently overruled | Leading systems take the words’ action on 52% [40%, 64%] of heard quiet-state protective calls, versus 6% [1%, 12%] of heard acute-alarm calls; grouping is post hoc. | §5.2 |
| Descriptions and rules recover part of the gap | On emotional cells whose rules were initially implicit, gemini-3.7-flash’s own-audio credit rises from 0.40 with neither intervention to 0.83 [0.75, 0.90] with both; other-cue credit reaches 1.00. | §5.4; Appendix A.9 |
| Oracle descriptions expose decision headroom | With specification-derived descriptions and exact transcripts, text-only gpt-oss-120b reaches 0.76 [0.70, 0.81] cue-bearing credit, versus at most 0.57 for unaided audio systems. | §7.2 |

The confirmatory evidence establishes that some systems’ actions respond to audio beyond the chosen transcript-only null on this bank. Passing does not establish safe deployment or consistently correct paired decisions. Recognition probes and intervention results support the author’s perception-to-decision diagnosis, but do not establish an internal causal mechanism or a deployable remedy.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Stimulus generalization:** Emotion results rely largely on one synthetic voice; human recordings come from the author, who knew the hypothesis. Broader speaker, accent, and natural-affect generalization is untested (§11).
- **Cue admission:** Most scored clips lacked human ratings and entered through a Gemini cue judge with poor reported agreement with listeners. Independent-admission checks retain key patterns but do not remove the construction risk (§2.3; Appendix A.3).
- **Exploratory selection and confounding:** Only null-test verdicts are confirmatory. Emotion groupings and failure decompositions are post hoc; stated-rule availability and grounding strength confound cue-type comparisons (§11).
- **Human reference:** Volunteers were self-selected, had different instructions and policy access, and contributed unevenly. Human parity is not established (§§3.3, 7.3, 11).
- **Null-test assumption:** The test assumes comparable audio-minus-transcript changes for the tested system and cascade when cues are unused. Voxtral Small’s pass largely reflects increased tool-call frequency and lacks stricter paired-action corroboration (§3.1; Appendix A.5).
- **Intervention validity:** Supplied descriptions encode intended rendering rather than independently verified perception; added rules derive from grounding that describes intended responses. Some description comparisons also change serving route (Appendix A.9).
- **Scoring and reproducibility:** First-call scoring misses cases where a correct protective action is followed by unsafe execution. Most items are held out, limiting direct public replication (Appendix A.1; §12).

## Open research questions
1. **Do the failures persist across natural speakers?** Current affect coverage is narrow (§11). Next experiment: independently validate fixed-word recordings from diverse speakers and repeat paired action evaluations.
2. **Can automatically inferred descriptions recover oracle-note gains?** Supplied notes come from specifications, while the tested model’s own descriptions often understate cues (§§5.3–5.4). Next experiment: compare a calibrated audio-description stage with oracle, sham, and direct-audio conditions.
3. **Does counterfactual training improve protective decisions without increasing over-triggering?** Training is proposed but untested (§9). Next experiment: train on newly generated pairs and evaluate on untouched items, reporting both error directions and paired correctness.
4. **Does the null test predict reliable action sequences?** It measures decision moments, and first-call scoring can conceal subsequent unsafe actions (Appendix A.1). Next experiment: score every action in multi-turn versions of the same contrasts.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text thoroughly documents the benchmark and reported comparisons, but broader validity is constrained by stimulus construction and exploratory analyses.

Unavailable here: actual audio and item-level run artifacts; the complete held-out scenarios; independent replication; representative natural-call evaluation; and deployable-description or training results.
