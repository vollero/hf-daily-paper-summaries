# Prompted Identity Degrades Cooperation in Multi-Agent LLM Systems

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-01.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.35928) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35928)


## One-sentence takeaway
In the tested heterogeneous LLM groups, visible model-family or color labels induced label-aligned factions, while truthful family labels increased coordination costs and reduced success relative to anonymized agents (§4; Tables 1–5).

## Problem and motivation
Model identities can reach agents through prompts, names, and message headers in heterogeneous multi-agent systems. The authors ask whether this incidental metadata changes cooperation despite a shared objective and no explicit incentive to favor same-family peers. They identify a gap in evaluations that measure aggregate performance or imposed communication structures without isolating identity metadata as a source of emergent factions (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Definition and measurement:** Define factionalism as both same-label homophily and label-aligned communities. Measure agreement from recipient choices and next-round commitments using Edge Density Ratio (EDR), Louvain communities, and Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI); require four corrected statistical tests to pass (§3.1; Appendix B).
- **Controlled identity intervention:** Compare unlabeled, truthful-label, and shuffled-label conditions, then repeat with neutral colors. Test balanced and unbalanced groups in Leader Election, Exclusion, and GPQA-Diamond (§§3.2–3.3, 4.3).
- **Costs and explanatory probes:** Measure success, rounds, generated tokens, and tokens per successful outcome. Post-hoc log-probability probes distinguish architectural writing similarity from label-associated vote adoption; additional checks remove Gemma and match sampling parameters (§§4.2–4.4; Appendices A, F–G).

## Evidence and main results

Numerical uncertainties below are reported standard errors.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Visible family labels induce factionalism across the main games | All 24 labeled/mislabeled configurations pass the factionalism rule; none of 12 unlabeled configurations does. Leader Election and Exclusion, balanced/unbalanced rosters, 9/12/25 agents, 50 runs per configuration. | §4; Table 1 |
| Shuffled identities steer communities toward announced labels | Balanced 12-agent Exclusion: visible-label AMI **0.58 ± 0.03**, versus true-family AMI **0.03 ± 0.02**, from the same mislabeled trajectories. | Tables 1, 3 |
| Arbitrary colors also produce factions | Balanced 12-agent Exclusion with shuffled colors: visible-label AMI **0.75 ± 0.03**, true-family AMI **−0.01 ± 0.02**. All color-labeled configurations satisfy the criterion. | §4.3; Table 4 |
| Truthful labels raise Leader Election costs | At 12 agents, labeled/unlabeled ratios adjusted for roster balance: rounds **1.46 ± 0.10**, generated tokens **1.82 ± 0.16**, success probability **0.86 ± 0.04**. | Table 2; Appendix F |
| Exclusion success suffers substantially | At 12 agents, adjusted labeled/unlabeled success ratio **0.61 ± 0.08**; tokens per successful outcome ratio **2.22 ± 0.32**, pooled across roster balance. | Table 17 |
| Larger Exclusion groups largely fail regardless of labeling | At 25 agents and a 30-round horizon, no labeled or mislabeled run succeeds; **8/100** unlabeled runs succeed. Rounds and success ratios are not estimated. | §4.2; Appendix F |
| Costs extend to externally scored questions | GPQA-Diamond, 12 agents: adjusted labeled/unlabeled ratios of **1.15 ± 0.03** for rounds, **1.09 ± 0.03** for tokens, and **0.89 ± 0.04** for success. Success requires electing a leader with the correct answer. | Table 2; Appendix E |
| The pattern survives additional controls | Gemma-free rosters reproduce graph-level factionalism; matched sampling reproduces label effects in balanced 12-agent games. Adoption probes align more with visible labels, while stylistic similarity tracks true architecture. | Tables 13–16, 19–20; §4.4 |

The interventions provide strong evidence that visible categorical metadata drives factional structure within these protocols. They also establish worse measured outcomes under truthful labels. They do **not** isolate factionalism as the sole mediator of those costs, prove a complete internal mechanism, or establish generalization to arbitrary agent workflows. GPQA success combines answer correctness with successful leader election, so its decline cannot be read as a pure reasoning-accuracy decline.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Closed models, larger groups, other decoding regimes, and richer workflows remain untested. GPQA retains the Leader Election protocol, limiting evidence for other externally evaluated tasks (§5).
- **Analyst inference—measurement:** Next-round vote matching is an agreement proxy, not proof that a particular message caused adoption. Absent contacts contribute zero to EDR densities, so contact selection also affects the statistic (§3.1; Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference—statistical scope:** Holm–Bonferroni correction covers four tests within each experiment, not all experiments or outcome measures (Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference—“no-stakes” interpretation:** The Leader Election prompt assigns the winner exclusive human communication authority, while Exclusion removes participants. Although there are no individual rewards, these descriptions could suggest instrumental reasons to prefer group representation; alternative prompt wording is untested (Appendix H).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** §4 says 50 runs for each reported configuration, whereas Appendix E specifies one run for each of 198 GPQA questions per experiment. The relationship between these statements is unclear.

## Open research questions
- **Does anonymization help richer workflows?** These remain untested (§5). Compare labeled and anonymized teams on coding or tool-based tasks with external outcome scoring.
- **Can capability descriptors recreate factions?** The authors recommend task-relevant descriptors without testing them (§1). Compare anonymity, accurate capability profiles, and arbitrary labels under the same protocols.
- **How much GPQA loss comes from failed elections versus wrong answers?** Reported success combines both (Appendix E). Separately measure election failures and conditional answer accuracy, then test an alternative aggregation rule.
- **How sensitive is factionalism to communication and prompt design?** The study uses fan-out two and fixed game prompts (§3.3; Appendix H). Vary fan-out, broadcast access, and leadership/removal wording while holding label assignments fixed.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for label-induced factionalism and coordination costs in the tested settings; broader applicability remains uncertain.

Material information unavailable or unclear in the supplied text: a code or full-trace release, exact endpoint defaults for response limits, reconciliation of GPQA run counts, separate aggregate GPQA election-failure and wrong-answer rates, and the aggregation underlying the abstract’s headline averages.
