# A2Z GameSpec-Bench: How Faithfully Can Coding Agents Generate Games from Game Design Specifications?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-01.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.39564) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39564)


## One-sentence takeaway
A2Z GameSpec-Bench shows that runnable agent-generated games can miss interconnected design requirements, while contract-based source, replay, and playtest feedback improves fidelity over self-revision in the tested setting.

## Problem and motivation
The paper evaluates whether coding agents preserve detailed game designs across implementation, rendered output, and player interactions. The authors argue that compact specifications and isolated evaluation channels in existing benchmarks inadequately capture dependencies in long-form Game Design Documents (GDDs): code can implement a requirement locally while gameplay bypasses its required transition (§1, Figure 2, Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Benchmark corpus:** 100 synthetic, validated long-form GDDs, divided equally into compact-scope **Small** and broader-scope **Big** designs. An agentic brief-to-design pipeline checks completeness, consistency, behavioral soundness, and formulas (§3.1, Appendix A).
- **Fixed dependency-aware contracts:** GDD requirements become source-linked rules, invariants, and prerequisite edges before builds are inspected. Contracts remain fixed across agents and revision rounds (§3.2, Appendix B).
- **Three-channel evaluation:** Source inspection, scenario replay with selected visual evidence, and adaptive code-as-policy playtests judge the same requirements. Normal play tests ordinary paths; adversarial tests initialize prerequisites for previously unverified rules (§3.3).
- **Requirement-level diagnosis and revision:** Code, frames, and execution traces provide targeted feedback. Overall GDD Fidelity equally averages the three evaluation axes (§3.4).

The contribution is this integrated benchmark and evaluation structure; code-as-policy testing and parts of the replay scoring protocol are explicitly adapted from prior work (§§3.3, D.2).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| High verifiability coexists with incomplete fidelity | Across 100 GDDs, Claude-Fable-5.1 leads with **77.0/100 GDD Fidelity**, versus **98.7% verifiable rate** in each split; verifiable rate averages compile/runtime passes across axes. Each configuration generates three builds per GDD. | Table 2; §§4.1–4.2 |
| Broader designs are harder | Across nine configurations, mean Fidelity is **71.1 Small versus 51.1 Big**, while verifiable rates are **93.9% versus 94.2%**. Splits contain 50 GDDs each. | §4.2 |
| Local implementation passes do not establish dependency reachability | On 100 GPT-5.6-Sol games, dependency-linked rules average **72.4% local pass versus 22.7% dependency-adjusted pass**, requiring every predecessor to pass at a source-score threshold of 0.8. This is a graph proxy. | Figure 2c; Appendix B.7 |
| Dependencies improve inspection coverage | With enumerated judgments fixed, coverage of **560 recorded violated targets** rises from **398/560 (71.1%) to 449/560 (80.2%)**. Gain: **9.1 percentage points**, 95% paired-game bootstrap interval **6.1–13.0**; one initial GPT-5.6-Sol build per GDD. | Table 3; §4.4; Appendix G.1.2 |
| Targeted feedback improves revision | On 50 Big GDDs, matched initial builds and two GPT-5.6-Sol revision rounds yield **74.3** Fidelity with three-axis feedback versus **67.0** with self-revision: **7.3 points / 10.9% relative**. | Figure 6; §4.5 |
| Adaptive frames better support rationales | On 50 Small GPT-5.6-Sol games with matched recordings and frame budgets, adaptive selection wins **60.5% of 1,159 comparisons favoring either method**, versus **39.5%** for fixed sampling. Audit measures rationale support, not reference-label accuracy. | Table 4b; Appendix G.3 |
| Adversarial testing extends conclusive evidence | On 86 target rules from 50 games, coverage rises from **40.5%** after normal play to **48.5%** with an adversarial second pass, versus **41.0%** with another normal pass under matched budgets. | Table S13; §4.7 |

These results support complementary evaluation and targeted revision within the benchmark. They do not establish complete gameplay reachability, human-validated evaluator accuracy, or generalization beyond the tested settings. In particular, unverified playtest rules score zero, so playtest scores reflect both satisfaction and evidence coverage (§3.3.3).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** The main corpus is synthetic and restricted to Phaser 2D single-player games. Generalization to human-authored specifications, other engines, and broader software tasks remains unestablished; three Three.js examples provide limited extension evidence (Appendix J).
- **Author-stated:** Generative-model contract construction and evidence interpretation can retain omissions and evaluator bias despite frozen contracts and repeated judgments (Appendix J).
- **Author clarification:** Dependency edges encode potential prerequisites; shared attributes need not imply compatible values or actual runtime blocking (Appendices B.6–B.7).
- **Analyst inference:** Assisted adversarial satisfaction does not establish that ordinary gameplay reaches the tested situation. The protocol explicitly separates prerequisite initialization from normal play (§3.3.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Agreement with model reference judgments establishes consistency rather than independent correctness. The reported reliability comparisons use model-generated references (§4.6, Appendix G.2).

## Open research questions

- **Does the framework transfer to human-authored GDDs?** Synthetic-document generalization remains open (Appendix J). Next experiment: evaluate independently authored studio specifications with expert-reviewed contracts.
- **How accurate are the contracts and verdicts?** Model agreement cannot eliminate shared omissions or bias (§4.6, Appendix J). Next experiment: compare sampled requirements and execution verdicts against independent human adjudication.
- **How much assisted satisfaction is naturally reachable?** Adversarial testing increases coverage by supplying prerequisites (§4.7). Next experiment: pair assisted tests with longer normal-play searches and report reachability separately.
- **Do revision gains generalize across agents and engines?** The controlled revision study uses GPT-5.6-Sol on Big Phaser designs (§4.5). Next experiment: repeat matched-budget feedback comparisons across additional agents and runtimes.
