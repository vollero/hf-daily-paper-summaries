# OSWorld-Science: A Benchmark of Computer Use Agents for Learning and Using Scientific Software

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-01.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.39903) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39903)


## One-sentence takeaway
OSWorld-Science evaluates computer-use agents on scientific software workflows and reports a best mean partial-credit score of 73.7%, while trajectory analyses expose failures in interface control, artifact delivery, and independent validation (§4; Fig. 9).

## Problem and motivation
Scientific software requires agents to interpret specialized interfaces, maintain workflow state, manipulate scientific objects, and deliver quantitatively valid artifacts. The authors identify gaps in existing evaluations: tasks grounded in scientists’ judgments of importance and difficulty, grading across heterogeneous scientific outputs, and explicit analysis of how the agent harness affects performance (§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Benchmark construction:** Expert proposals and iterative human–AI task design produce 146 tasks covering scientific analysis, simulation, annotation, and molecular workflows (§3–4).
- **Artifact-based evaluation:** Task-specific execution graders inspect application state and submitted files, using exact matches, numerical tolerances, partial credit, and—in OpenFOAM—restart and shortcut checks (§3; Appendix A; §F.3).
- **Harness and empirical analysis:** An adapted OSWorld agent harness supports model adapters, GUI/terminal actions, logging, and reliability controls. The paper compares 12 VLMs and examines reasoning effort, screenshot history, prompt language, and failure trajectories (§3–4).

The contribution is an evaluation framework and associated findings; the supplied text does not introduce a newly trained model.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Best reported overall score remains below full completion | Across 12 VLMs, Claude Fable 5.1 scores 73.7%, versus Opus 5 at 57.9% and GPT-6 Astra at 57.4%. These are task-weighted mean scores with partial credit and VOID scored zero, not binary completion rates. | §4; Appendix B; Fig. 9 |
| Failures frequently lack a deliverable | Of 1,530 released runs, 402 receive full credit. Among 1,100 remaining runs after excluding harness-ended/unresolved cases, 667 lack a deliverable; 613 of those exhaust their budget. | §4; §D.1; Table 8 |
| Apparent interaction-style advantages do not survive controls | Full-credit runs have a median 11-percentage-point higher CLI share across matched tasks, but none of five interaction measures predicts full credit after model/task fixed effects and Bonferroni correction. | §4; Fig. 4; §D.1, p. 26 |
| More reasoning has uneven descriptive benefits | On 23 QuPath tasks, one run per task/configuration: Opus 5 rises from 44.1% at medium effort to 59.7% at max, with mean output tokens increasing from 13.8k to 59.3k. Sonnet 5 peaks at 22.0% at high effort and scores 18.3% at max. | Table 2; Fig. 5 |
| Longer history can shorten trajectories, but gains are uncertain | For Opus 5 at medium effort on QuPath, increasing history from one to ten screenshots changes mean score from 35.2% to 57.0%, steps from 52 to 22, and output tokens from 24.7k to 10.6k. No non-default ablation cell differs significantly from its default after correction. | Fig. 5; Tables 2, 6 |
| Statistics scores often reflect terminal work | Across 240 statistics runs, 214 operate none of the four tracked applications; SAS Studio or RStudio is used in only 10 runs. Application-mode use is associated with a within-task score difference of −0.139 and 29 additional steps; the authors explicitly reject causal interpretation. | §D.3; Tables 14, 16 |
| Simulation success includes delivery and precision | ANSYS yields 66 full successes in 168 runs and a 40.0% mean partial-credit score; OpenFOAM yields 77 in 168 and 47.3%. An OpenFOAM case with a correct solve scores 0.125 after a six-digit export fails precision/restart gates. | §F.2–F.3; Tables 23, 25; Fig. 26 |

These results establish performance under the reported tasks, graders, budgets, and harness conditions. They do not establish general scientific competence or causal benefits from CLI use, longer history, or more reasoning. Selected case studies support the authors’ argument that self-consistency checks can preserve an original error, but do not quantify the benefit of an independent-validation intervention (§4; Fig. 6).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Coverage limitation:** The supplied source is truncated in the middle; this analysis does not cover the complete paper.
- **Authors’ stated sampling limits:** Ablations and detailed domain analyses use one run per task/configuration. Domain analyses lack variance estimates, and an earlier ANSYS batch flips two outcomes, illustrating run variability (Fig. 5; §D.2; §F.2–F.3).
- **Uneven coverage:** Task counts are imbalanced; the authors recommend aggregate and domain-macro reporting. Released cross-domain trajectory analysis excludes biology (§4; §D.1).
- **Harness comparability:** Appendix A exempts Kimi-K3 from the shared mmagent design. OpenFOAM additionally documents different action feedback for Kimi-K3 and screenshot scaling for Qwen-CUA (§F.3).
- **Environment and grading defects:** Some tasks name absent software; biology runs encounter installation, endpoint, service, and evaluator faults. Statistics checks accept inadequate reusable-program submissions, and an ANSYS success uses a prohibited analytical alternative (§D.1; §D.3; §D.6; §F.2).
- **Analyst inference—construct validity:** When terminal alternatives satisfy grading, high scores may measure artifact production more strongly than operation of the named application; the statistics analysis directly supports this concern (§D.3).
- **Analyst inference—reporting consistency:** Main-text API costs differ from Fig. 8—for example, Kimi K3 is reported at $11.7 versus $10.09. Domain labels and counts also shift between task-design, inventory, and evaluation descriptions. These discrepancies prevent a single fully reconciled summary (§3–4; Appendix B).

## Open research questions
1. **Do harness improvements generalize across software?** Configuration sweeps cover only QuPath and have no corrected significant differences. A next experiment could repeat history and reasoning sweeps across domains with multiple runs per cell (Fig. 5).
2. **How much performance is lost to coordinate conversion?** Several domain analyses attribute failures to screenshot/action frame mismatches. A controlled rerun with explicit coordinate adapters could separate interface calibration from scientific reasoning (§D.2; §D.6; §F.2).
3. **Can independent validation improve scientific correctness?** Selected cases contrast source-grounded checks with checks derived from the same mistaken assumption. Randomly assigning independent-reference validation could measure its effect on correctness and cost (Figs. 6, 24–26).
4. **Can grading enforce actual software use and valid programs?** Documented shortcuts and weak source checks leave this unresolved. A next experiment could require application provenance and execute submitted routines against unseen inputs (§D.3; §F.2).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the benchmark design and reported findings, but truncation and inconsistencies limit interpretation.

Material unavailable or unresolved information includes the omitted middle material, complete task-specific grading rules, reconciled model/task denominators and API costs, repeated-run uncertainty for overall rankings, and independently verified accessibility of the claimed releases.
