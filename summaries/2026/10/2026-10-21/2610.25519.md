# ATEST: Adaptive Resource-Aware Test-Time Scaling for LLM Inference

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-21.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.25519) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25519)


## One-sentence takeaway
ATEST combines answer-vote stability with online GPU-energy prediction to adapt sampling under changing load, achieving full query coverage within the tested energy budgets while trading away some latency and throughput.

## Problem and motivation
Adaptive test-time scaling usually allocates samples from reasoning signals, while GPU-serving systems manage execution resources. The authors identify a gap between these decisions: whether another sample is useful also depends on its current execution cost (§II-C).

Their measurements show that shared-GPU load substantially changes energy cost even with a fixed sample count (§III, Fig. 3). Ignoring this variation can exhaust a shared energy budget or force query deferrals, which the paper counts as incorrect answers (§IV).

## Approach and contributions
- **ATEST-Online:** Starts with one sample and incrementally follows the budget ladder `{1,3,4,6,8}`, retaining previous samples. Monte Carlo look-ahead estimates whether the current plurality answer will remain the winner at the maximum budget. Recursive least squares learns step-energy costs from measured execution and GPU state; sampling continues when estimated stability gain per joule clears a threshold and predicted energy fits the remaining budget (§V-B, Algorithm 1).
- **ATEST-Calibrated:** Uses offline accuracy and cost models plus live GPU state to select the entire sample budget before generation. This permits one-batch execution but requires dataset-specific profiling and leaves the cost model fixed (§V-C).
- **Systems evidence:** The contribution is the coupling of reasoning allocation with runtime resource feedback, supported by changing-load measurements, an ablation separating load adaptation from budget depletion, and comparisons identifying when offline calibration remains useful (§§III, VII-D–VII-G).

## Evidence and main results

All results below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed sampling becomes more expensive under load | On A100 with `B=4`, high-load energy per answered query is 3.7× idle on GSM8K and MATH, and 4.1× on ARC-Challenge. | §III, Fig. 3 |
| Online mode maintains coverage and budget compliance | Phi-3-mini/A100, 500 queries per dataset across idle→high load: zero deferrals and 92–100% budget use; accuracy is 62.6% GSM8K, 42.6% MATH, 79.4% ARC-Challenge, and 71.6% CommonsenseQA. | §VII-A; Table III discussion |
| High-load advantage includes avoiding deferrals | GSM8K, 125 high-load queries: Online versus Calibrated gives 60.0% versus 44.0% accuracy, 0% versus 42.4% deferrals, and 175.8 versus 206.4 J/query. Both use `B=1` among answered queries. | §VII-B, Fig. 4 |
| Cost learning drives load response | With a non-binding budget and fixed remaining-energy signal, ReasoningOnly-Strict uses mean `B=3.12` at every load on all datasets; enabling online cost learning restores load-dependent budgets even without GPU-state inputs. | §VII-D; Table V discussion |
| Profiling dominates the reported end-to-end comparison | Across four datasets and 2,000 served queries, calibration requires 5.73 GPU-hours, 7.16 MJ, and 176,000 samples. Including profiling, Calibrated uses approximately 42× Online’s GPU time and 48× its energy. | §VII-C; Table IV discussion |
| Online control has execution costs | Online versus Calibrated: GSM8K p95 latency is 1.08 versus 0.51 s; CommonsenseQA throughput is 16.6 versus 29.7 QPS under the load schedule. | §VII-E; Table VI discussion |
| Stable load can favor calibration | Separate no-injected-load GSM8K run, 500 queries: Calibrated accuracy is 68.6%, versus Online’s 63.2%. | §VII-F; Table VIII discussion |
| Behavior extends to additional tested settings | L40S experiments with Phi-3-mini and Qwen2.5-3B: Online answers every query across eight dataset/model settings, uses 92–99.5% of budget, and lowers joules/correct versus Fixed-4 in seven settings. | §VII-G; Table VII discussion |

The evidence supports resource-aware sampling under this controlled workload and accounting scheme. It does not establish universal budget safety or superiority: most overall accuracy gaps are described by the authors as ties, calibration can win under stable load, and its profiling cost can be amortized over longer deployments (§§VII-A, VII-C, VII-F).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Evaluation uses four datasets, two open models, two GPU types, and 500 test queries per dataset. Stepwise execution adds overhead, and short-task energy predictions remain noisy (§IX).
- **Author-reported comparison caveat:** Identical injector settings produce different sampled GPU utilization for some short-task methods; the authors therefore emphasize GSM8K and MATH for high-load comparisons (§VII-B).
- **Analyst inference—external validity:** A fixed idle-to-high schedule with dense matrix-multiply interference does not establish behavior under arbitrary production co-tenants or abrupt, reversing load changes (§VI-D).
- **Analyst inference—coverage versus reasoning:** Accuracy counts deferrals as wrong, so gains combine service coverage and answer quality; they do not isolate reasoning improvements among answered queries (§VI-F).
- **Analyst inference—budget guarantees:** Conservative predictions prevented overspending experimentally, but prediction-based admission and stopping do not constitute a proved hard-budget guarantee (§V-B).
- **Comparison breadth:** Named reasoning-only adaptive methods are not directly evaluated; the paper substitutes its ReasoningOnly-Strict ablation (§VI-C).

## Open research questions
1. **Does adaptation remain reliable under abrupt or heterogeneous interference?** The study uses a fixed load progression. A next experiment could replay randomized load reversals and different co-tenant workloads, measuring transient prediction error, coverage, and overspending.
2. **Can stepwise control retain its benefits with lower serving overhead?** Short tasks lose throughput (§VII-E). Test cross-query batching and overlapping controller decisions with generation, comparing accuracy, energy, and p95 latency.
3. **How well does vote-stability gain track correctness gain?** The controller optimizes estimated winner stability rather than directly estimated correctness (§V-B). Measure this relationship against labeled outcomes and compare alternative stopping signals under matched energy budgets.
4. **When does profiling become worthwhile?** The reported end-to-end advantage depends on a limited serving horizon (§VII-C). Sweep deployment length and workload drift, comparing Online, Calibrated, and the proposed calibrated initialization of Online (§VIII).
