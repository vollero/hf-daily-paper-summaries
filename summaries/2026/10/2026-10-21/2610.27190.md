# On the Fragility of Tasks under Composition: Implicit Budgeting in Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-21.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.27190) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27190)


## One-sentence takeaway
Combining independently solvable tasks reduces reasoning tokens per task and disproportionately harms tasks with long isolated traces, while batching by measured fragility saves a reported 43% of output tokens on average with broadly preserved accuracy (§§3–4).

## Problem and motivation
Single-task benchmarks may overstate reliability when models must answer several questions in one prompt. The authors distinguish accumulating independent errors from a stronger failure: composition reducing each constituent task’s own accuracy. They investigate whether this happens when task content stays unchanged and the model has already demonstrated isolated solvability (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled composition evaluation:** Select tasks answered correctly in three independent isolated attempts, concatenate them, and compare per-task accuracy against an independently measured isolated baseline (§3).
- **Budgeting characterization:** Attribute reasoning tokens using task tags; identify approximately proportional token shrinkage with composition size and greater accuracy loss on tasks with long isolated traces (§3.1; Appendix A.2.1).
- **Model-specific fragility:** Show that degradation patterns are more consistent across composition sizes within a model than across models (§3.2).
- **Inference intervention:** Assign smaller batches to fragile tasks and larger batches to robust tasks, using either measured degradation or predictions from fixed task embeddings and a model-specific linear head (§4).
- **Evaluation assets:** Construct symbolically checked Integrals and manually checked OneWordQA datasets (§2; Appendix A.2.6).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Composition harms previously solved tasks | On GPQA-Diamond, Claude Opus 5 loses about 7 percentage points at composition size k=10 on tasks selected for three successful isolated attempts; isolated evaluation uses independent runs. | §3; Figure 2 |
| Token reduction approximately follows a power law | Fits of per-task reasoning ratio versus composition size have median slope −0.54 and median R²=0.91. An exception, R1 Qwen 7B on MATH-500, has R²=−0.43. | §3.1; Appendix A.1.2 |
| Long isolated traces predict greater vulnerability | Across evaluated open-weight models, long-trace groups generally lose more accuracy as token ratios shrink. Forced isolated budgets show similar patterns but stronger degradation than composition. | Figure 5; Appendix A.1.3 |
| Fragility is substantially model-specific | Disattenuated Pearson correlation averages 0.81±0.04 across composition sizes within models, versus 0.24±0.03 across models at fixed size; reported uncertainty is bootstrap 95% interval half-width. | §3.2; Appendix A.2.3 |
| Measured-fragility batching saves tokens in aggregate | Authors report 43% fewer output tokens and +0.6 percentage points accuracy, averaged across models and datasets excluding AIME, relative to isolated solving. | §4.1; Table 2 |
| Aggregate preservation hides individual losses | On GPQA-Diamond, Qwen3 14B measured-fragility batching reduces output tokens by 54.1%, but accuracy falls from 61.5% to 57.9%, reported as −3.7 percentage points. | Table 1 |
| Proprietary-model savings require measured fragility here | Claude Opus 5 on GPQA-Diamond: measured batching uses 521 versus 689 output tokens per task, a reported 24.3% reduction, with reported accuracy change −0.4 percentage points. Predictor batching saves only 0.5% and loses 2.0 points. | Table 1; §4.2 |
| Held-out prediction yields uneven savings | Authors report 31.0% average token savings. Dataset-average savings include 38.2% on Integrals and 4.3% on MATH-500; individual settings can increase tokens or exceed the target accuracy loss. | §4.2; Table 2 |
| Post-training changes tolerance more than shrinkage | OLMo 3 7B’s RLVR stage shows less degradation at similar token ratios than its SFT/DPO stages, while all stages retain similar budget shrinkage. | §5; Figure 6 |

The evidence supports composition-induced per-task degradation and a useful relationship between reasoning length, token reduction, and fragility. Budget-forcing experiments strengthen the budgeting explanation, but do not establish it as the sole cause. The proposed minimum-reasoning thresholds remain an author interpretation rather than directly measured quantities.

Efficiency results concern generated tokens, including thinking and answers. They do not establish net savings after calibration and predictor training, or equivalent reductions in latency or monetary cost.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** The cause of reasoning shrinkage remains unknown; experiments cover independent, same-dataset compositions, and detailed trace analysis covers open-weight models (§7).
- **Author-stated:** AIME offers little batching opportunity because nearly every task exceeds the allowed degradation already at the smallest nontrivial batch size (Appendix A.1.6).
- **Measurement risk:** Attribution relies on models naming task tags. R1 Qwen 7B names every task in only 61.5% of GPQA-Diamond traces at k≥3, weakening token-allocation evidence in that setting (Appendix A.2.1).
- **Claim qualification:** The stated accuracy-loss budget is a selection target, not an achieved guarantee for every model–dataset pair. Table 1 contains larger losses, and the broad predictor claim in §4.2 requires qualification by its individual results.
- **Analyst inference:** Generalization across new tasks within a dataset does not establish transfer across domains or models; predictors are model-specific and evaluated through within-dataset cross-fitting.
- **Analyst inference:** Correlation magnitudes need care because attenuation correction is unclipped and can exceed the ordinary correlation range, as illustrated in §5 (Appendix A.2.3).
- **Reproducibility gap:** Table 3’s numerical body is absent from the supplied text, limiting independent assessment of truncation and parsing confounds.

## Open research questions
1. **What causes the implicit budget to shrink?** The authors leave its origin unresolved (§7). A next experiment could vary task count, prompt length, and answer-format demands independently.
2. **Does restoring reasoning prevent composition failures?** Forced truncation reproduces related patterns but is more damaging than composition (Appendix A.1.3). Test interventions that expand composed-task reasoning while holding task content fixed.
3. **Can prediction deliver net deployment savings?** Calibration is expensive and proprietary-model prediction performs poorly (§4.2). Measure savings on a fresh task stream after charging all labeling, embedding, and training costs.
4. **Does the mechanism extend to dependent workflows?** Agent and conversation implications are extrapolations beyond the tested scope (§7). Evaluate dependent task chains and mixed-domain batches with controlled context growth.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The controlled experiments and detailed methods support the central empirical pattern; causal attribution and deployment benefits remain less established.

Material missing information includes Table 3’s numerical values, underlying figure data, uncertainty estimates for batching results, end-to-end calibration costs, exact pinned dataset revision identifiers, and verified contents of the linked code and data assets.
