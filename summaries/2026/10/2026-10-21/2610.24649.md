# Good Judgment, Bad Advice: Decision-Model Controllers Raise Agent Cost Without Improving Success

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-21.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.24649) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24649)


## One-sentence takeaway
In a controlled terminal-agent study, Jev increased model cost per attempt by 19% with identical observed pass rates, showing that informative completion judgments did not produce useful interventions under the tested policy (§4.1).

## Problem and motivation
Agent scaffolds increasingly ask cheap models when to verify work, change approach, or stop. Such controllers must both predict outcomes and choose actions that improve cost or success. The authors identify a gap between evaluating judgment quality offline and demonstrating useful control online (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled evaluation:** A pre-specified study holds a Claude Sonnet 5.5 terminal-agent loop fixed and compares no controller, deterministic rules, Claude Haiku 5.5, Jev 1.13, and a placebo sampling Jev’s development-phase choice frequencies. Evaluation covers 21 held-out Terminal-Bench 2.1 tasks, three attempts per arm, totaling 315 trials (§3).
- **Restricted controller interface:** Deterministic triggers detect failure, repetition, finish requests, and periodic checkpoints. Controllers receive compact transcript states and select fixed advisory messages or termination actions, subject to shared guards (§3; Appendix A).
- **Judgment–intervention analysis:** Offline replay measures ranking and calibration; online logs examine verification, trajectory length, and stopping behavior. The contribution is an empirical evaluation and diagnosis, rather than a new controller-training method (§§4.3–4.4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Jev increased cost without changing observed success | On 21 held-out tasks, 63 attempts per arm: Jev/native geometric-mean cost ratio **1.19**, 95% CI **1.02–1.36**; both passed **73.0%**. Pass-rate difference **0.0 pp**, CI **−11.1 to +12.7 pp**. | §4.1; Table 2 referenced |
| Jev was cheaper than Haiku but more expensive than placebo | Paired task-level cost ratios: Jev/Haiku **0.81**; Jev/placebo **1.22**. No pass-rate difference was detectable. Numerical intervals for these comparisons are absent from the supplied text. | Abstract; §4.2; Table 2 referenced |
| Other informed controllers also increased cost | Against native, rules cost ratio **1.24** (95% CI **1.12–1.38**), Haiku **1.47** (**1.16–1.86**), placebo **0.98** (**0.78–1.16**), on the same evaluation. | §4.2; Figure 2(a) |
| Better completion ranking coexisted with poor calibration | Offline replay of native evaluation states: finish AUROC **0.78** for Jev, **0.62** for rules, **0.60** for Haiku. Jev’s mean finish probability was **0.50**, while **87%** of the corresponding runs passed; it chose verify on **29/53** finish requests. | §4.3; Table 3 referenced |
| Longer trajectories accompanied the cost increase | Jev averaged **16.0** steps per attempt versus native’s **13.3**. Of its additional **$0.029** per attempt, **$0.011** occurred after the first verification message. The authors label the progress-message explanation correlational. | §4.4 |
| Direct controller overhead was small; abandonment was unused | Jev calls accounted for **0.11%** of spend and averaged **0.52 seconds** per decision. No controller selected abandon in the **315** online trials; placebo assigned it zero probability. | §4.4 |

The evidence supports increased model spending under this particular Jev policy and scaffold. It does **not** establish equivalent success: the pass-rate interval permits meaningful gains or losses, and the authors explicitly avoid interpreting the result as demonstrated noninferiority (§4.1). Offline discrimination also does not establish that a different action would improve an individual run.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope and power:** Only 21 held-out tasks, mostly easy, and one reasoning model, loop, decision model, and message design. A planned open-weight replication was not run (§6).
- **Author-stated variability:** Sampling was nondeterministic and serving hosts were not pinned, although host mixes were similar across arms (§6).
- **Author-stated placebo limits:** Frequencies came from a small development sample and excluded abandonment. The placebo tests decision content rather than holding intervention frequency fixed (§6).
- **Author-stated label limits:** Offline labels reflect eventual trial outcomes, not counterfactual outcomes under alternative actions; difficulty strata use only two calibration attempts (§6).
- **Analyst inference:** Verification and progress-message logs suggest mechanisms but do not isolate their causal contributions. A calibrated-threshold policy and separate action ablations were not evaluated (§§4.4–5).
- **Cost scope:** Reported cost excludes sandbox compute (§3).

## Open research questions
1. **Would calibrated completion thresholds reduce unnecessary verification?** Good ranking did not translate into calibrated decisions. Fit calibration and thresholds on development data, then compare cost and success on new held-out tasks (§§4.3, 5).
2. **Can controllers recognize failures early enough to stop profitably?** Abandonment was never selected and progress AUROC was weak. Test revised stopping policies on new tasks with frequent capped or timed-out failures (§§4.3–5).
3. **Which advisory actions produce the extra spending?** Existing decomposition is partly correlational. Randomize verification and progress guidance separately while preserving the same triggers (§4.4).
4. **Does the result generalize across agents and task difficulty?** The study uses one agent and few intermediate-difficulty tasks. Replicate with fixed alternative models and a larger, more balanced task sample (§6).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported within-study cost finding; **medium** in the proposed mechanism and broader implications.
