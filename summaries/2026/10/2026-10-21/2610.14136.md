# Correction Training for Diffusion Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-21.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.14136) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14136)


## One-sentence takeaway
A corrector trained on a diffusion model’s own predictions improves image generation through repeated refinement, with substantial reported FID gains at approximately matched inference budgets, although excessive correction can reduce diversity and introduce artifacts.

## Problem and motivation
Diffusion denoisers train on real images mixed with noise but sample from states shaped by their own earlier predictions. The authors argue that this exposure bias allows structural errors to persist through sampling. Classifier-free guidance (CFG) improves quality but can reduce diversity and increase saturation (§§1–2.2).

The identified gap is explicit training to refine a model’s clean-image predictions before those predictions determine the next sampling state.

## Approach and contributions
- **Prediction correction:** Train a corrector on the denoiser’s prediction using the original clean target. The default jointly trains denoiser and corrector while stopping correction-loss gradients through the base prediction (§2.3).
- **Repeated inference refinement:** Apply the corrector repeatedly at a fixed timestep before updating the noisy state, despite supervising only one correction during default training. Experiments identify iteration beyond supervised depth as important to the gains (§§2.4, 4.3).
- **Compatibility with pretrained models:** Main pretrained experiments use clean-sample inputs with gated native noise- or velocity-space outputs, preserving pretrained output representations (Appendix B.2).
- **Efficiency and analysis:** Shared backbones reduce parameter overhead; distillation transfers corrected predictions into a standard denoiser. Capacity controls and classifier two-sample tests investigate possible explanations (§§3.3–4.3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Large unguided class-conditional gains | ImageNet-256 FID-50K: DiT **9.58 → 2.07**, SiT **8.55 → 1.92**, SiT REPA **5.53 → 1.31**, at approximately **250 NFE**, including correction calls. | §3.1; Table 1 discussion; Appendix B.1, Table 11 |
| Iteration improves a parameter-matched model | After **80K** from-scratch updates on ImageNet-256, the denoiser–corrector pair achieves FID@8K **8.74** at K=1 and **5.66** at K=3, versus **8.93** for the deeper denoiser; each uses **6,720 transformer-layer evaluations** per sample. | §4.1; Table 5 |
| Gains persist at matched effective depth | The smaller denoiser–corrector pair achieves FID@50K **4.21**, versus **4.88** for the monolithic denoiser, with **240 denoising steps** and **6,720 layer evaluations** each after from-scratch training. | Table 5; Appendix D.1 |
| Unconditional improvement | Authors report lower ImageNet FID than the baseline and best evaluated autoguidance setting at approximately matched NFE; exact scores are absent from the supplied text. | §3.1; Figs. 8–9; Appendix B.3 |
| Text alignment improves | Authors report higher GenEval and DPG-Bench scores with and without CFG at approximately matched NFE using the same-checkpoint baseline; scores are unavailable. | §3.2; Fig. 12 |
| Human preference favors the combined setting | More than **60%** of responses favor CFG followed by correction for quality and diversity: **80** single-object prompts, **800 judgments per metric**; baseline w=6 versus combined w=4, K=8, both with **50 Euler steps**. Compute is not matched. | §3.2; Table 2 caption; Appendix C.3 |
| Distillation reduces correction overhead | SiT-XL/2 student trained against a K=2 teacher reportedly retains comparable ImageNet-256 FID while reducing NFE by approximately **67%**; exact FIDs are unavailable. | §3.3; Table 4 caption; Appendix B.3 |
| Better generation need not mean better reconstruction | Denoising error increases for correction counts k≥3 in the reported analysis, while classifier tests on SiT intermediate states generally indicate a smaller training–inference gap. | §4.2; Figs. 16–17 |

The evidence supports improved generation under the tested architectures and sampling budgets. It does not prove that reducing exposure bias causes the gains: the authors present this explanation as a hypothesis supported by distribution-discrimination tests. Diversity preservation is relative, since correction itself can lower recall (§3.1).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-observed failures:** Excessive correction can worsen FID, reduce recall, and produce unnaturally regular textures or shapes (§4.2; Appendix A.3).
- **Incomplete explanation:** A theoretical account of iterative correction remains open (§6).
- **Limited design exploration:** Ablations vary individual components rather than all combinations; some parameterization experiments are small-scale and limited to one correction (Appendices A, B.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Human preference gains cannot isolate correction from weaker guidance or additional inference compute, because both change in the main comparison (§3.2; Appendix C.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Matched NFE and layer evaluations support compute-allocation comparisons, but do not establish equal wall-clock latency.
- **Evidence boundary:** Selected visual comparisons do not establish distributional mode coverage; the authors explicitly acknowledge this (Appendix C.1).

## Open research questions
- **Does gap reduction cause quality gains?** Classifier tests show association, not causation (§4.2). Next experiment: compare correction variants matched for classifier-measured gap and test whether their FID gains track that measure.
- **Can correction depth be selected adaptively?** Optimal depth and correction intervals vary, and excessive iteration degrades results (Appendix A.3). Next experiment: compare adaptive stopping against fixed-depth correction at matched NFE.
- **Does distillation preserve diversity and alignment?** The reported distillation result emphasizes ImageNet FID (§3.3). Next experiment: measure student precision/recall and text-to-image alignment against the corrected teacher.
