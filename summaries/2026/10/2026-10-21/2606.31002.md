# Beyond Compilation: Evaluating Faithful Natural-Language-to-Lean Statement Formalization

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-21.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.31002) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.31002)


## One-sentence takeaway

On university-level statement formalization, compilation substantially overstates semantic success, but the authors also show that an LLM judge’s rejections can predominantly reflect evaluator errors when assessing stronger generators (§§3–4).

## Problem and motivation

Lean checks whether a generated declaration is well typed, not whether it expresses the intended natural-language claim. A compiling translation can omit hypotheses, change domains, or become vacuous. Without canonical Lean targets, multiple valid encodings also prevent straightforward reference matching (§§1–2).

The authors address a measurement gap: the difference between compilation and semantic acceptance combines actual translation errors, judge mistakes, and ambiguous or defective source statements. These causes must be separated before interpreting formalization performance (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Reference-free evaluation and validation.** Acceptance requires Lean compilation plus faithfulness grades of at least 9/10 from both GPT-5.2 and Gemini-2.5-Pro. Validation combines blinded human audits, a same-sample LeanScorer comparison, a third judge, threshold sensitivity, and bounded formal-equivalence checks (§§2.4, 3.1).
- **Benchmark and error decomposition.** The authors collect textbook statements without canonical Lean targets, compare several system families, and distinguish translation errors from judge and benchmark problems. Human labels cover every rejection of their agent; a human-validated, two-stage model review assesses other systems’ rejections (§§2–4).
- **Controlled tool study.** A factorial ablation varies Herald drafting, search, and Lean feedback to separate effects on compilation, semantic acceptance, and interaction cost (§5).
- **Research artifacts.** The authors report releasing statements with defective items flagged, outputs, judge grades, human labels, rejection reviews, Lean checks, evaluation code, and tool trajectories (Appendix A).

The contribution is principally empirical measurement and diagnosis; the paper explicitly builds on existing judging, equivalence-checking, and agent techniques (§6).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Criterion agrees strongly with audited human labels | On 59 effective Aristotle outputs from a random benchmark sample, agreement with human majority is 54/59, or 91.5% (95% CI 81.6–96.3%). On the 53 compiling outputs, agreement is 48/53, or 90.6%. | §3.1; Table 1 |
| Higher agreement than the tested LeanScorer configuration | On the same 59 outputs and compilation results: 54/59 correct versus 45/59 for public two-stage LeanScorer using DeepSeek-V3-0324 and threshold 0.6; exact McNemar p=0.0225. | §3.1; Table 2 |
| Compilation exceeds semantic acceptance across the main systems | Gaps range from 1.3–29.5 percentage points across eight systems. Most use 227 statements; Kimina-Prover, Kimina-Autoformalizer, and StepFun use 217 input-matched statements. | §3.2; Figure 1 caption |
| Most of the agent’s gap is human-confirmed mismatch | The agent compiles 198/227 outputs (87.2%) and receives acceptance on 131/227 (57.7%). Humans reject 54/67 compiling rejections, confirming 23.8 percentage points of its 29.5-point gap. | §3.2 |
| Most one-shot rejections are classified as translation errors | Two-stage review labels 132/145 rejections as translation errors; these account for 78–100% of the gap for six of seven one-shot systems. | §4.2; Table 8 |
| Later-model results expose judge limitations | With **GPT-5.2 alone** judging, GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 compile 94.3% and 96.0% of 227 statements; both receive 83.3% acceptance. Review labels only 1/25 and 3/29 rejections as translation errors. | §4.3 |
| Formal checks substantiate inconsistent grading | Lean proves equivalence for nine pairs of later-model outputs receiving different acceptance decisions. Separately, BEq certifies 113/183 accepted-output pairs against human-confirmed anchors; remaining pairs are unresolved. | §3.1, Table 4; Appendix H.4 |
| Feedback improves validity more than semantic acceptance | Averaged over other factors in the 227-item factorial study, feedback raises compilation by 49.3 percentage points; 22.1 points of that gain fail the semantic criterion. | §5.3; Table 10 |

These results establish that compilation is insufficient and that semantic acceptance is an imperfect measurement instrument. Human review directly supports the agent’s error gap; conclusions for other systems depend substantially on model-based review. The later-model experiment does **not** measure their overall semantic error rates because accepted outputs were not audited (§4.3).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ stated scope limits:** The benchmark contains 227 unevenly distributed statements from four textbooks, excludes research mathematics, and includes 18 flagged defective statements. Public sources create possible training exposure (§§2.2, 7).
- **Imperfect reference labels:** Human audits cover only the agent and Aristotle. Reviewers disagree, and later Lean checks identify human-majority mistakes. Two reviewers are paper authors and report limited LLM assistance (Appendix E).
- **Review uncertainty:** The two-stage review finds 47/57 human-majority errors in validation. Its first stage also reviews its own model’s outputs, and its sensitivity may not transfer to later-model errors (§§4.1, 4.3).
- **Judge dependence:** Almost all GPT-positive outputs are Gemini-positive, so consensus offers limited additional filtering; correlated judge errors remain possible (§2.4).
- **Tool attribution limits:** Search exposes some Lean checks, overlapping with Feedback, and excludes a dedicated semantic retriever (§7).
- **Analyst inference:** Stopping at the first REPL pass, without enforced semantic checking, may influence the observed feedback gap. The paper does not isolate this stopping policy experimentally (Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference:** Later-model review had access to accepted counterpart outputs, unlike validation review, which limits direct transfer of its validation performance (Appendices G–H).

## Open research questions

1. **How faithful are stronger models’ accepted outputs?** Only their rejections were reviewed. Next experiment: independently audit random accepted and rejected outputs, stratified by domain (§4.3).
2. **Can semantic checking during repair reduce drift?** The runner stops at compilation success and evaluates semantics afterward. Next experiment: compare the current policy with enforced semantic checks under matched interaction budgets (Appendix B).
3. **Can routing recover complementary successes?** Other tool configurations succeed on statements missed by the all-tools agent. Next experiment: evaluate a routing or early-stopping policy on held-out statements (§5.4).
4. **Do findings generalize beyond public textbooks?** Research mathematics and contamination-controlled inputs are absent. Next experiment: repeat evaluation on newly authored, context-complete statements with independent expert labels (§7).

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported compilation and acceptance findings; **medium** for model-based error attribution and generalization.

The supplied text omits many table bodies, full prompt blocks, individual examples, and detailed tool-cost results despite retaining their captions. Consequently, complete per-system breakdowns, all uncertainty estimates, and exact implementation prompts cannot be assessed here. True semantic error rates for the later models remain unmeasured.
