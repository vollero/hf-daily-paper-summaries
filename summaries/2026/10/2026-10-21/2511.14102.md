# Specter: Fast MoE Inference via Quantization-Enabled Multi-Step Speculative Offloading

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-21.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2511.14102) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14102)


## One-sentence takeaway
Specter uses an INT4 version of an MoE target to predict future expert demand and coordinate offloading, reporting a mean 1.50× TPOT speedup over SpecMoEOff in single-request evaluation while retaining target-model verification (§4.2.1).

## Problem and motivation
MoE weights can exceed GPU memory, requiring expert transfers from host memory over PCIe. Data-dependent routing places these transfers on the inference critical path. The authors argue that existing within-token prefetching provides too little computation time to hide transfers, whereas speculative decoding supplies lookahead across future tokens (§§2.1–2.2).

The challenge is making that lookahead useful: independent drafts may lack corresponding expert routes, while deeper speculation increases the number of required experts, cache pressure, and transfer demand. A straightforward combination of speculation and offloading therefore risks I/O thrashing (§2.3).

## Approach and contributions
The contribution is the coordinated system design, rather than speculative decoding or quantization individually:

- **Aligned draft and state sharing:** Quantize routed expert MLPs to INT4 while retaining non-expert components and shared experts in FP16. Share eligible parameters and the target-validated prefix KV cache; choose speculation depth through offline TPOT profiling (§3.2).
- **Prediction-guided offloading:** Accumulate draft expert activations in an Expert Lookahead Buffer, issue incremental prefetch batches during drafting, and protect cache-resident experts until their verification work finishes (§3.3).
- **Integrated execution:** Fuse draft expert computation, group verification tokens by expert, and overlap resident-expert computation with missing-expert transfers using asynchronous streams (§3.4).

Standard speculative acceptance and correction remain authoritative; draft routing predictions guide scheduling rather than replacing target routing (§3.2.3).

These results support decoding improvements within the tested hardware and workload settings. Cumulative ablation supports the combined design but does not isolate each component’s independent effect. Output-distribution preservation is an author claim grounded in standard target verification and state management; the main results do not provide a numerical distribution-equivalence test (§3.2.3).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-described constraints:** Deeper speculation and larger batches expand expert working sets. Phi-3.5-MoE retains substantial exposed transfer latency, and batching raises per-request TPOT despite improving throughput (§§2.3, 4.2.2, 4.5).
- **Analyst inference—hardware scope:** All experiments use one A100 workstation. Performance on commodity GPUs, other PCIe configurations, or multiple GPUs remains unestablished (§4.1.1).
- **Analyst inference—workload scope:** The documented artifact main/comparison runs use five inputs per dataset, greedy decoding, short prefixes, and fixed output lengths. Longer-context memory analysis does not establish comparable end-to-end speedups at long contexts (Appendix A.9; §4.3).
- **Analyst inference—attribution and comparisons:** Cumulative component removal cannot disentangle interactions. Baselines also use different execution/offloading designs; same-cache and same-memory comparisons answer different questions (§§4.1.3, 4.5).
- **Reporting gaps:** Several plots lack numerical values in the retrieved text; Table 6 context-length units and some component-study settings are unclear. Statistical uncertainty for headline speedups is not reported in the supplied text.

## Open research questions
- **Does offline depth selection generalize to changing requests?** Depth is profiled offline, while expert demand varies by workload. Compare fixed versus online depth selection under mixed prompt lengths and routing patterns (§§3.2.2, 4.1.2).
- **How does the design perform with long-context generation?** The paper reports long-context memory savings, but documented main runs use short prefixes. Sweep context length while measuring TPOT, draft acceptance, and remaining expert-cache capacity (§4.3; Appendix A.9).
- **Which optimizations matter under different transfer bottlenecks?** Transfer hiding varies substantially across models, and ablations are cumulative. Run factorial component ablations across GPU memory budgets and measured PCIe bandwidths (§4.5).
- **Can batching retain low per-request latency?** Batch growth expands expert demand and raises TPOT. Compare adaptive microbatching and cache allocation while reporting latency distributions and aggregate throughput (§4.2.2).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly supports the architecture and reported results, but broader generalization is uncertain.

Material missing information includes complete numerical plot data, uncertainty estimates for headline comparisons, several sensitivity/ablation settings, full baseline tuning details, and numerical output-equivalence results. Artifact availability is reported by the authors and was not independently verified.
