# ARCS: Towards Precise Text-to-SQL via Structured Disambiguation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-13.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.09396) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09396)


## One-sentence takeaway

ARCS decomposes ambiguous database questions into selectable interpretations and adjustable thresholds, reducing simulated interaction effort while exposing disambiguation as a major bottleneck in text-to-SQL (§§2, 5.2–5.3).

## Problem and motivation

Ambiguous questions can produce executable SQL that answers a different question from the user’s intended one. The authors argue that existing benchmarks often assume a single, fully specified intent, while ambiguity-focused benchmarks restrict ambiguity to predefined patterns or modified databases. Free-form clarification also requires users to articulate constraints and track dialogue (§§1–2, Appendix A.1).

The proposed gap is an evaluation setting covering multiple interacting ambiguities over existing databases, with annotations that separate ambiguity resolution from SQL generation.

## Approach and contributions

- **Structured interaction:** Identify ambiguity points separately; offer multiple-choice interpretations for finite alternatives and value/operator controls for numeric vagueness. Users can reject proposed ambiguities and compare SQL results across selections (§2).
- **ARCS benchmark:** Use six existing databases, generate candidate questions with gpt-4.1, and have the authors select, revise, and annotate ambiguity points, interpretations, and parameterized SQL. “Naturally occurring” therefore describes ambiguity grounded in existing databases, rather than questions collected from actual users (§§4.1–4.3).
- **Two-dimensional taxonomy:** Cross semantic/syntactic ambiguity with column/table/value/computation ambiguity to distinguish linguistic understanding from database grounding (§3).
- **Evaluation framework:** Combine a two-stage LLM user simulator with end-to-end execution accuracy and isolated disambiguation/SQL-generation metrics. The concrete implementation uses ReAct agents and database tools (§§4.4–4.5, Appendix D).

The authors’ claims of benchmark priority and exhaustive coverage are not independently established by the supplied text.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Ambiguous text-to-SQL remains difficult | On ARCS with structured disambiguation, best reported end-to-end execution accuracy is 57.88% for gpt-5; gpt-6-sol reaches 51.77%; best open-source model, gptoss-120b, reaches 26.05%. | §5.2; Table 1 referenced, body unavailable |
| Complete ambiguity identification is difficult | All evaluated models except gpt-5 and gpt-6-sol achieve Full Recall on at most 30% of tasks; no model exceeds 14% Perfect disambiguation. | §5.2 |
| SQL ability and disambiguation ability differ | For gpt-5, claude-opus-4.5, and gemini-3-pro, ground-truth-disambiguated execution accuracy spans 65.27–68.81%, while Full Recall spans 22.51–61.09%. | §5.2 |
| Structured interaction reduces simulated effort with slightly lower accuracy | On ARCS using o4-mini at medium reasoning effort: structured achieves 42.44% EX and effort 18, versus unrestricted conversational clarification at 43.73% and effort 34; unstructured interpretation generation achieves 28.94% and effort 39. Effort is 0.1 × simulator input tokens + output tokens. | Figure 5; §4.5.1 |
| Taxonomy prompting helps Ambrosia more | With the same structured implementation, o4-mini’s EX rises from 52% to 80% on a 400-sample Ambrosia subset when given its taxonomy; ARCS improvement is described only as marginal. | §5.4; Table 2 caption |
| Small human study favors structured interaction | Eight regular tabular-data/LLM users evaluated both interfaces on retail tasks. On 1–7 scales, effort and frustration each decrease by 0.88 points, mental demand by 0.75, and transparency increases by 0.62; confidence rises from 5.25 to 5.38. | Appendix E.1–E.2 |

These findings support a substantial disambiguation challenge and lower interaction burden in the tested settings. They do not establish statistical equivalence between interfaces, broad usability gains, or that training objectives cause the observed model differences. The authors’ “taxonomy-proof” characterization is stronger than the reported single prompting comparison establishes.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Internal inconsistency:** §4.4 reports 331 task instances from 101 questions; §1 and Appendix B.1 report 311 tasks. The supplied text does not reconcile the evaluation denominator.
- **Annotation validity:** Authors acknowledge extensive corrections during annotation (Appendix B.1). **Analyst inference:** Author-only annotation without reported independent agreement leaves the claimed completeness of interpretations uncertain.
- **Curated scope:** Guidelines exclude unanswerable questions, overly vague phrases, and interpretations judged unrealistic; intended results must be non-empty. **Analyst inference:** Performance may not generalize to these excluded cases (§4.4; Appendix B.1).
- **Simulator and scoring:** Authors identify simulator information leakage and introduce a two-stage mitigation. Quantitative residual-leakage validation and detailed LLM-alignment validation are unavailable (§4.4; Appendix D.4).
- **Metric boundary:** Execution accuracy permits extra columns, including answers covering multiple interpretations. **Analyst inference:** Successful execution need not demonstrate precise intent resolution (§4.5.1).
- **Human evidence:** The authors describe the user study as small-scale; it covers eight experienced participants and one database. Variability and significance tests are not supplied (Appendix E).

## Open research questions

1. **Do simulator rankings hold with real users?** The human study measures interface experience rather than validating model rankings. Next experiment: compare execution accuracy and interaction burden across models with real users on multiple databases.
2. **Can database exploration improve ambiguity precision?** Reported failures include insufficient contextual exploration and spurious alternatives (§5.5). Next experiment: give the disambiguation agent column-description and query tools, then measure Full Recall and Perfect against the schema-only implementation.
3. **Does ambiguity-focused training improve intent resolution independently of SQL generation?** Training-objective underrepresentation is an author hypothesis (§5.2). Next experiment: compare ambiguity-trained and SQL-only-trained models on held-out databases using both isolated metrics.
4. **How robust is the interface outside curated answerable cases?** Construction excludes unanswerable and overly vague questions. Next experiment: add empty-result, unanswerable, and poorly bounded requests, measuring appropriate rejection and clarification.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The method and several results are explicit, but material information is unavailable:

- A reconciled task count and evaluation denominator.
- Full numerical contents of Tables 1–4 and most plotted results.
- Exact prompts, simulator/alignment model identities, and detailed validation results.
- Uncertainty estimates, independent annotation agreement, and user-study variability.
- Confirmed release contents and licensing.
