# SkillMarker: A KGW-Based Watermarking Method for Agent Skill Ownership Verification

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.15725) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15725)


## One-sentence takeaway
SkillMarker combines constrained rewriting with keyed token watermarking to preserve measured agent-skill utility and detect reused instructions, but limited robustness samples and uncalibrated detection leave reliable ownership attribution unestablished.

## Problem and motivation
Plaintext agent skills can be copied, redistributed, or incorporated into other skills. The authors seek ownership verification and leakage tracing without changing operational instructions. Their identified gap is the difficulty of embedding strong watermark signals in compact, structured text where small changes to conditions, identifiers, or required actions can alter behavior (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Quality-constrained rewriting:** Protect code, structural delimiters, recognized literals, uncertain regions, and short spans; rewrite remaining prose using preservation instructions and examples. Group token variants and exempt formatting tokens to reduce structural damage (§2.2).
- **Signal-oriented candidate search:** Generate alternatives with beam search under KGW logit bias, reject candidates failing preservation checks, and rank reconstructed-text candidates by green-token rate, breaking ties with perplexity. This is a local selection procedure, without a global optimality guarantee (§2.3).
- **Detection and evaluation toolkit:** The authors report an open-source toolkit combining embedding, independent quality auditing, and full-text/window detection, plus evaluations of downstream utility, copy-paste reuse, and skill merging. Detection requires the tokenizer and watermark configuration, without model inference (Abstract; §§2.1–2.4, 3).

The contribution is the adaptation and integration of these components for distributed skill instructions; KGW itself is prior work (§4.1).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Measured downstream utility largely survives watermarking | Across four datasets, pooled check pass rates change **−2.61% to +0.93% relative to original skills**. On skill-use-bench, original/watermarked pass rates are **59.85%/60.41%**, using **142 scored tasks per setting**. | §3.2; Fig. 3 |
| Token costs vary by dataset | Mean total agent-run tokens increase **14.1–29.3%** on the first three datasets and decrease **3.3%** on skill-use-bench, relative to original skills. | §3.2 |
| Window detection helps under dilution, but fragmentation hurts | For **2,048 copied tokens** inserted into **4,096 background tokens**, contiguous-copy detection is **100% window-based versus 77.8% full-text**; splitting into eight fragments lowers window detection to **77.8%**. Evaluation uses three sources and three seeds per condition. | §3.3.1; Fig. 4 |
| Fragmentation reduces localization quality | In the same copy/background setting, contiguous insertion gives **70.6% precision / 100% recall**; eight fragments give **36.8% / 56.2%**. | §3.3.1; Fig. 5 |
| Multi-scale merging detection improves recall alongside substantial control errors | With **1/2/4 total input skills**, multi-scale detection rates are **91.7%/72.2%/77.8%**, versus **58.3%/47.2%/44.4%** for single-window detection. Both have observed control false-positive rates of **0.0%/11.1%/16.7%**. | §3.3.2; Fig. 6 |
| Literal preservation is demonstrated in one structured example | Protected spans remain unchanged; saved full-text nominal **p = 4.62 × 10⁻⁹** for a Qwen3-8B rewrite. The authors explicitly disclaim semantic or functional equivalence from this example. | §3.4; Figs. 7–8 |

These results support measured utility preservation and detection of selected reused skill texts. They do not establish universal instruction equivalence, calibrated attribution confidence, or broad adversarial robustness. Importantly, §2.4 describes Bonferroni-corrected window detection, but Appendix A.2 explicitly states that the reported copy-paste tests use **uncorrected nominal probabilities**; merging detector parameters were selected on the evaluation cohort.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated statistical limits:** Repeated contexts and grouped token memberships can violate binomial-null assumptions; nominal probabilities require independent calibration (§2.4). Elevated merging-control false positives reinforce this concern (§3.3.2).
- **Author-stated evaluation limits:** Copy-paste experiments use three selected skills with strong signals. Merging detector selection has no held-out test set ( §3.3.1; Appendix A.2).
- **Measurement limits:** Downstream evaluation uses one agent model and one same-model judgment per case. Bootstrap intervals capture skill/case heterogeneity, not repeated execution or judgment variability (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Without reported component ablations or competing watermark-method experiments, the supplied evidence cannot isolate each design choice’s benefit or establish superiority over alternatives.
- **Analyst inference:** Copy-paste and merging results do not establish resilience to deliberate paraphrasing, watermark removal, or confusion among multiple distribution keys; these tests are not reported.

## Open research questions
- **Can detection be calibrated on unseen skills?** Nominal-null violations and same-cohort detector tuning leave this unresolved. Next experiment: freeze detector settings, then measure detection and false positives on held-out skills and background texts.
- **Which components provide the utility–signal tradeoff?** No component ablations are supplied. Next experiment: compare protection, token grouping, and candidate search variants while measuring pass rates, preservation failures, and calibrated detection.
- **Does leakage tracing distinguish distribution keys reliably?** Distinct-key tracing is proposed, but source-identification accuracy is not reported. Next experiment: distribute differently keyed versions and measure correct attribution and wrong-key matches after reuse.
- **How robust is the watermark to deliberate rewriting?** Reported reuse tests cover copying and merging. Next experiment: apply controlled paraphrasing and removal attempts, jointly evaluating detection and retained skill functionality.
