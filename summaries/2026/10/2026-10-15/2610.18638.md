# To Align or Not To Align: Check Your COMPASS Before You Train

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.18638) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18638)


## One-sentence takeaway
COMPASS combines initial local-neighborhood agreement with an application-specific compatibility proxy to predict which pretrained model pairs will align or merge successfully, with strong reported correlations in the evaluated settings (§5).

## Problem and motivation
Selecting two models for alignment or merging usually requires performing and evaluating the transfer itself. The authors identify a gap between metrics for selecting individual pretrained models and diagnostics for selecting compatible **pairs**. They propose using local representational agreement to reduce this empirical search (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Compatibility diagnostic:** COMPASS multiplies **ease**, the pair’s pre-transfer mutual k-nearest-neighbor overlap, by **ceiling**, an application-specific reference-library score. Alignment ceiling averages a vision encoder’s overlap with reference text encoders; merging ceiling is one minus its pretrained base model’s average overlap with other reference vision models (§§3, 5; B.1.4).
- **Theoretical motivation:** Conditional bounds connect initial overlap to finite-time neighborhood convergence and target-distribution discrepancy to achievable overlap. These motivate the diagnostic rather than guarantee downstream retrieval or classification performance (§4; Appendices F–G).
- **Ranking and interpretation:** The continuous product ranks pairs; median splits produce four diagnostic regimes: act now, budget-limited, ceiling-limited, and incompatible (Figure 1).
- **Empirical validation:** Experiments cover frozen vision–text encoder alignment and same-backbone task-arithmetic merging, with additional adapter, reference-library, and training-budget checks (§5; H.8–H.14).

## Evidence and main results

All results below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| COMPASS predicts alignment outcomes better than either component or CKA | Across 72 pairs, Pearson correlation with post-CSA I2T R@1 is **0.90/0.91** on Flickr30k/COCO, versus **0.84/0.83** for ease, **0.85/0.83** for ceiling, and **0.63/0.68** for CKA. | §5; Figure 3 |
| Merging gains over ease alone are smaller | Across 392 same-backbone task-pair merges from 14 ViT backbones, correlation with mean retention is **0.70**, versus **0.67** for ease, **0.64** for ceiling, and **0.25** for CKA. Retention averages merged/solo accuracy ratios over the pair’s two tasks. | §5; Figure 3; B.3 |
| Ranking supports economical selection within the tested pools | Spearman correlation is **0.91/0.93** for Flickr30k/COCO and **0.70** for merging. COMPASS ranks the optimal alignment pair first and the optimal merge within its first **5 of 392** candidates. | §5; H.6 |
| Selection advantage depends on budget | For Flickr30k I2T, top-20 mean-outcome regret is **2.1%** for COMPASS versus **12.7%** for CKA; CKA also ranks the best pair first and is slightly stronger at top-5 selection. | H.6.2 |
| Pooled merging prediction partly reflects backbone differences | **74%** of retention variance lies between backbones. Task-pair-conditioned probes raise median within-backbone correlation from **0.28 to 0.44** across 14 backbones, each with 28 pairs. | H.8 |
| Feature extraction can be reused across pairs | Scoring the **19 × 10** alignment grid requires **29** model probe passes rather than **190** downstream alignment runs; authors claim over two orders of magnitude lower wall-clock cost. | §5; H.10 |
| Ceiling increasingly tracks outcomes with longer training | Across 18 alignment pairs, Spearman correlation between ceiling and downstream performance rises from **0.79 at 10 epochs** to **0.92 at 300 epochs**. | §5.1; H.14 |

The evidence supports useful prediction and ranking **within these candidate pools and transfer recipes**. It does not establish universal compatibility, causal effects of neighborhood agreement, or a fundamental performance ceiling. The stronger pooled merging correlation also overstates the demonstrated ability to distinguish task pairs sharing one backbone.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ scope limits:** Ceiling requires application-specific knowledge and has no universal formula. Extension beyond the studied applications remains future work (Appendix A).
- **Authors’ theoretical limits:** Reachability and gap-decay assumptions are sufficient conditions that may fail; the finite-time bound can be uninformative. Regimes describe practical transfer under finite budgets, not impossibility (Appendix A; F.1–F.5).
- **Analyst inference—restricted coverage:** Only a designed subset of vision–text combinations receives downstream evaluation. The complete sweep uses CSA; alternative adapters cover a smaller subset. Merging primarily tests equal-weight averaging of same-backbone checkpoints (B.1.2; B.3; H.9).
- **Analyst inference—representation sensitivity:** SigLIP-text findings explicitly depend on mean-pooled hidden-state readout, showing that measured compatibility can reflect feature extraction choices (H.13).
- **Analyst inference—uncertainty and reproducibility:** Fine-tuning uses one fixed seed, and supplied text provides no across-seed uncertainty for merging correlations. Code is promised upon acceptance, despite the checklist’s open-access assertion (B.3; B.7; checklist).
- **Analyst inference—protocol ambiguity:** The main discussion describes CSA training, but B.2 specifies a deterministic closed-form fit. H.14 reports epoch sweeps without identifying the adapter in that subsection, limiting interpretation of its budget evidence.

## Open research questions
- **Can ceiling be defined for new transfer operations?** The authors require application-specific construction (Appendix A). A next experiment could predefine a ceiling proxy for another operation and evaluate its rankings before observing transfer outcomes.
- **How useful is COMPASS within a fixed backbone?** Within-backbone merging correlations are weaker than pooled correlations (H.8). Compare generic and task-conditioned probes over repeated fine-tuning seeds, measuring selection regret within each backbone.
- **How much does feature readout change the ranking?** SigLIP-text results are readout-specific (H.13). Repeat scoring and retrieval with alternative pooling and layer-selection rules.
- **Do diagnostic regimes predict effective interventions?** Budget-limited and ceiling-limited labels recommend different actions, but Appendix A allows recipe changes to overcome poor scores. Prospectively compare added data, larger adapters, and paradigm changes for pairs in each regime.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the core method and headline results, but the source was truncated, so this is not complete coverage.

Material gaps include omitted appendix content, numerical bodies of many tables, exact timing comparisons, correlation confidence intervals, across-seed merging variability, clarification of the epoch-sweep adapter, and an available code-release link.
