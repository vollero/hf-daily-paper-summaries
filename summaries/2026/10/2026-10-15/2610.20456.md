# The COUNT: Counting N-grams Unlocks Scaling on Host Memory and Training-Free Adaptation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.20456) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20456)


## One-sentence takeaway
COUNT converts frozen, host-resident n-gram statistics into transformer input embeddings, improving reported language-model efficiency and enabling training-free domain adaptation through table replacement (§§2, 4–5).

## Problem and motivation
Learned n-gram embeddings increase model capacity with limited per-token computation, but their weights and optimizer states compete with backbone parameters for GPU memory. Count-based language models offer read-only corpus statistics that can reside in host RAM, yet existing combinations typically fuse next-token predictions at the output. The authors investigate whether these statistics can instead supply useful dense input representations (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Count-derived input embeddings:** For matching suffix histories, retrieve frequent next-token candidates and count-based scores, use those scores to combine rows of the LM’s existing unembedding matrix, and add the resulting vector to the token embedding. Experiments use stupid-backoff scores without renormalization (§2).
- **Host-memory scaling:** Freeze the count tables while training the backbone, keeping the large datastore in host RAM. The count-derived representation adds no separate learned embedding table; the experimental recipe also applies embedding amplification (§§2, 3.3).
- **Training-free table changes:** Interpolate domain-specific statistics with the original table, or substitute smaller tables at deployment, without updating model weights (§5).
- **Evaluation and assets:** Compare backbone scaling, learned n-gram embeddings, output fusion, throughput, and representation choices. The authors report releasing Dolma 3 counts, table-building/training code, and COUNT and baseline checkpoints (§1; Reproducibility statement).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Improved fitted compute–loss tradeoff | Across 60M–1B backbones and D/N ∈ {10,20,40}, fitted compute-optimal curves imply standard transformers require **46–89% more compute** to match COUNT’s mean validation token cross-entropy over 11 sources, and **6–56% more** to match mean reference-answer BPB over 74 task configurations. Compute is estimated as C=6ND. | §4.1; Figs. 1–2; metric definitions §3.4 |
| Favorable learned-parameter tradeoff | COUNT mostly occupies the measured Pareto frontier against standard transformers and learned n-gram embeddings at fixed D/N. Accuracy is noisier, with occasional frontier points from other methods. | §4.2; Fig. 3 |
| Domain adaptation without weight updates | For 1B COUNT checkpoints at D/N ∈ {10,20,40}, a **0.25 domain / 0.75 general** score mixture improves aggregate in-domain task loss across math, biomedical, and law evaluations. Accuracy improves on average for math and law; biomedical results are mixed. Exact aggregate effect sizes are absent from the supplied figure text. | §5.1; Fig. 4 |
| Smaller deployment tables retain benefits | The authors report reducing the original roughly **797 GB** table by **more than an order of magnitude**, without retraining, while retaining improvements over the standard transformer for the tested 1B checkpoints. Exact metric values are unavailable. | §5.2; Fig. 5 |
| Modest measured throughput overhead | At the 1B configuration, COUNT retains **93.1–97.8%** of standard-transformer training TPS on eight H200s, **95.5–95.8%** of prefill TPS, and **95.9–96.7%** of decode TPS on one H200. Timings include lookups and transfers; inference uses C4 prompts and D/N=40 checkpoints. | §6; Fig. 6; Appendix A.4 |
| Representation choices matter | At 1B, the unembedding basis beats the input-embedding basis on LM loss, task loss, and accuracy at every tested training budget. Large inference-time changes to candidate count K or history count J lose most of COUNT’s advantage. | Appendices B.4, B.7; Figs. 13–14, 18 |

These results support count-derived input representations within the tested pretraining regime. The compute-equivalent gains come from fitted scaling laws, not measured end-to-end cost savings. The paper explicitly acknowledges that C=6ND omits COUNT’s embedding-gather work (§6). Training-free adaptation is demonstrated for pretrained COUNT checkpoints; the text does not establish equivalent behavior when attaching COUNT to an arbitrary existing transformer.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Resource accounting:** The authors acknowledge additional runtime work despite unchanged backbone compute accounting. Offline counting cost is described qualitatively, and the supplied text provides no complete end-to-end cost comparison (§§2.3, 6).
- **Scaling interpretation — analyst inference:** Table capacity changes from 17.1 GB for smaller backbones to 797 GB for larger ones. Consequently, the main scaling ladder does not isolate backbone scaling from datastore scaling (§3.2).
- **Comparison scope — analyst inference:** The learned baseline uses a specified Over-Encoding configuration, while late fusion uses fixed inference weights and only the longest matching history. Results establish comparisons with these implementations, rather than every possible learned-memory or output-fusion design (§3.3; Appendix B.1).
- **Adaptation validity:** Biomedical accuracy is mixed, and the highlighted interpolation weight was selected from the tested grid. Independent selection and held-out confirmation are not described (§5.1).
- **Evaluation scope — analyst inference:** Backbones stop at the nominal 1B configuration. Accuracy primarily measures answer ranking; GSM8K and MATH adaptation accuracy uses four-choice versions, limiting conclusions about generated solutions (Appendix A.3).
- **Privacy:** The authors warn that rare, high-order counts can reveal source fragments; aggregated counts do not establish privacy protection (Ethics statement).

## Open research questions

1. **Do gains persist beyond the tested model scale?** The ladder ends at nominal 1B. Train larger backbones with both fixed and scaled table capacities to separate these effects (§3.2).
2. **What is the full economic break-even point?** Compute estimates omit lookup work, and counting costs are not quantified. Measure preprocessing, training, serving, and memory costs over matched-quality workloads (§6).
3. **How little domain data is sufficient?** Adaptation corpora contain billions of tokens despite the discussion of smaller-data opportunities. Subsample each corpus and select interpolation weights on separate validation tasks (§5.1; §8).
4. **Can smaller tables be optimized during training?** Large inference-time K/J changes reduce gains. Pretrain matched configurations across table size, K, and J to map quality–memory–throughput tradeoffs (Appendix B.4).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly specifies the method and headline findings, but most numerical table bodies and figure data are absent. Material gaps include exact per-run scores, uncertainty estimates, full optimization and throughput configurations, numerical peak GPU memory, scaling-fit diagnostics, quantified table-construction cost, and independent adaptation-weight validation. Asset availability is author-reported and was not independently checked.
