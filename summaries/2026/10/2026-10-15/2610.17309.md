# StartLux-Decision: AutoThink Decision Models with Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Deliberative Decisions

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.17309) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17309)


## One-sentence takeaway
StartLux-Decision combines single-pass probability prediction with selective reasoning, while its RLCD 2 training substantially reduces reasoning cost without clearly changing held-out accuracy; its strongest independent evidence comes from computational contrast-pair decisions.

## Problem and motivation
Decision models assign probabilities to structured choices so downstream systems can act, escalate, or defer. The authors identify two gaps: single-pass prediction struggles with multi-step computation, and decision training can impair reasoning generation and termination (§1, §2.3.3).

They also investigate a separate reliability problem: confidence calibrated on individual predictions can become misleading after candidate selection or confidence-based routing (§5.4).

## Approach and contributions
- **Two decision-model variants:** Instant uses bidirectional full-attention layers for single-pass option-letter probability readout. AutoThink retains causal attention, reasons when initial confidence is below 0.9, and combines initial and post-reasoning probabilities with 25% and 75% weights (§2.2).
- **Three-stage training:** full-parameter decision supervision, adapter-based task and attention adaptation, then reasoning supervision and reinforcement learning for AutoThink (§2.3).
- **RLCD 2:** a frozen starting model scores probability distributions before and after generated reasoning. Group-relative policy optimization rewards proper-score improvements and penalizes tokens beyond a soft budget. The authors’ component analysis attributes termination gains primarily to the length penalty and calibration gains to predictive rewards (§§3.1–3.4).
- **DecisionPivot:** a programmatically verifiable benchmark emphasizing pairs that differ in one answer-changing detail; pair accuracy requires both answers to be correct (§4.3).
- **Policy-aware calibration and deployment assets:** calibration conditions on selection or routing information; the authors report releasing model weights, inference code, quantized variants, and support for vLLM, llama.cpp, and MLX (§1, §5.5, §6).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| RLCD 2 sharply improves reasoning efficiency at 9B | On 2,986 held-out examples with reasoning always invoked and a 4,096-token generation limit: termination rises from 45.9% to 93.9%; mean length falls from 2,494 to 868 tokens; default-answer accuracy changes from 80.38% to 80.48%. | §3.3; Table 3 |
| Efficiency gains are scale-dependent | Under the same held-out setting, 27B mean length changes from 313 to 303 tokens and termination from 97.2% to 97.4%; accuracy-change uncertainty intervals include zero at all three evaluated scales. | §3.3; Table 3 |
| Restrictive budgets can damage computational accuracy | At 4B, DecisionPivot pair accuracy falls from 60.4% before RL to 52.9% with a 512-token soft budget, versus 59.9% with the released 3,072-token budget; default gate is 0.9. | §3.4; Table 3 |
| Strong public Decision Index performance | Instant exceeds comparable-scale Decision 3.0 models by 7.4–16.9 index points. At 27B, AutoThink scores 73.37 versus Instant’s 72.83 on the public DI 0.3 subset, which also guided development. | §§4.1–4.2; Table D.1 |
| AutoThink improves independent contrast-pair evaluation | Across 7,800 DecisionPivot core examples in 13 families, AutoThink gains 18.4, 12.4, and 11.5 percentage points over Instant at 4B, 9B, and 27B. The 27B model reaches 86.9% pair accuracy versus 82.2% for reasoning-enabled GLM-5.3. | §4.3; Figure 9 |
| Reasoning helps within a fixed checkpoint | On DecisionPivot, 27B AutoThink rises from 48.0% pair accuracy without reasoning to 86.9% at the default gate, averaging 304 reasoning tokens per example, including directly answered cases. | §5.2; Figure 13 |
| Calibration gains do not uniformly accompany reasoning | On DecisionPivot, confidence-bin calibration error is 2.4/3.3/5.2 percentage points for 4B/9B/27B AutoThink, versus 7.3/6.4/5.1 for Instant. | §4.4; Figure 11 |
| Policy-aware calibration mitigates selection/routing gaps | Across 184 settings in four pipelines, signed confidence-minus-accuracy gaps narrow from [−11.7, +20.5] to [−2.7, +2.1] percentage points; transfer to unseen datasets leaves up to 6.1 points of overconfidence. | §5.5; Figure 15 |

The evidence supports efficient selective reasoning and strong performance on the evaluated computational tasks. It does not establish broad unseen-task generalization, independent Decision Index test performance, or calibration preserved under arbitrary deployment shifts. Complete-variant Instant–AutoThink comparisons also combine attention and training differences; fixed-checkpoint reasoning comparisons provide cleaner evidence about inference-time reasoning (§4.1, §5.2).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ disclosure:** public Decision Index scores informed data recipes, attention choices, and seed averaging; they are development results rather than independent test results. Private leaderboard evaluations were not run (§4.1).
- **Authors’ finding:** additional training data mainly help represented task families, with no clear transfer outside their coverage (§5.1).
- **Authors’ finding:** shorter reasoning can hurt long computations; reasoning also adds generation cost (§3.4, §5.2).
- **Authors’ disclosure:** policy calibration remains sensitive to distribution shift and may require renewal when candidate counts, generators, routing rates, or task families change (§5.5, Appendix C.5).
- **Analyst inference:** exact reproduction is constrained by non-redistributed model-generated training examples and incomplete final-mixture documentation; Table A.2 describes an earlier mixture (§A.3–A.5).
- **Analyst inference:** runtime top-choice agreement does not establish probability equivalence, which matters for confidence routing and calibration (§6.1).

## Open research questions
- **Can gains transfer to unseen decision families?** Training-data ablations show limited transfer (§5.1). Next experiment: hold out entire task families during training and model selection.
- **Can routing improve beyond a fixed confidence threshold?** The theoretical analysis explicitly does not establish the released gate’s optimality (§C.2). Next experiment: compare confidence gating with predicted reasoning benefit at matched compute.
- **Can budgets preserve difficult long computations?** RLCD 2 loses accuracy on some long-trace groups (§3.4). Next experiment: compare adaptive per-input budgets with the fixed budget at equal aggregate token cost.
- **How robust is policy calibration after deployment changes?** Unseen-dataset transfer retains overconfidence (§5.5). Next experiment: vary task family, generator, and candidate count while measuring recalibration sample requirements.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the central conclusions and explicitly identifies major evaluation caveats. However, several tables appear only as captions, leaving some baseline scores, ablations, intervals, and deployment results unavailable. Complete training hyperparameters, final data provenance, and independent private Decision Index results are also unavailable in the supplied text.
