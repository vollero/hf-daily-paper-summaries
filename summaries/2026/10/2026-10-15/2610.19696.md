# Share the Symbol, Not the Vector: Training-Free Fact Communication Across Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.19696) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19696)


## One-sentence takeaway
SYNC transfers facts between frozen language models through shared word indices and receiver-local activation directions, achieving strong selective fact use on controlled tasks without establishing a general advantage over text.

## Problem and motivation
Language models can exchange private information through text, but communicating internal states across architectures often requires learned translators or compatibility assumptions. The authors seek a shared interface that avoids both: models agree on symbols while retaining their own internal representations (§1).

The central evaluation problem is whether a receiver uses transmitted facts selectively—changing its answer when one attribute changes while preserving answers about unchanged attributes—rather than merely responding to an activation edit (§3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Symbol-indexed communication.** A prompted sender selects supported inventory words, assigns them to objects, and transmits indices without seeing questions. Each receiver independently constructs word directions by averaging symbol-minus-neutral activation differences across twelve public contexts; neither model receives parameter updates (§§2.1–2.2).
- **Fact-position writing and reuse.** The receiver adds each direction to a saved neutral state at an object’s reserved slot, then reuses the resulting prefix cache for multiple questions. The selected configuration writes once at relative depth 0.2 with strength 1 (§2.3; Appendix H).
- **Transfer and depth prediction.** A setting selected on four development receivers transfers to new senders and receivers. A synthetic object–symbol binding probe predicts usable write depths without evaluation scenes or task outcomes (§§4.1–4.3).
- **Controlled extensions.** Experiments test layout changes, multi-token landmark symbols, four-object conjunctions, and graded write strengths. These establish specific extensions rather than unrestricted communication (§§4.4–4.5; Appendix K.1).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed-setting cross-model transfer | On 572 confirmation scenes, all 25 reported cross-model pairs meet the recovery rule; four-answer success ranges from 72.0% to 98.3% across three senders and nine receivers. | §4.1; Appendix A, Table 59 |
| Symbol–direction correspondence matters | Every individual edit-norm-matched reassignment leaves at most 4.4% four-answer success; original mappings outperform reassignment after Holm correction for every pair. | §4.1; Table 55 |
| Early writing substantially improves delivery | On seven newer receivers, early writing exceeds the original three-layer middle-band configuration by 65.0–96.2 percentage points on the confirmation task. | §1; §§4.1–4.2 |
| Binding probe predicts usable depths | All 45 held-out receiver–depth classifications are correct; balanced accuracy is 1.00 versus 0.87 [0.81, 0.92] for the depth-only rule. Usability means at least 50% complete-fact four-answer success on 92 development scenes. | §4.3; Appendix B, Table 61 |
| Layout robustness has limits | Complete-fact four-answer success is reported as 89.6–100% under changed layouts; the largest standard-to-new-layout loss is 8.3 points, while text remains at 99.3–100%. | Abstract; §4.4; Table 57 |
| Multi-token landmark transfer remains below text | On 172 Countries scenes, sender-message internal success is 23.9–64.0%, exceeding structurally zero no-message success but trailing same-message text by 31.7–63.2 points. | §4.4; Appendix C.2, Table 63 |
| Conjunction use varies by receiver | On 576 four-object conjunction scenes, three development receivers achieve 94.4–95.7% four-answer success; GLM achieves 41.8%. With complete facts, GLM reaches 44.6% internally versus 89.2% as text. | Appendix K.1, Tables 86–88 |
| Graded-strength advantage depends on encoding | Averaged over competing weights 64, 128, and 191, internal delivery beats printed numeric weights by 36.1 [31.0, 41.4] points on Qwen3-32B and 33.8 [20.8, 48.1] on Qwen3.5-27B, using complete facts on 144 scenes. | Appendix D, Table 64 |
| No measured latency advantage | Internal delivery takes 1.19–1.28 times as long as text in the complete-fact timing study; automatic-system end-to-end time is unmeasured. | §6; Appendix J.6, Table 85 |

These results support selective use of transmitted symbols in the tested settings. Four-answer success requires both changed and unchanged answers to remain correct across worlds, making it stronger evidence than an isolated logit shift. However, no-message success is zero **by construction**, and the results do not establish superiority to text, unrestricted generalization, or identical representations across models. Earlier sparse-extraction studies use different configurations and should not be pooled with the final interface (Appendices L–N).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope:** inventories and question forms remain restricted; confirmation scenes reuse attribute combinations, and every single-object fact tuple has appeared previously. Open-vocabulary communication, relational facts, and final-interface TipSheets evaluation remain untested (§6; Appendix I.2).
- **Authors’ stated comparison limits:** input embeddings match or outperform fitted directions on tested single-token tasks. Multi-token embedding comparisons and early-layer J-lens writing remain untested (§6; Appendix K.2).
- **Authors’ stated implementation limits:** two mixture-of-experts receivers fail cache-equivalence checks; another prespecified receiver is excluded because many answers are unscored. Reported outcomes therefore cover compatible, scorable receivers (Appendix A).
- **Authors’ stated causal limits:** the early-versus-middle comparison changes depth, write count, and edit size together. Diagnostics localize useful information flow without identifying a unique mechanism (§6; Appendix G).
- **Analyst inference:** reassignment losses partly reflect format failures on some receivers, although receivers retaining high validity provide stronger evidence of content-specific use (§4.1; Appendix K.1).
- **Source limitation:** the supplied source is truncated, and many tables contain captions without numerical bodies. Complete coverage and independent verification of all reported contrasts are unavailable.

## Open research questions
1. **Can the interface handle unfamiliar vocabulary and relational roles?** Fixed inventories and familiar query forms leave this open (§6). A next experiment could hold out symbols and question structures while adding directed relations and interleaved object layouts.
2. **Do fitted directions offer an advantage for multi-token concepts?** Single-token input embeddings suffice, but the corresponding multi-token comparison is missing (§6; Appendix K.2). Compare full-span text embeddings and fitted directions on the same Countries scenes.
3. **Which intervention property causes early-layer recovery?** Existing comparisons do not fully separate depth, edit magnitude, and processing opportunity (§6; Appendix J.2). Run matched-norm single-layer interventions across depths with controlled cache restoration.
4. **Can senders generate useful graded weights?** Weight experiments supply complete facts without a sender, and the weights are not calibrated probabilities (Appendix D). Test prompted sender weights against numeric and verbal text encodings on held-out uncertainty judgments.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the core method and principal findings, but coverage is incomplete.

Material missing information includes omitted source passages, numerical bodies for many tables, complete per-cell results for several extensions, executable artifacts, verified release status, and automatic-system end-to-end timing.
