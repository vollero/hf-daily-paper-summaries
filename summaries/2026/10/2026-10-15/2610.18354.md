# Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.18354) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18354)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across the tested strategic games, Thompson sampling beats the implemented embedding and reflection selectors on cost-adjusted gain, but selection remains short of the best fixed model and its reliability is uncertain because selector experiments use one seed per matchup.

## Problem and motivation
The authors address choosing an LLM during interaction with an unknown, potentially adaptive opponent. They identify a gap between strategic benchmarks that fix each player’s model and conventional routers that optimize quality and cost without modeling opponents. Model selection becomes harder when feedback depends on both players, opponent choices are hidden, and alternative models’ counterfactual payoffs are unavailable (§§1, 3.2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Strategic-selection formulation:** A repeated, contextual, general-sum meta game sits above the underlying game. Each selector fields a portfolio model, observes its own outcome, and updates its selection policy; opponent identities and actions remain hidden (§3.2, Algorithm 1).
- **Noise analysis:** The authors adapt signal-to-noise, decision-accuracy, and power-analysis methods to assess replication needs for fixed-model strategic evaluations (Appendix B).
- **Empirical comparison:** They evaluate a portfolio of ten open-weight models across five games, then adapt existing routers, reflection, and bandit methods for selection against fixed and adaptive opponents. The contribution is the formulation and diagnostic evaluation, rather than a new selection algorithm (§4, Table 1).

Selection optimizes normalized utility and inference cost with equal weight, using OpenRouter prices as cost proxies (§3.4).

## Evidence and main results

All utility and gain scores below are normalized and multiplied by 100; reported ± values are the paper’s 95% confidence intervals.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic rankings depend on the game | Across fixed-model pairwise matchups, Llama3.3 70B leads Cournot utility at **48.9 ± 2.9**, while Granite4.1 3B leads Chicken at **87.5 ± 0.3**. Each matchup uses one round and 300 seeds. | Table 2 |
| Replication requirements are game-specific | Against a 300-seed reference, pairwise ranking decision accuracy reaches 95% at 20 seeds for Cournot and 200 for Blotto. Detectable-gap fractions plateau around 150 seeds without making every gap detectable. This analysis concerns single-round fixed-model play. | Appendix B.3, Figure B.2 |
| Plain Thompson sampling leads implemented selectors against fixed opponents | In Cournot, gain is **47.7 ± 0.8** for TS, **36.2 ± 0.1** for RouterDC, **31.3 ± 0.7** for Causal LLM Router, and **26.9 ± 0.5** for LLM Selector. TS leads these selectors in every evaluated game; 300 rounds, one seed per fixed opponent. | Table 3 |
| Selection does not surpass the best fixed model | In the extended Cournot comparison, Llama3.1 8B earns gain **72.6 ± 0.9**, versus TS at **47.7 ± 0.8**; 300 rounds, one seed per matchup. | Table D.3 |
| Non-stationary variants do not improve adaptive-opponent performance | In selector-only Cournot matchups, gain is **72.4 ± 0.8** for TS, **65.9 ± 0.7** for sliding-window TS, **63.1 ± 0.8** for discounted TS, and **60.2 ± 0.7** for EXP3; 300 rounds, one seed per matchup. | Table 4 |

These results support scenario-dependent model competence and the strength of a simple feedback-based baseline within the tested settings. They do not establish that embeddings or reflection inherently cannot capture strategic signals: that explanation is the authors’ interpretation, without a decisive mechanism-isolating ablation. Tables 2–4 use different opponent pools and should not be compared directly (§4).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Evaluation covers five games with parsed actions; free-form persuasion, broader applications, and risks beyond expected gain remain untested (§6).
- **Replication risk:** Selector experiments use one seed per matchup for cost reasons. The authors explicitly state that their fixed-model noise analysis does not directly transfer to adaptive selector trajectories (Appendix B.3). **Analyst inference:** reported selector intervals therefore do not establish robustness across independent learning runs.
- **Narrow adaptive evaluation:** Table 4 reports only Cournot. **Analyst inference:** conclusions about handling adaptive opponents remain game-specific.
- **Limited sensitivity evidence:** The supplied text reports fixed utility-cost weighting and selected bandit hyperparameters, without sensitivity sweeps (§3.4; Appendix C.3).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Table 3 describes fixed opponents, whereas Table D.3 describes full pairwise comparisons including selectors while repeating their scores. The exact aggregation relationship is unclear.
- **Reproducibility gaps:** Exact price inputs, complete prompts, training configurations, and a study-specific code/log release are not supplied.

## Open research questions
1. **Do selector rankings survive independent replication?** Single-seed learning trajectories leave this unresolved. Repeat each selector matchup across seeds and estimate uncertainty over complete trajectories.
2. **Can structured strategic context improve selection?** The authors propose learning dispositions and opponent behavior but do not test such a selector (§5.1). Compare structured history features against outcome-only TS under matched prior information.
3. **When does adaptation help?** Non-stationary variants lose in the reported Cournot setting. Test controlled opponent-switching schedules across the game inventory, sweeping discount and window parameters.
4. **Does selection transfer to natural-language interaction?** Parsed actions omit persuasion and conversational risks (§6). Evaluate negotiated interactions with comparable payoff rules and measure both expected gain and loss tails.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The formulation and reported rankings are clear, but selector robustness and causal explanations are less secure. Material missing information includes independent selector replications, precise selector-CI construction, unambiguous opponent-pool aggregation, full training and prompting details, exact cost inputs, sensitivity studies, and study-specific release availability.
