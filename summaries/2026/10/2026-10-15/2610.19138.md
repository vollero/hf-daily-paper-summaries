# MA-Bench: Measuring Operational Planning in Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.19138) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19138)


## One-sentence takeaway
MA-Bench evaluates evidence-grounded mission analysis using expert rubrics, finding that the best tested model satisfies 62% of items under a 3,000-word cap and that inferred judgments are substantially harder than explicit facts (§§5.2–5.3).

## Problem and motivation
Operational planning requires turning incomplete, conflicting documents into defensible determinations without a unique correct answer. A fluent brief can omit essential judgments or turn uncertainty into fact. The authors identify a gap between benchmarks with verifiable outcomes and evaluation of open-ended operational analysis (§§1–2).

The benchmark targets mission analysis: defining tasks, constraints, assumptions, risks, and information needs before developing courses of action. It does not evaluate an executed operational plan (§2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Benchmark assets:** Ten synthetic, expert-validated scenarios, each containing ten to twelve documents, paired with 1,257 expert-authored binary rubric items (§§4.1–4.2).
- **Evaluation structure:** Requirements distinguish extraction, cross-document synthesis, and inference; separate desirable determinations from detrimental assertions; and support severity-weighted scoring across mission-analysis steps. Formatting and prose earn no credit (§§4.2–4.3).
- **Calibrated grading and model study:** A reasoning-model judge evaluates briefs against self-contained rubrics without accessing source packets. The authors compare twenty models, validate grading against credentialed planners, and test alternate judges, length limits, and reasoning effort (§5).

The authors state that packets, rubrics, prompts, scorer, briefs, and item-level verdicts are released; the supplied text provides no concrete release location (Abstract; §6).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Considerable headroom remains | Under the benchmark’s 3,000-word cap and medium effort where configurable, Muse Spark 1.3 leads with 62% items correct and weighted score 0.574; best open-weights model GLM-5.3 scores 0.505. | §5.2; Fig. 1; Table 4 caption |
| Inference is weakest | Pooled benchmark-condition positive-item pass rates: extraction 58.1%, synthesis 49.0%, inference 33.4%; the ordering holds for every model. | §5.3; Fig. 3 |
| Omission dominates classified failures | Keyword analysis of 31,882 failed positive items assigns 60.2% to omission; approximately 28% remain unassigned. This measures judge-rationale classifications. | §5.3; Appendix H |
| Detrimental assertions also occur | Pooled benchmark-condition mistake rates are 25.9% of detrimental opportunities and 28.3% of critically detrimental opportunities, approximately four wrong moves per brief. | §5.3 |
| Judge agreement is credible but imperfect | Against the expert majority on 164 non-tied verdicts, production-judge agreement is 78.0%, Cohen’s κ=0.54, and fail-class F₁=0.82. Adding the judge changes panel Fleiss κ from 0.42 to 0.41. | §5.6; Table 2 caption |
| Rankings persist across judges | On 216 briefs spanning six scenarios and ten models, pairwise judge agreement is 80.0–85.1% and model-ranking Spearman correlation is 0.89–0.94. | §5.5; Table 1 caption |
| Length materially affects scores | In uncapped runs using benchmark model settings, the average weighted-score gain is 0.098; 18 of 20 models improve, while capped versus uncapped ranking correlation is 0.95. | Appendix J; Fig. 8 |
| More reasoning effort gives limited gains | Across five models swept from low to high effort under the cap, weighted scores move by at most 0.08, without a consistent direction. | §5.4; Appendix J |

These results support a gap between recovering stated facts and producing required planning judgments under this protocol. They also support broad ranking robustness across graders. They do **not** establish human-relative planning competence, operational effectiveness, or reliable separation of adjacent models: the authors report scenario-bootstrap intervals approximately ±0.08 wide (§§5.2, 6).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated validity gap:** No human-written briefs establish a human baseline. Holistic expert assessment against source packets is still needed to show that higher rubric scores mean better mission analysis (§6).
- **Narrow scope:** Ten synthetic scenarios cover one planning stage within one doctrinal tradition; generalization to real planning packets or other traditions is untested (§6).
- **Grading uncertainty:** Agreement is lower on detrimental items, with reported κ of 0.21–0.37. Experts and the judge both encountered polarity difficulties (§§5.6, 6).
- **Protocol effects:** The cap truncates many briefs, and content-filter terminations are scored as delivered. Consequently, scores reflect prioritization, output handling, and provider behavior alongside analytical capability (§§5.1–5.4).
- **Analyst inference:** Alternate-judge agreement tests consistency more directly than rubric completeness or operational usefulness; graders share the supplied rubric rather than independently assessing the packet (§§4.3, 6).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Section 5.1 reports 1,020 graded briefs, whereas §5.5 says the cross-judge regrades 1,050. The text does not reconcile these counts.

## Open research questions
- **Do rubric scores predict expert judgments of overall quality?** The authors explicitly leave this unvalidated. Next experiment: obtain blinded holistic ratings using full packets and compare them with rubric scores (§6).
- **Can structured planning workflows reduce inference omissions?** The protocol permits multi-step systems, but the reported study uses fixed prompt-plus-packet generation. Next experiment: compare staged extraction, synthesis, and judgment generation with the baseline under matched output and compute budgets (§§1, 5.1).
- **How well does performance transfer beyond this doctrine and synthetic corpus?** The evaluation covers one tradition and stage. Next experiment: construct independently authored scenarios from another planning framework and repeat expert calibration (§6).
- **Can concise briefs preserve uncapped coverage?** Uncapped generation improves scores, leaving the best prioritization strategy unresolved. Next experiment: compare direct capped generation with long-draft-then-compress generation, grading both final briefs under the same cap (Appendix J).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied narrative supports the principal findings, but most referenced tables contain captions without their data, and several example cards and prompt bodies are absent.

Material missing information includes the full model-results tables, exact scoring-weight mapping, actual prompts and rubric examples, detailed scenario inventory, a concrete release location, and reconciliation of the reported brief counts. Human performance and holistic operational validity remain unmeasured.
