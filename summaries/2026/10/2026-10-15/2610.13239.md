# Jag: Direct Box Prediction for Efficient Visual Grounding

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.13239) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13239)


## One-sentence takeaway
Jag adapts a compact multimodal model to predict a box directly, achieving accuracy close to fine-tuned autoregressive grounding with substantially faster inference, but weaker precise box placement (§§4.2–4.3).

## Problem and motivation
Visual grounding identifies the object described by an expression and returns its bounding box. Autoregressive multimodal models generate coordinates sequentially, adding decoding and parsing costs. The authors investigate how geometric supervision can adapt pretrained image–language representations for direct prediction, beyond fitting a regression head to frozen features (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Existing-state box readout:** Jag adds an MLP to Qwen3.5-0.8B’s last valid input-token state, predicting normalized box center and dimensions simultaneously. It requires neither a new regression token nor generated coordinates (§§3.2–3.3).
- **Geometric task adaptation:** L1 and GIoU losses train the head, language backbone, and visual merger; the remaining vision encoder stays frozen. Training begins with head-only adaptation, although ablations find no consistent benefit from this preliminary stage (§§3.4–3.5, 4.3).
- **Accuracy–efficiency evaluation:** Comparisons cover pretrained and fine-tuned autoregressive baselines, specialized grounding models, readout choices, and independent categorical coordinate heads (§4).

Continuous box regression itself is established prior work; the specific contribution is its integration with an existing input state and joint adaptation of this compact pretrained model (§§1–2).

## Evidence and main results

All results below are author-reported. Acc@0.5 measures the percentage of predictions with IoU ≥ 0.5.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong improvement over the untuned base | Across five benchmark test splits, Jag gains 9.63–17.51 percentage points in Acc@0.5 over Qwen3.5-0.8B without task fine-tuning. | §4.2; Table 2 discussion |
| Competitive specialized-model accuracy | Across all 30,969 test expressions, Acc@0.5 is 88.30% for Jag, 88.00% for LocateAnything, and 79.57% for NExT-Chat; Jag leads on three of five splits. | §4.2; Appendix A.1 |
| Lower measured inference cost | On one A100-PCIE-40GB, single-request mean end-to-end latency is 65.97 ms: 1.82× faster than NExT-Chat, 3.62× than LocateAnything, and 9.35× than Hi-Token. Sampled peak GPU process occupancy is 2.52 GiB. | §4.2; Figure 2; Appendix D.1 |
| Backbone adaptation matters | On the shared 80,000-example ablation subset, joint language-backbone, merger, and head adaptation improves Acc@0.5 by more than 60 points on each dataset over head-only training. | §4.3; Figure 3; Appendices A.1, C.1 |
| Continuous output beats the tested discrete control | Across multiple seeds, continuous regression exceeds four independent categorical coordinate heads on all three datasets; quantization and supervision change together. | §4.3; Table 3 discussion |
| Direct prediction trades precision for speed | Full-data Jag is within one Acc@0.5 point of fine-tuned AR on each dataset, but AR leads at IoU 0.75 and 0.9. AR’s mAcc advantage is 4.24, 4.39, and 6.28 points on RefCOCO, RefCOCO+, and RefCOCOg. | §4.3; Table 4 discussion; Appendix C.2 |
| Within-backbone speed benefit persists | On the first 256 RefCOCO testA expressions, Jag gives an 8.90× mean single-request speedup over AR and 3.54× throughput at batch size eight; models run on separate physical A100 GPUs. | Appendix D.2 |

The evidence supports efficient task specialization and a measurable speed–precision tradeoff. The Base comparison combines fine-tuning and output changes; it does not isolate direct prediction’s accuracy contribution. The AR comparison is more informative about output formulation, although training FLOPs and backbone update counts differ (Appendix C.1). ## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Evaluation covers single-object localization in static natural images. Multiple referents, video, segmentation, and specialized domains require additional design and testing (Limitations).
- **Author-stated deployment dependence:** Latency depends on resolution, expression length, hardware, and serving configuration. Cross-model measurements use different native preprocessing, attention, and decoding settings (Limitations; Appendix D.1).
- **Precision limitation:** AR performs better at stricter overlap thresholds, and on long RefCOCOg expressions (Appendix C.2).
- **Analyst inference—limited causal separation:** The continuous/discrete comparison changes quantization and supervision together; data-budget experiments increase examples and optimizer updates together (§§4.3–4.4).
- **Statistical scope:** Most reported configurations use individual training runs. Image-cluster intervals describe test sampling for fixed models; subgroup contrasts are exploratory without multiple-comparison correction (Appendices A.1, A.3).
- **Target-size coverage:** No evaluated subgroup contains boxes below 1% of image area, leaving very small targets untested (Appendix B.2).

## Open research questions
1. **Can direct prediction close the precision gap?** AR leads at stricter IoU thresholds. Test a geometrically supervised refinement head under matched training and latency budgets (Appendix C.2).
2. **What causes continuous regression’s advantage?** The discrete control changes both quantization and supervision. Compare structured categorical heads and continuous heads with more closely matched geometric objectives (§4.3).
3. **Do gains transfer beyond the evaluated scope?** Tiny targets and specialized domains lack evidence. Evaluate boxes below 1% area and representative domain-specific referring expressions (Appendix B.2; Limitations).
4. **How stable are deployment gains?** Timing covers limited hardware and workloads. Repeat matched comparisons across resolutions, expression lengths, batch sizes, and serving configurations (Appendix D; Limitations).
