# AgentSafeEvo: Adversarial Co-Evolution for LLM Agent Safety

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.19422) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19422)


## One-sentence takeaway
AgentSafeEvo jointly trains an adversarial task generator and a tool-using defender, improving Qwen3-8B’s evaluated safety and planning performance, although its shared task weighting does not minimize attack success in every ablation.

## Problem and motivation
Tool-using agents can encounter malicious content in user requests, tool descriptions, and tool responses, making safety depend on actions throughout an interaction. The authors identify a gap between language-level adversarial training and agent training that adapts entire adversarial tasks: the agent-level prior work they discuss modifies injections within predefined web tasks rather than co-evolving task construction (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Adversarial task co-evolution:** An attacker generates user queries, tool definitions, and environment observations; a defender executes the resulting tasks. Both receive GRPO updates from their interactions. The attacker maximizes the negative mean defender reward, while defender rewards combine separately judged safety and legitimate-task completion (§§3.2.1–3.2.2).
- **Learnability-guided weighting:** Within-task variation in defender rewards determines a shared weight applied to both policies’ normalized advantages. The authors hypothesize that this variation identifies useful learning opportunities; the weighting changes update contributions rather than rewards (§3.2.2).
- **Training data and initialization:** TOUCAN-derived synthetic benign and adversarial tasks initialize the attacker, followed by joint training on benign and red-team seeds. Coverage includes 50 scenarios, approximately 7,000 tools, and six attack categories with 13 subtypes (§4.1; Appendix B).

## Evidence and main results
Results below are reported means ± standard deviations across three independent runs unless identified as cross-evaluation.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Co-evolution improves safety over fixed-attacker training | Qwen3-8B: Agent-SafetyBench ASR falls from **31.01±0.33%** with Static-AT to **17.84±0.24%**; AgentHarm harm score falls from **30.00±2.05** to **6.53±0.38**. | Table 1, §4.3 |
| Task completion under attack also improves | Qwen3-8B versus Static-AT: AgentDojo UA rises from **61.43±0.15%** to **66.52±1.43%**, with ASR **6.71±0.43% → 6.18±0.18%**. MSB NRP rises from **40.64±0.93** to **46.00±0.54**. | Table 1 |
| General capability gains are uneven | Qwen3-8B versus Static-AT: ALFWorld accuracy **30.89±0.43% → 43.72±3.03%**; ToolHop **39.43±0.09% → 48.04±1.40%**; BFCL-V3 **66.73±0.50% → 65.98±0.14%**. | Table 2 |
| Shared weighting improves the combined evaluation profile | Versus unweighted co-evolution, Agent-SafetyBench ASR improves **22.91±0.35% → 17.84±0.24%**, and ALFWorld accuracy improves **36.70±0.75% → 43.72±3.03%**. However, weighting only the attacker achieves lower Agent-SafetyBench ASR, **14.13±0.33%**, alongside lower ALFWorld accuracy, **29.05±0.43%**. | Table 3, §4.4 |
| Both opponents improve, but substantial vulnerability remains | Cross-evaluation on **100 held-out seeds**: final-attacker ASR against the base defender is **92.59%**, versus **49.82%** against the final defender. | Figure 3(a), §4.3 |
| Evolved attacks transfer to additional targets | On tasks generated from the held-out seeds, SFT-to-evolved attacker ASR increases **43.7% → 80.7%** against Llama3.3-70B and **12.3% → 22.5%** against GPT-5. | Figure 3(b), §§4.3–4.4 |

The evidence supports improved benchmark performance for the trained Qwen3-8B defender and stronger attacks against several fixed targets. It does not establish universal safety, defender-training transfer across model families, or uniformly improved capabilities. The weighting ablations support a safety–utility balance rather than the lowest ASR alone.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Benchmark improvements do not establish deployment safety; tools, permissions, and environments require further assessment. Collaborative multi-agent safety remains future work (§5; Ethics Statement).
- **Reproducibility restriction:** The red-team synthesis pipeline, including its prompts and implementation details, is withheld. Aggregate data descriptions and training settings are supplied (Appendix B; Reproducibility Statement).
- **Analyst inference—judge dependence:** Training relies on one model’s safety and utility judgments. The supplied text reports no human agreement study or reward-judge substitution ablation, leaving sensitivity to judgment errors unresolved (§3.2.1; Appendix C.3).
- **Analyst inference—limited generalization evidence:** Defender training is evaluated with one backbone. Cross-model attack transfer does not establish that other defenders would gain similarly (§4.1; Figure 3).
- **Analyst inference—proxy uncertainty:** Reward variability is proposed as a learnability proxy, but comparisons against alternative proxies and sensitivity studies for its weighting coefficient are not reported (§3.2.2; Table 8).

## Open research questions
- **Does defender improvement transfer across backbones?** Only Qwen3-8B is trained as defender. Repeat matched co-evolution and Static-AT experiments with another model family.
- **How robust is training to reward-judge errors?** Judge validity is not independently quantified. Audit sampled trajectories with human labels and retrain using an alternative judge.
- **Is reward variability the best task-selection signal?** The paper ablates weighting placement but does not compare alternative learnability measures. Compare variability weighting against difficulty- and learning-progress-based weighting under matched rollout budgets.
- **Does shared weighting help collaborative agents?** Communication and delegation risks are explicitly deferred. Evaluate coordinated agents with adversarial messages and delegated tool actions (§5).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported method and benchmark comparisons; **medium** in broader generalization.

Material missing information includes independent judge validation, training compute time and cost, cross-backbone defender experiments, uncertainty estimates for attack-transfer results, and alternative weighting-proxy comparisons. Public asset contents are not established by the supplied text, and the synthesis pipeline is explicitly withheld.
