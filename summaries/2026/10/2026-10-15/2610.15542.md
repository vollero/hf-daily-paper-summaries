# Apertus 1.5: From Open Text Models to Open Multimodal Reasoners

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.15542) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15542)


## One-sentence takeaway
Apertus 1.5 extends open text checkpoints into image-and-audio-input, text-output models through staged discrete-token training, with measured efficiency benefits and post-training gains accompanied by capability trade-offs.

## Problem and motivation
The authors identify a gap between capable proprietary or open-weight models and models whose data, code, training recipes, and behavioral principles are also available. They aim to add multimodal understanding and reasoning while preserving existing language capabilities and making development inspectable and reusable (§1).

**Coverage limitation:** The supplied source omits a large middle portion, including most main evaluation sections; this analysis covers only the available excerpts.

## Approach and contributions
- **Multimodal upgrade of existing checkpoints:** Continue training Apertus 1.0 8B and 70B with text, images, and audio. Pretrained Emu3.5 and WavTokenizer codecs convert media into discrete tokens offline; the transformer consumes their embeddings without modality-specific encoder computation in its forward pass (§§2.1–2.3).
- **Staged prediction objectives:** Initially predict all modalities using unpaired data, then train on paired media–text data with text-only targets, followed by cooldown. Released output heads retain only text-token rows (§3.2; Tables 2–3).
- **Long-context and assistant training:** Extend context progressively to 262,144 tokens, then apply SFT, long-context SFT, RLVR, and preference alignment. Thinking is a request-time template flag. The 8B receives SDPO charter alignment; the released 70B stops without it (§§1, 3.3, 4.1; Figure 1).
- **Reusable development artifacts:** The authors report releasing weights, intermediate checkpoints, training and evaluation code, data inventories, SFT mixtures, and preference data. The contribution is the integrated upgrade and release recipe; the discrete-token approach and codecs build on prior work (§§1.1, 2.1).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Image compression reduces token cost while degrading reconstruction | On 350 preprocessed Stage 1 images, Emu3 → Emu3.5: mean tokens/image 8,372 → 2,080; PSNR 26.70 → 24.04 dB; SSIM 0.806 → 0.740; LPIPS 0.075 → 0.099. | §2.4, Table 1 |
| Unpaired image training helps in a smaller controlled study | With Llama 3.2 3B, Emu3.5, fixed subsequent alignment, and 10% text replay, increasing representation training from zero to roughly 57B image tokens raises reported GQA scores 0.50 → 0.54, DocVQA 0.67 → 0.71, and SEED-Bench 0.61 → 0.65 after alignment and SFT. | §2.4, “Two-Stage Recipe” |
| Multimodal training throughput matches the cited text-only range | At 256 nodes and 8,192-token sequences, the first multimodal production stage achieves 5,474 tokens/s/GPU versus 5,300–5,500 for text-only ablations. | §2.1, citing §6.2 |
| Final post-training improves selected instruction and tool metrics | Thinking disabled: 8B SFT → final SDPO improves IFEval prompt-level loose accuracy 80.2% → 90.0% and BFCL v3 accuracy 32.2% → 48.2%; 70B SFT → final DPO improves these from 83.5% → 89.8% and 43.3% → 56.0%. | Tables L.1–L.3 |
| Later alignment introduces trade-offs | Thinking disabled, 8B DPO → SDPO: charter-alignment length-controlled win rate rises 56.8% → 66.7%, while MATH-500 Math Verify falls 54.2% → 50.2% and BFCL v3 accuracy falls 52.9% → 48.2%. | Tables L.1–L.3 |
| Refusal rises substantially during post-training | OR-Bench hard-subset refusal rate, SFT → final: 8B 3.6% → 81.5%; 70B 2.9% → 67.6%. Authors caution that some prompts legitimately warrant refusal, so only stage trends are meaningful. | Table L.3 and caption |
| A smaller helpfulness judge performs better on reported macro averages | Qwen3.6-27B versus Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507: judging accuracy is 84.0% versus 77.5% on JudgeBench, 76.5% versus 76.1% on RewardBench 2, and 83.3% versus 79.3% on RM-Bench. | Table J.10 |

These results support specific reconstruction, training-throughput, and post-training findings. The representation-learning experiment uses a different, smaller backbone, so it does not directly establish the effect size for production Apertus models. The abstract claims broad competitive performance and thinking gains, but the omitted evaluation tables prevent checking their full comparisons and settings.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-reported:** Coarser image tokenization loses fine detail; the authors suggest this contributes to OCR weakness, without establishing causality (§2.4).
- **Author-reported:** The 70B run consumed fewer tokens than intended because resources were reassigned. Its limited multimodal advantage over 8B therefore cannot cleanly isolate model-size effects (§§3.2, 3.4).
- **Author-reported:** Synthetic captions may contain factual errors; upstream caption prompt fragments were sometimes reproduced by the model (§§2.2.1, 3.4).
- **Author-reported:** Original-page opt-out filtering is unavailable for records lacking original page URLs. Image/audio content otherwise generally retains publisher filtering (§3.1.1).
- **Author-reported:** Released models do not yet combine thinking and tool calling as illustrated by the template (Figure 8). Manual safety tests show weaknesses in the tested low-resource language; several risk categories were only partially evaluated or unfinished (Appendices M–N).
- **Analyst inference:** Correlated judge aspects and benchmark accuracy support a cheaper judging choice, but do not establish that helpfulness-only optimization preserves every alignment dimension (§J.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Stage comparisons demonstrate observed changes, but do not independently isolate every training component. Truncation also prevents assessing the complete ablations, uncertainty estimates, and final multimodal comparisons.

## Open research questions
1. **Can finer image tokens improve OCR at an acceptable cost?** Reconstruction losses and the authors’ OCR explanation leave causality unresolved (§2.4). Compare tokenizer resolutions under matched training compute and evaluate OCR alongside general image understanding.
2. **How much does multimodal scaling depend on data budget?** The shortened 70B run confounds capacity with exposure (§3.4). Train both sizes at matched multimodal exposure and track learning curves.
3. **Can alignment preserve reasoning and tool use while limiting unnecessary refusal?** Table L.3 shows competing stage trends. Sweep alignment strength and evaluate charter preferences, mathematics, tools, and separately adjudicated benign refusal cases.
4. **How robust is safety across lower-resource languages and attack variants?** Appendix M reports language-specific failures and qualitative tests without supplied sample counts. Run a documented multilingual adversarial suite with human review and uncertainty estimates.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The available method descriptions and tables support the findings above, but the source is substantially truncated.

Missing material includes most final multimodal, thinking-mode, long-context, and competitor evaluations; full RLVR and alignment recipes; complete ablations; statistical uncertainty; manual safety sample counts; and detailed energy results. Artifact availability is reported by the authors and was not independently verified.
