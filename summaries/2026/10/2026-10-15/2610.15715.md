# History Replays Itself: Unleashing Long-Context Capability in Linear Attention

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.15715) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15715)


## One-sentence takeaway
NoteBank substantially improves frozen recurrent models’ long-range retrieval by archiving selected text and replaying retrieved notes, although downstream gains vary and its tiny analytical FLOPs overhead corresponds to measurable runtime overhead.

## Problem and motivation
Recurrent language models compress history into a fixed-size state that must support both ongoing computation and later recall. Evidence can disappear before a future question reveals its relevance. The authors investigate whether independently preserving sparse evidence can recover capabilities that the pretrained model’s recurrent state alone fails to expose (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Question-independent archiving:** Select short text spans using native-key distinctiveness during causal context processing; store original token IDs alongside compact native-query addresses (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Native retrieval and replay:** Encode the question from a copy of the retained state, retrieve notes through per-channel cosine matching and voting, then replay their text in document order before answering from a separate state copy. The training-free variant needs no external encoder or reader (§3.2).
- **Optional read adaptation and diagnostics:** LoRA adapts only replay and answering. Routing interventions and matched adaptation controls distinguish preservation, access, and evidence use (§§3.2, 6).

The central contribution is this memory interface for existing checkpoints; the experiments demonstrate recovery of long-range retrieval rather than removal of finite-state information limits.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong frozen-model retrieval recovery | GDN mean accuracy rises from **1.7% to 70.3%** at 1M tokens across six RULER-NIAH tasks, 100 examples/task, top-2 replay. Reported gains over Plain are **66.6** percentage points for GDN-2 and **37.8** for Mamba-2. | Fig. 1; §4.1; App. C.2 |
| Downstream training-free gains vary | On 12 LongBench-v1 tasks, 30 examples/task, macro scores rise **15.49→16.07** for GDN and **6.79→13.43** for Mamba-2; replay uses top-1 and top-4, respectively. | §4.2; Table 1; App. C.2 |
| Adaptation explains most adapted GDN gains | With matched adaptation budgets, GDN LongBench scores are **22.72 without NoteBank versus 23.03 with top-1 NoteBank**. Frozen scores are 15.49 and 16.07. | §6.3; Table 4 |
| Larger hybrid gains are modest | Qwen3.5-9B improves **55.7→56.8** on the 2,100-question MemoryAgentBench subset, using task-macro scoring and top-4 replay; LongBench-v2 accuracy improves **46.1→49.3** on 375 QA/dialogue examples with top-8. YaRN is enabled for both baselines. | §4.3; Table 2 |
| Resetting explains much Mamba-2-8B recovery | MemoryAgentBench task-macro accuracy is **0.0% Plain**, **13.0% clean start**, and **14.7% top-4 NoteBank**; replay adds a smaller gain beyond reset. | App. D.1; Table 6 |
| Arithmetic overhead is tiny; runtime overhead is larger | Top-2 NoteBank adds **0.0171%–0.0250%** analytical FLOPs across three recurrent backbones at 1M tokens. Measured GDN latency rises **125.11→140.64 s**, and peak GPU memory **2.93→3.27 GiB**, on H100, batch 1, 32 generated tokens. | §§5.1, 5.3; App. C.5–C.6 |
| Persistent payload is compact | GDN plus NoteBank uses **74.5 MiB** at 1M tokens, approximately **1/2,600** of a theoretical matched BF16 full-MHA KV cache. Weights, transient workspace, cache copies, and Python-object overhead are excluded. | §5.2; App. C.7 |

These results support substantial retrieval recovery within the tested architectures and budgets. They do not establish lossless memory, uniform downstream improvement, or equivalent gains from NoteBank alone where adaptation or state reset contributes. The storage ratio compares against theoretical full MHA, rather than measured total system memory.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ findings:** Coverage and routing remain bottlenecks, particularly for Mamba-2; multi-query and multi-value retrieval are harder than single-needle retrieval. More or longer replay is not consistently beneficial (§6; App. D).
- **Scaling boundary:** Archive storage and exhaustive matching grow linearly with history. Only the replay budget stays bounded (§3.3).
- **Comparison caveats:** Main-text Mamba baselines are unmodified, whereas NoteBank resets recurrent states before replay; appendix controls reveal the reset contribution. Published Transformer/state-caching references differ in training conditions (App. C.3, D.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Generalisation evidence is limited by selected benchmark subsets and LongBench-v1’s small fixed sample. Efficiency evidence is hardware- and workload-specific (App. C.2, C.5).
- **Diagnostic boundary:** Answer-containing oracle notes need not supply sufficient supporting evidence; router timing scopes also differ (§6.1; Table 8).

## Open research questions
- **Can routing recover more preserved evidence?** Mamba-2 has substantial oracle-routing headroom (§6.1). Compare native, lexical, and combined routing with identical archives, readers, replay budgets, and timing scopes.
- **Can archiving better preserve multi-part evidence?** Multi-query/value tasks remain difficult under limited replay (App. D.1). Test coverage-aware writing against distinctiveness selection at matched archive size.
- **When does replay help beyond adaptation or reset?** Matched controls show these alternatives explain much of some downstream gains (§6.3; App. D.1). Repeat factorial comparisons across additional backbones and larger downstream samples.
