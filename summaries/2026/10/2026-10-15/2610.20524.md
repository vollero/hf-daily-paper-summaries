# Physical Time as a Missing Dimension of Robot Policy Memory Dynamics

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.20524) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20524)


## One-sentence takeaway
Temporal Flow Policy (TFP) makes elapsed time govern persistent robot-memory updates, improving reported timing-task success over time-aware memory baselines and linking clock inputs to action timing through controlled interventions (§§3–4).

## Problem and motivation
Observation order does not uniquely determine elapsed physical time: the same visual history can require different actions depending on how long ago an event occurred or how quickly an object moved. The authors identify a gap between recording temporal information and explicitly using measured intervals to evolve policy memory, especially under changing observation rates, jitter, and dropout (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Time-driven memory:** Separate heads predict candidate memory content and positive channel-wise time constants from current features and previous memory. The update is \(h_n=k_n\odot h_{n-1}+(1-k_n)\odot\hat h_n\), with \(k_n=\exp(-\Delta_n/\tau_n)\) (§3.2).
- **Policy integration:** Memory modulates Diffusion Policy and π₀.₅ action decoders through adaptive normalization. Training uses causal demonstration histories and native imitation objectives without event-time supervision (§§3.3–3.4).
- **Behavioral evidence:** Comparisons, architectural ablations, linear probes, and frozen-weight clock interventions test whether retained history and measured intervals affect control (§§4.1–4.2).

The contribution builds on continuous-time recurrent dynamics; the authors distinguish TFP through independently predicted content and timescales and its integration with diffusion and flow-matching policies (Appendix A.3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Higher controlled-task average success | Across three tasks and four observation schedules, equally weighted, with three training seeds: Diffusion Policy TFP **77.5%** versus S4D+Δt **62.1%**; π₀.₅ TFP **73.3%** versus **66.8%**. Training used fixed 0.1-second intervals. | §4.1; Table 1 discussion; Appendix E.1 |
| Action timing responds to supplied intervals | Under jitter with weights and execution schedules fixed, halving intervals delayed actions and doubling advanced them. | Event age is linearly recoverable | Frozen Diffusion Policy TFP memory predicted time since the Passage cue with approximately **0.04 s MAE**, versus **0.83 s** using episode time alone; probes used demonstration histories at nominal 10 Hz. | §4.2; Appendix D.6 |
| Exponential evolution outperforms a learned interval gate | Diffusion Policy success, averaged over four schedules and three seeds: Button **81.2% versus 14.5%**; Passage **97.3% versus 15.0%**. | §4.2; Table 2 discussion |

These reported results support the usefulness of time-driven memory in the evaluated settings. Clock interventions provide direct evidence that supplied intervals influence behavior; probes establish recoverable information rather than proving that a particular decoded variable causes actions. Most table bodies are absent from the supplied retrieval, limiting independent inspection of detailed comparisons and uncertainty.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-specified mathematical boundary:** Interval composition holds only with candidate content and rates fixed. TFP recomputes both at each query, so the property does not establish complete-policy invariance to observation schedules (Appendix B.1).
- **Analyst inference—comparison scope:** Real-robot comparisons use reactive baselines, so they do not isolate TFP’s advantage over alternative persistent memories on hardware (§4.3).
- **Analyst inference—baseline comparability:** Standard LIBERO’s reactive baseline comes from official reported results; reproduced memory variants share protocols, while published policies retain their own settings (§4.4).
- **Analyst inference—generalization boundary:** Controlled schedule tests cover specified rates, jitter, and dropout. They do not establish robustness to arbitrary delays, timestamp errors, or much longer waiting periods (Appendix D.1).
- **Evidence availability:** Referenced SDs, confidence intervals, and many ablation results are unavailable because the supplied text contains table captions without their data. No explicit limitations section or release statement appears.

## Open research questions
- **Does TFP outperform other memories on hardware?** Hardware evaluation compares against reactive policies. Next experiment: evaluate interval-equipped GRU, S4D, and LTC on the same tasks and demonstrations.
- **How robust is TFP to clock errors and longer gaps?** Tests vary observation schedules and deliberately scale intervals, but do not report timestamp-noise robustness. Next experiment: sweep interval noise, bias, and gaps beyond the evaluated range.
- **Does physical-time evolution explain the LIBERO gains?** Benchmark improvements alone do not isolate timing from persistent-history benefits. Next experiment: compare measured-time, fixed-time, and learned-gate variants under matched benchmark cadence perturbations.
