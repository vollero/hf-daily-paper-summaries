# RAR: Rate-Adaptive Rollout for Time-Consistent Interactive World Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.16302) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16302)


## One-sentence takeaway
RAR combines physical-time positional encoding with multi-rate training to support rate-controlled interactive video rollout, showing strong within-range rate interpolation while leaving absolute timing accuracy and out-of-range generalization unresolved (§§3–4; Appendices B, D–E).

## Problem and motivation
Fixed-rate world models associate each generated transition with a fixed physical duration. When generation throughput changes, the simulated world consequently advances too slowly or quickly relative to wall-clock time. A fixed frame budget also forces a trade-off: finer temporal resolution covers a shorter physical horizon (§1, Fig. 1).

The authors identify a gap between offline variable-frame-rate generation and continuous interactive rollout: the latter needs consistent controls and history when temporal step sizes change online (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Stride-RoPE:** Replace temporal frame indices with physical-time coordinates, using \(p_n=ns\) at constant stride. Equal elapsed times then produce equal relative rotary phases across rates. Visual and control conditioning share this mapping; spatial coordinates and rotary frequencies remain unchanged, with no additional learned parameters (§3.2).
- **Multi-stride training:** Sample synchronized video and controls at different strides while keeping sequence length fixed. Fine strides expose local dynamics; coarse strides expose longer physical horizons (§3.3).
- **Continuous rate switching:** Accumulate strides as \(p_{n+1}=p_n+s_n\). A cache storing pre-RoPE keys permits updated temporal rotations without clearing history or recomputing historical features. Sink frames retain fixed positions under the adopted cache recipe (§3.3).
- **Empirical validation and adaptation:** Evaluate rate interpolation, dynamic switching, training-budget trade-offs, and adaptation of pretrained SANA-WM. These are demonstrated applications of the framework; asset release is not established (§4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Longer physical coverage with modest perceptual degradation | At fixed generated-frame counts, lowest versus highest evaluated rates extend horizons by **5× on Doom, 4× on CS2, and 3× on FPV**; LPIPS increases **6.1%–11.4%**, with broadly comparable DOVER. | §4.2.1; Table 1 referenced, but cells unavailable. |
| Within-range rate fidelity | Mean \(|SR-1|\): **3.5% Doom, 3.5% CS2, 4.0% FPV**. Across six unseen intermediate environment–rate settings, SR is **0.960–1.024**. | §4.2.1. |
| Better interpolation than FPS embedding | Across nine seen settings, SR MAE is **7.31% Embed versus 5.09% RAR**; across six unseen settings, **14.50% versus 1.62%**, under matched backbone and optimization-step budgets. | Appendix C, Table 5 discussion. |
| Dynamic schedule tracking | FPV Pearson correlations: **0.998 smooth**, **0.970 abrupt**, measured on globally calibrated, regressed responses over retained cases. | §4.2.2, Fig. 5; Appendix E. |
| Lower-rate learning preserves comparable high-rate quality | Doom, bidirectional stage, 213-frame windows, 10k updates: RAR versus fixed-rate baseline LPIPS is **0.3834/0.4376 at 14 FPS**, **0.3413/0.3863 at 35 FPS**, and **0.3135/0.3113 at 70 FPS**. | Appendix F, Fig. 8. |
| Long-rollout benefit depends on evaluation length | Both models trained with 213-frame windows; jointly denoised 70-FPS evaluation after 10k updates: RAR/baseline LPIPS is **0.3453/0.3432 at 425 frames**, versus **0.3614/0.3929 at 849 frames**. | Appendix F, Fig. 9. |
| Matched-time efficiency advantage | Doom fixed-rate 425-/849-frame training costs **1.56×/5.25×** as much per update as 213-frame training; authors report lower RAR LPIPS at approximately matched-time checkpoints. | §4.3; Appendix F, Fig. 10. |
| Pretrained adaptation is promising but incompletely quantified | Authors report no LPIPS increase at evaluated rates and improved or comparable SR except at the baseline’s native rate; numerical table cells are absent. | §4.4. |
| Extrapolation deteriorates | Low-rate SR: **0.739 at 7 FPS Doom**, **0.636 at 8 FPS CS2**, **0.634 at 10 FPS FPV**, indicating under-scaling relative to ground-truth cross-rate trends. | Appendix D. |

The evidence supports relative temporal scaling and interpolation within evaluated training ranges. SR explicitly removes absolute motion scale, and dynamic switching uses per-rollout calibration and regression; neither establishes absolute physical-speed accuracy. Longer coverage at lower rates also comes with coarser temporal sampling. The horizon-efficiency experiments concern bidirectional generation, including jointly denoised long sequences, rather than directly establishing equivalent streaming-rollout gains.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Out-of-range rates show substantial errors and are excluded from the method’s claims (Appendix D).
- **Author-stated measurement uncertainty:** Intermittent motion makes optical-flow-based tracking less reliable in Doom and CS2. Low-rate SANA-WM residual errors may reflect limited adaptation and measurement uncertainty (§4.4; Appendix E).
- **Analyst inference—selection risk:** SR excludes low-quality cases and unreliable motion regions. Switching evaluation first retains the half of cases with lowest LPIPS, then filters for ground-truth measurement reliability; results may overstate performance across all attempted rollouts (Appendices B, E).
- **Analyst inference—deployment gap:** Prescribed rate schedules demonstrate controllability, but the supplied text does not report an automatic throughput-feedback controller under fluctuating hardware load (§§3.3, 4.2).
- **Reporting gaps:** Numerical table bodies are missing, and rate–horizon table references conflict with displayed captions. Sample counts, filtering thresholds, and several training settings therefore remain unavailable.

## Open research questions
1. **Does rate adaptation preserve absolute timing?** SR and calibrated tracking remove absolute scale. Next experiment: compare simulator-state displacement and event times against ground truth without calibration.
2. **Does adaptation work under real throughput fluctuations?** The paper evaluates target schedules rather than a measured-load feedback loop. Next experiment: vary runtime load and measure cumulative simulated-time error, latency, and quality.
3. **Do horizon gains transfer to streaming generation?** Budget comparisons use bidirectional training and joint denoising. Next experiment: compare causal streaming models under matched training time and cache budgets.
4. **Can broader stride training improve extrapolation?** Out-of-range SR deteriorates. Next experiment: expand the training-stride range and evaluate both boundary behavior and retained fine-rate quality.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The method and narrative results are clear, but missing table cells limit independent comparison.

Material gaps include complete rate sets and per-rate scores, evaluation sample counts, exact filtering thresholds, full training and hardware settings, numerical SANA-WM results, uncertainty for most static comparisons, and code/model/data release availability.
