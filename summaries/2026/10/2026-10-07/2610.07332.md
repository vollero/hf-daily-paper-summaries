# Structuring MoE Expert Selection for Agentic Reinforcement Learning

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-07.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.07332) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07332)


## One-sentence takeaway

Operation-aware and locally consistent MoE routing, guarded by policy-entropy gating, improves tested agentic RL settings, with gains up to 12.9 percentage points in AppWorld TGC and 41.1% in batched rollout throughput, although improvements are not universal (Table 1; §4.4).

## Problem and motivation

Standard agentic RL optimizes task rewards without explicitly organizing MoE expert selection. The authors observe that pretrained routers already reuse experts within generated fields and show greater routing similarity across turns performing similar operations. They argue that post-training can disrupt this structure, limiting task performance and efficiency (§§1, 3; Figures 2, 8).

The identified gap is the co-design of long-horizon agent behavior and native MoE routing, rather than improvements to reward estimation or agent scaffolding alone (§5).

## Approach and contributions

- **Routing analysis:** The authors characterize operation-associated expert usage and within-field consistency in two pretrained Qwen MoE models. These measurements establish routing associations, rather than demonstrating that experts possess particular semantic skills (Figures 2, 8; Appendix A).
- **Hierarchical routing control:** Turn-level control maximizes mutual information between field-averaged router distributions and operation labels extracted through task-dependent rules. Token-level control reinforces the preceding expert set only when adjacent selections are sufficiently similar, excluding field and turn boundaries (§§3.1–3.2; Appendix C.6).
- **Stability mechanism and implementation:** Policy-entropy gating suspends auxiliary routing gradients outside a target range while RL continues. The implementation injects scaled gradients at router logits, updating routers and upstream representations while preserving native top-k routing and forward computation (§3.3; Appendix C.5).
- **Empirical evaluation:** The combined framework is tested with PPO, GRPO, LOOP, and GiGPO on AppWorld and AutomationBench, with component, label, threshold, and efficiency ablations (§4; Appendix E).

The novelty claimed is their combination for native MoE agentic post-training; MI-based specialization and temporal consistency have acknowledged precedents (§5.2).

| Controls change intended routing diagnostics | In AppWorld GRPO analyses, token control increases within-field Jaccard similarity by a reported 7.7%; turn control increases late-stage expert–operation MI by 23.5%. Absolute values are unavailable in the supplied figure text. | §4.3; Figure 5 |

These results support useful routing regularization in the evaluated configurations. They do not establish universal gains exceeding ten points: Table 1 includes smaller gains and one regression. Routing diagnostics show the intended associations, while the efficiency intervention supports a batching-based explanation rather than conclusively isolating the mechanism in normal agent rollouts (Appendix D.3).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ stated sensitivities:** Strong routing updates and permissive token thresholds can cause collapse. PPO’s inconsistent gains are attributed tentatively to insufficient turns for stable MI estimation (§4.2; Appendices E.5–E.6).
- **Authors’ stated efficiency boundary:** The synthetic routing intervention benefits batch sizes larger than one and is explicitly not a quality-preserving inference configuration (Appendix D.3).
- **Analyst inference—label validity:** Hard-coded labels approximate semantics. Appendix F.2 labels actual state updates as Create because their APIs use POST; semantic generalization beyond these rubrics remains uncertain.
- **Analyst inference—evaluation scope:** Evidence covers two model–benchmark pairings, with thinking mode disabled, one evaluation trajectory per task, and R3 enabled in main experiments. Broader architecture and deployment generalization remains untested in the supplied text (Table 4; Appendix E.3).
- **Analyst inference—selection and uncertainty:** Results use best checkpoints selected on AppWorld dev and an AutomationBench “public set.” Whether AutomationBench selection uses the withheld scoring subset is unclear. Run seeds, repeated-run confidence intervals, and significance tests are not reported (§4.2).
- **Analyst inference—comparison consistency:** Baseline values differ across main and appendix tables, whose settings and checkpoint conventions also vary; they should not be pooled as one controlled comparison (Tables 1–3, 8–10).

## Open research questions

- **How robust is control to imperfect operation labels?** Labels are task-dependent and can misrepresent operation semantics. Next experiment: compare current rubrics with manually audited labels and controlled label corruption on identical tasks.
- **Does the framework transfer across architectures and agent formats?** Evaluation uses two Qwen models with thinking mode disabled. Next experiment: test another native MoE architecture and explicit-thinking agents with matched training budgets.
- **How much efficiency gain comes directly from routing?** End-to-end measurements and a synthetic intervention support, but do not fully isolate, the proposed batching mechanism. Next experiment: profile expert kernels and active-expert counts while matching batch size, generated-token count, and environment workload.
- **Can gating generalize without threshold tuning?** Stability depends on selected entropy and control thresholds. Next experiment: compare fixed and adaptive gates across multiple seeds, models, and longer training runs.
