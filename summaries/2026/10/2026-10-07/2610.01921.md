# Cross-Lingual Alignment for Decoder-Only Models using MoE Routers

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-07.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.01921) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01921)


## One-sentence takeaway
The authors report that aligning mean-pooled MoE routing distributions between English and translated sequences improves average multilingual benchmark scores over LM-only continual pretraining in 13 of 14 model–language settings, with one tie and a mean gain of 0.9 points (§5.2).

## Problem and motivation
Decoder-only LLMs lack a natural sequence embedding, while multilingual tokenization complicates token-level alignment across translations. The authors argue that pooled hidden states are unreliable alignment targets, leaving parallel text underused for explicit cross-lingual supervision. They seek an alignment interface that improves transfer to underrepresented languages without requiring token correspondence (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Router alignment objective:** Mean-pool sparse, normalized top-k expert weights over each translated sequence, then minimize English-to-target KL divergence across selected middle layers alongside target-language LM loss. Gradients flow only through the target sequence; no negative pairs are used (§3.2; §6).
- **Packed implementation:** Process source and target sequences together with attention boundaries preserved, and stop source computation after the last alignment layer. The authors claim small overhead, but provide no measured overhead comparison (§3.3; Figure 2).
- **Controlled empirical study:** Compare full-model router alignment against LM-only CPT, router-only updates, and a limited pooled-hidden-state alignment baseline; measure routing, hidden representations, and downstream performance (§§4–5).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Consistent aggregate improvement | Four MoEs, seven target languages, 14 model–language pairs; one epoch over 200,000 examples per run. Router alignment beats LM-only CPT in 13 pairs and ties in one; mean gain 0.9 average benchmark points, maximum 2.1. Averages use available tasks and retain FLORES’s original scale. | §5.2; Appendices B, F |
| Largest reported gain | Qwen3 on Kyrgyz: available-task AVG rises from 43.5 with LM-only CPT to 45.6 with router alignment. | Table 3 |
| Routing becomes more aligned | On FLORES parallel evaluation data, described as out of distribution, auxiliary training produces the largest decrease in entropy-normalized JS routing divergence across all tested settings; baseline routing changes are minor. No numerical divergence values are supplied. | §5.1; Figures 1, 3 |
| Hidden-state alignment also increases | SoftCKA comparisons for Qwen3 show greater similarity to English for Telugu, Kyrgyz, Kannada, and Thai after router-alignment training. Numerical effect sizes are unavailable. | §5.1; Figure 4 |
| Router pooling beats tested hidden-state pooling | Marco-Nano available-task AVG: Sinhala baseline 26.1, hidden alignment 26.3, router alignment 26.8; Hungarian 37.4, 36.9, and 38.1, respectively. | §5.2; Table 6 |
| Router-only updates are inconsistent | Sometimes match or exceed LM-only CPT, but performance and internal alignment changes vary substantially across settings. | §5.2; Tables 3–6 |

The evidence supports a modest, consistent aggregate benefit from the proposed training intervention in these settings. The authors describe this as causal evidence for alignment-driven transfer. **Analyst inference:** the experiment more directly establishes the benefit of adding the objective; it does not isolate representational alignment as the sole mechanism. Learning rates differ between conditions in several comparisons, and heterogeneous task averages limit interpretation across languages.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated limits:** Short CPT on post-trained models is sensitive and inefficient; parallel corpora are predominantly sentence-level and narrow; only languages with existing proficiency are tested (§§4.1, 6).
- **Authors’ stated limits:** Hidden-state comparison covers only Marco-Nano and two languages. Negative pairs and interaction with load balancing are untested. Results do not convincingly establish larger benefits for reasoning than translation or comprehension (§§5.2, 6).
- **Analyst inference:** Small gains lack supplied seed variance, confidence intervals, or significance tests. Missing task-level results prevent checking whether gains are broadly distributed.
- **Analyst inference:** Layer selection is partly subjective for Granite and Marco; no layer-selection ablation is supplied. Different learning rates across conditions complicate attribution to the auxiliary loss alone (Appendices B–C).

## Open research questions
- **Does alignment help more during pretraining?** This is proposed but untested (§6). Compare matched pretraining runs with and without the objective, tracking downstream gains and routing development.
- **Is alignment the mechanism behind improvement?** Router-only results suggest a role for upstream representations without isolating it (§5.2). Compare router alignment against matched internal-supervision controls with equal learning rates and compute.
- **Does the objective remain stable at scale?** Collapse prevention and load-balancing interactions are untested (§6). Sweep alignment and load-balancing strengths, with and without negatives, while measuring expert utilization and transfer.
- **What happens to language-specific knowledge?** The paper raises preservation concerns but establishes no clear tradeoff (§§2.2, 7). Test alignment strengths on local-knowledge evaluations alongside transfer tasks.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The objective and aggregate results are explicit, but the supplied text omits task-level scores, several table bodies, numerical alignment curves, evaluation prompts and per-task metric definitions, seed variability, measured computational overhead, exact realized data mixtures, and sparse-KL zero-probability handling. The “Code Availability” heading contains no release information.
