# AdvSim2Real : Training Web Agents Against Adaptive Prompt Injection in a Web World Model

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-07.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.08773) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08773)


## One-sentence takeaway
AdvSim2Real improves a 4B web agent’s judged completion under adaptive injections in a simulator and transfers clean capability gains to Chromium, but executable robustness under attack remains unverified (§5–6).

## Problem and motivation
Web agents must read pages containing both necessary task data and potentially malicious instructions. The authors seek **task-preserving robustness**: completing the authorized task despite injected instructions when completion remains feasible. They identify two gaps: defenses trained on fixed injections miss adaptive attackers, while adversarial training on fixed tasks loses useful learning signal as the agent masters them (§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Two-stage training framework:** Inside frozen WebWorld-14B, Stage 1 alternates task-curriculum and executor updates, rewarding tasks with intermediate judged success. Stage 2 freezes the curriculum and alternates adversary and executor updates, mixing clean tasks, fresh attacks, and historical attack inputs with fresh rollouts (§4, Algorithms 1–2).
- **Success-flip reward:** The adversary receives credit only when a rendered injection turns an accepted clean control into a judged failure. This excludes already-failed controls, although it does not verify post-injection feasibility or fully control continuation randomness (§4, Equation 5; §D.4).
- **Evaluation assets:** The authors report releasing code, adapters, results, trajectories, and a synthetic form-filling benchmark with protected fields, forbidden controls, and deterministic browser contracts (§1.1; reproducibility statement).
- **Empirical separation of capability and robustness:** Clean browser transfer supports Stage-1 capability gains; simulated attack evaluations support gains for the complete checkpoint sequence. The authors explicitly acknowledge that individual mechanisms are not isolated (§5–6).

The intermediate-success curriculum reward and historical-attack retention build on prior methods; the claimed novelty is their integration with the frozen web simulator and success-flip training (§2, §D.5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Complete pipeline improves clean and attacked completion | On 150 tasks in WebWorld-14B, Base → Robust iter 3: clean **74.89% → 81.33%**; equally weighted Adv v1–v3 completion **48.07% → 57.48%**. Means over three rollout seeds. | §5, Table 1 |
| Gains extend to an unseen attacker | Under Kimi-K3 using the same reactive, one-injection interface: Base **23.00% → 30.72%** for Robust iter 3, a reported **33.6% relative gain**. Two seeds; 293/300 final-checkpoint episodes received verdicts. | §5, Table 3; §C.4 |
| Clean capability transfers to executed browser tasks | On the same 150 tasks in Chromium, Base → Capability iter 3: deterministic strict success **25.56% → 44.44%**; correct target fields **52.50% → 73.24%**, counting 746 target values per seed over three seeds. | §5, Table 2; §C.3 |
| Removing Stage 1 mainly affects clean performance | Stage-2-only endpoint: **76.67% clean**, **57.04% learned-adversary completion**, versus **81.33%** and **57.48%** for the full pipeline. Three rollout seeds; initialization and compute are confounded. | §5, Table 7; §C.5 |
| Hosted larger-model comparison is close under attack | On matched task/seed identities, Robust iter 3 versus hosted Qwen3.5-9B: **57.40% versus 56.96%** attacked; **81.33% versus 78.22%** clean. The attacked difference is smaller than the reported 4B seed spread. | §5; §C.1 |
| Simulator success can overstate executable success | Conjunctive-selection tasks pass **none of 60 Chromium episodes per capability checkpoint**, while world-model judged completion ranges from **40.0% to 58.3%**. | §C.3, Figure 6 |

The evidence supports improved **judged** completion within this simulator and improved **clean executable** completion on its corresponding browser forms. It does not establish browser robustness under injection, robustness on unseen websites, or causal benefits from each proposed mechanism. Rollout seeds measure variability of one trained adapter per checkpoint, rather than independent training runs (§6, §C.8).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated measurement limits:** The simulator can invent entered values, and the judge can accept incorrect arithmetic. The judge also reads injected observations; selected feedback failures establish reward errors without measuring their frequency (§3, §C.7).
- **Author-stated threat limits:** Injection rendering can omit payloads or remove necessary controls. Neither task feasibility nor attacker-objective success is verified; attack completion aggregates include episodes where no injection was requested (§3, §A.2).
- **Author-stated comparison limits:** Reactive attackers generate different payloads for different executors. Paired training continuations share histories and seeds, but not server random state (§6, §D.4).
- **Author-stated attribution and reproducibility limits:** No compute-matched component isolation, independent training-seed evaluation, or confidence intervals are reported. Hosted model versions and inaccessible 9B trajectories constrain replication (§6, §C.5, §C.8).
- **Analyst inference:** Benchmark breadth is limited by synthetic form templates and related task variants; aggregate gains therefore provide weak evidence for broad web-agent generalization (§1, §A.1).

## Open research questions
1. **Does simulated robustness transfer to browser execution?** Only clean capability checkpoints were browser-tested. Next experiment: evaluate Robust checkpoints under browser-rendered, feasibility-preserving injections using deterministic contracts (§5–6).
2. **Which components produce the gains?** Success-flip rewards, difficulty shaping, and historical attacks were not individually isolated. Next experiment: compute-matched component ablations across independent training seeds (§6).
3. **Can an attacker optimized against the final defender erase the improvement?** Kimi-K3 was unseen during training, but no external optimization-based attacker was run against the executors. Next experiment: bounded-query adaptive red-teaming of the frozen final checkpoint (§6, §D.5).
4. **How much does grading error change robustness estimates?** Wrong arithmetic labels are documented, but their prevalence is unknown. Next experiment: blinded audits and executable checks of sampled clean/attacked pairs (§C.7).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied prose supports the main findings and qualifications, but the retrieved text contains table captions without their numerical bodies. Full per-seed counts, most standard deviations, and complete benchmark composition cannot be inspected here. Reported releases were not independently verified.
