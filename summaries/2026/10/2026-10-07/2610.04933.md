# DiVeR: Decision-Critical Verifier Learning for VLA Test-Time Scaling

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-07.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.04933) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04933)


## One-sentence takeaway
DiVeR improves Best-of-N robotic action selection by weighting verifier training toward states with greater action-representation disagreement, with controlled ablations supporting gains over uniform weighting (Table 4).

## Problem and motivation
Robotic demonstration collection is expensive, motivating inference-time improvements to frozen Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies. A verifier can rank multiple sampled action chunks, but trajectory-supervised verifiers typically assign equal importance to every visited state.

The authors argue that routine states dominate trajectories while sparse decision-critical states offer more consequential choices; uniform training therefore dilutes useful discrimination signals (Introduction; Figure 1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Criticality proxy:** Sample candidate actions from a frozen VLA and measure their mean per-coordinate action-expert representation variance. This estimates candidate disagreement without step-level labels or additional environment interaction for criticality estimation (§4.2, Equation 8).
- **Decision-weighted learning:** Standardize criticality within each trajectory, exponentiate it into weights, and train an outcome-supervised verifier with weighted binary cross-entropy. The implementation clips weights and uses class-balanced minibatches (§4.3; Appendix B.1).
- **Lightweight action ranking:** A small MLP scores internal action representations; inference executes the highest-scoring sampled chunk. The principal novelty is criticality weighting within an existing generator–verifier framework, supported by simulation, robot, and component evaluations (§3; Tables 1–4).

The default representation averages final-layer action tokens at the final denoising step. Criticality estimation occurs during training-data preparation, rather than requiring a separate criticality computation at inference (Appendix B.1; Algorithm 1).

The strongest evidence for the proposed weighting is the comparison against a uniform verifier using the same representations. The oracle correlation supports criticality as a useful proxy, but does not establish perfect identification of consequential states. DiVeR does not win every individual setting: TACO slightly leads for π₀ at N=4 on LIBERO-Long, and some RoboCasa categories favor other methods (Tables 1–2).

Experiments are reported as averaged over three seeds (§5.1), but uncertainty is not consistently supplied for baselines or robot results. The verification-latency comparison does not establish an equivalent reduction in total control-loop latency.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Physical evaluation covers a limited manipulation-task set and does not characterize broad environment or embodiment transfer. Performance depends on the frozen policy’s candidate quality and diversity (Appendix G).
- **Analyst inference:** Generalization evidence remains bounded: cross-category testing excludes navigation, and its numerical Figure 4 entries are unavailable in the supplied text (§5.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Baseline adaptations affect comparison scope. KDPE uses a normalized joint-space kernel, while flow-policy MG-Select uses a sampled density ratio rather than its original autoregressive likelihood score (Appendix E).
- **Analyst inference:** The unweighted discriminator’s density-ratio derivation does not prove that the weighted verifier estimates causal success probabilities; the authors explicitly interpret its output as a ranking score (Appendix D).
- **Reporting issue:** Several component and input-ablation passages incorrectly reference Table 6; their corresponding data appear in Tables 4 and 5. Statistical significance of the success-rate differences is not established.

## Open research questions
- **When does disagreement misidentify criticality?** The oracle correlation is positive but imperfect and tested in one policy–environment setting. Next experiment: repeat branched-rollout validation across policies and task categories, examining high-variance states with little selection benefit (Appendix C.1).
- **How broadly does the verifier transfer?** Robot embodiment transfer is uncharacterized. Next experiment: evaluate a trained verifier on new embodiments and environments, comparing zero-shot transfer with retraining (Appendix G).
- **Can criticality guide inference compute allocation?** DiVeR weights training, while inference samples candidates at every step. Next experiment: compare adaptive candidate budgets against fixed budgets at matched total sampling cost (Algorithm 1; Appendix C.3).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text gives clear methods, numerical results, and useful ablations, but lacks numerical entries for Figures 4 and 6, consistent baseline uncertainty, significance tests for performance gains, oracle rollout counts, the hyperparameter-validation split, and confirmed code/model/data release information. The relationship between Table 6’s verification timing and Appendix C.3’s per-chunk timing is also unclear.
