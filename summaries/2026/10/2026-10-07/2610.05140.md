# AutoSciBench: Autonomous Benchmark Generation for Evaluating Scientific Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-07.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.05140) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.05140)


## One-sentence takeaway
AutoSciBench uses structured task planning, solver feedback, and accumulated refinement experience to generate harder scientific-agent benchmarks, with quality improvements supported by model judges rather than independent expert validation (§§3–4).

## Problem and motivation
Scientific-agent benchmarks can saturate as agents improve, while updating them requires substantial expertise and labor. The authors identify a gap in automatic benchmark generation: scientific evaluation needs executable data environments and tasks requiring interpretation of indirect evidence, beyond generating questions alone (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Concept–recipe task construction:** A concept specifies scientific domain, data modality, and required operations; a recipe specifies data construction, question design, and answer verification. Implementation produces a question, scientific files, and a unique reference answer (§§3.1–3.3; A.5–A.6).
- **Feedback-guided refinement:** Three solver trials and a judge identify ambiguity, errors, and shortcuts. The framework revises recipes, then concepts after repeated unsuccessful recipe revisions, seeking verifiable tasks that defeat all valid trials (§3.3; A.7–A.8).
- **Experience-guided generation:** Refinement histories become reusable experience cards that guide subsequent concepts, including lessons from unsuccessful runs (§3.4; A.9).
- **Cross-domain evaluation:** The authors generate tasks in computational biology, materials science, and clinical imaging, and test difficulty, model-rated quality, and component ablations (§4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Generated benchmarks lower average solver accuracy | Authors report reductions of **22.4 percentage points** in computational biology and **25.5 percentage points** in materials science relative to human-curated benchmarks; underlying averaging details cannot be recovered from the supplied table captions. | Abstract; §4.2; Table 1 referenced |
| Difficulty extends beyond the refinement solver | Authors report generally lower generated-benchmark accuracy for other solvers too; materials-science scores separate models more than the near-ceiling original benchmark. Numerical cells are absent. | §4.2; Table 1 referenced |
| Generated tasks receive higher model-rated quality | Both Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.6 Sol rate generated benchmarks higher on average across all domains, using **1–5** verifiability, usefulness, and fitness scores. Exact scores are absent. | §4.3; Tables 2, 4–5 referenced |
| Experience guidance improves difficulty in one ablation | With Claude Opus 4.8 in materials science, both conditions share **10 initial concepts**, generate **50 tasks**, and retain refinement: experience guidance reduces solver success from **66.7% to 35.0%** and raises hard-task proportion from **15.0% to 50.0%**. | §4.5; Table 3 |
| Refinement exposes an interpretation failure | After introducing UMI sequence errors, all **three solver trials** incorrectly count distinct sequences as separate original molecules. | §4.4; Fig. 4 |
| Structured planning supports refinement | Full concept–recipe planning reduces solver success more across rounds than removing recipes or both planning stages, in materials science with Claude Opus 4.8. Curve values are absent. | §4.6; Fig. 5 |
| Later models reduce benchmark difficulty | On their respective generated benchmarks, Opus 5.0 exceeds Opus 4.8 accuracy by **19.5 percentage points**, and GPT-6 Astra exceeds GPT-5.6 Sol by **15.1 percentage points**; detailed aggregation is unavailable. | §4.2; Table 1 referenced |

The evidence supports the authors’ claim that their pipeline produces tasks that challenge tested solvers, and that experience guidance helps in the reported ablation. It does not establish independently verified scientific correctness, expert-rated usefulness, or sustained effectiveness of repeated updates with newer agents. Lower accuracy alone cannot establish that every task requires better scientific reasoning.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated clinical boundary:** Clinical-imaging examples use public de-identified assets and sometimes constructed measurements or synthetic longitudinal cases; the authors explicitly state that benchmark performance does not indicate clinical readiness (C.5–C.6).
- **Analyst inference—coupled construction and judging:** The same model handles all stages within each generation condition, and the quality judges are the generator model families. This creates potential shared-error or preference bias; agreement between two judges does not independently validate answers (A.10; §4.3; B.1).
- **Analyst inference—limited difficulty sampling:** “Hard” means no valid refinement trials succeed among three trials, so it is a sampled, solver-specific designation rather than proof of general difficulty (A.7).
- **Analyst inference—narrow component evidence:** Experience and planning ablations are reported only for materials science with Claude Opus 4.8, limiting conclusions across domains and generators (§§4.5–4.6).
- **Analyst inference—comparison constraints:** Original CompBioBench scores come from its leaderboard, with some reasoning efforts differing or unreported; original clinical-imaging performance is omitted because its scoring differs from exact match (§4.2; Table 1 caption).
- **Analyst inference—answer-format scope:** Requiring unique exact-match answers favors tightly scoped tasks and leaves applicability to open-ended scientific conclusions uncertain (A.6; A.1).
- **Evidence availability:** The retrieved text contains table and figure captions but omits most numerical cells, curves, and actual prompt contents, restricting auditability.

## Open research questions

1. **Do model-rated quality gains survive expert review?** Quality assessment uses two model judges (§4.3). Next experiment: blinded domain-expert review of matched original and generated tasks, including independent answer recomputation.
2. **Does experience guidance generalize across domains and generators?** Its quantitative ablation covers one domain–model setting (§4.5). Next experiment: repeat that controlled ablation in computational biology and clinical imaging with both generators.
3. **How robust is difficulty beyond the construction solver?** Cross-solver transfer is reported, but refinement targets one model using few trials (§4.2; A.7). Next experiment: evaluate held-out solver families with more trials and independently checked references.
4. **Can repeated updates keep pace with stronger agents?** Later-model gains motivate adaptation, but do not demonstrate an update cycle (§4.2). Next experiment: regenerate with newer solvers and compare difficulty, quality, diversity, and cost before and after updating.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied narrative clearly establishes the framework and reported trends. Material missing information includes most table values and plotted curves, aggregate accuracy calculations, statistical uncertainty, actual prompt text, independent expert validation, and confirmed code/data release availability.
