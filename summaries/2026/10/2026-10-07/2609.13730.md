# JumpStart Your Policy Learning with Lessons from 160,000 Training Runs

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-07.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.13730) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13730)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across 162,864 offline policy-training runs, the authors show that tuning and benchmark composition change algorithm rankings, and derive transferable defaults and dataset-conditioned recommendations that improve over existing defaults and a global leaderboard (§§2, 4–6).

## Problem and motivation
Offline reinforcement learning and imitation learning train policies from fixed datasets when further interaction is costly or unsafe. Comparisons can confound algorithm quality with hyperparameters, dataset selection, environment versions, and reporting choices. The authors identify a gap in studying these factors together through extensively tuned, diverse evaluations (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled empirical corpus:** Compare ten existing algorithms across 114 datasets using standardized evaluation, random search, and TPE; release policies, scores, configurations, code, and an extensible leaderboard (§§3, 7).
- **Evaluation sensitivity:** Quantify how tuning budgets, seeds, benchmark composition, and trajectory sources affect performance and rankings (§4).
- **Transferable defaults:** Pool top-performing configurations across training environments, taking numerical medians and categorical modes to construct fixed defaults for held-out environments (§5.2).
- **Algorithm recommender:** Predict algorithm performance from observable dataset and environment characteristics, then recommend three candidates; select random forest following generalization and calibration tests (§6).

The contribution is empirical analysis and reusable infrastructure rather than a new policy-learning algorithm.

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Tuning changes rankings | On 49 shared datasets, tuned IQL overtakes BCQ under both IQM and optimality gap relative to original-implementation reference runs. | §1; Fig. 1; Appendix B |
| Hyperparameters usually outweigh seed variability | Across 731 algorithm–dataset cells, configuration variability exceeds seed variability in 92% of cells, or 83% after removing outlier configurations. | §4.1; Fig. 7 |
| Strong performance precedes stable rankings | Continuous-control best observed normalized scores gain mostly within 25–50 trials, but ranking agreement improves more slowly; discrete ranking agreement rises rapidly within 10–25 trials. | §4.1; Figs. 5–6 |
| Benchmark subsets can change the winner | Among 2,000 randomly sampled subsets per size, a different algorithm wins under both metrics in 24.70%, 23.80%, 20.80%, and 15.45% of subsets containing 10, 12, 15, and 20 pairs, respectively, relative to the full continuous-control suite. | §4.2.1; Fig. 9 |
| No universal leader emerges | ACT leads continuous-control aggregates and BCP leads discrete-control aggregates under both metrics; winners change across collections and expert sources. | §4.3; Figs. 13–15 |
| Transferred defaults improve over library defaults | Across five held-out folds covering 25 environments and 140 matched cells, IQM increases from 0.47 to 0.70 and optimality gap falls from 0.55 to 0.41; fully tuned results reach 0.93 and 0.27. | §5.2; Fig. 18 |
| Dataset-conditioned recommendations improve selection | Leave-one-family-out evaluation on 57 continuous-action datasets from 30 families: random forest improves top-three overlap from 0.409 to 0.485 and reduces best-of-three regret from 0.091 to 0.030 versus the fold-local global-mean baseline. The paired 95% interval for regret reduction is [0.011, 0.130]. | §6; Algorithm 1; Fig. 20 |

These results support the authors’ claim that conclusions depend on the comparison protocol and that their defaults and recommender help within the evaluated suite. They do not establish a universally best algorithm or performance on arbitrary deployment tasks. Scores are normalized against the dataset’s mean trajectory return, and the main leaderboards summarize the top quartile of searched configurations rather than a typical untuned run (§§3.1, 4.1).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ scope caveat:** Minari datasets are not identical to D4RL, so absolute scores may differ from prior reports (§3.1).
- **Authors’ methodological caveat:** Hyperparameter sensitivity and default-support analyses are marginal; they do not capture interactions. Transferred defaults remain starting points for tuning (§§5.1–5.2).
- **Authors’ generalization boundary:** The recommender has only 57 dataset-level examples. RL-source-held-out testing is omitted because removing that source would also remove locomotion and substantial feature coverage (§6).
- **Authors’ model boundary:** Diffusion results concern a standalone U-Net policy trained separately on each dataset; the authors explicitly caution against extending them to pretrained action-generation systems (§4.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Simulator-based evaluation of final checkpoints over 100 episodes supports controlled comparison, but does not demonstrate policy selection where environment evaluation is unavailable (§3.2).
- **Textual ambiguity:** §3.1 reports 52 discrete datasets after filtering, whereas Fig. 17 and Table 9 describe 54 Atari environments. Appendix A also says datasets were not created or contributed, despite describing the authors’ enlarged Atari reproduction.
- **Reproducibility gap in supplied text:** Search-space and per-dataset tables contain captions but omit their substantive entries, preventing detailed checks of configurations and scores.

## Open research questions
- **Can defaults capture hyperparameter interactions?** The current coordinate-wise heuristic and density analysis omit joint structure (§5.2). Compare it with joint configuration selection on the same held-out folds.
- **Does recommendation transfer beyond this benchmark?** Validation holds out families within the available continuous-action corpus (§6). Freeze the recommender and test it on independently collected task families.
- **How should search and seed budgets be allocated adaptively?** The paper separates their variability but does not establish an adaptive allocation strategy (§4.1). Compare fixed-budget allocations with sequential search-and-replication rules.
- **Can recommendations guide practice without target rollouts?** Labels reflect extensively tuned, environment-evaluated performance (§§3.2, 6). Prospectively compare recommended shortlists with global shortlists under constrained training and offline-only selection.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied prose gives clear protocols and several numerical results, but important supporting tables and plots are unavailable as substantive data.

Missing information includes exact search ranges and transferred configurations, per-algorithm policy counts, per-dataset scores and intervals, numerical leaderboard margins, direct release access details, and reconciliation of the discrete-analysis counts.
