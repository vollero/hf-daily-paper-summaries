# Learn2Play Bench: How Well Do LLM Agents Learn from Experience in Unfamiliar Environments?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-09.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.08215) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08215)


## One-sentence takeaway
Learn2Play Bench evaluates experience-based adaptation in unfamiliar text games and finds that raw histories can rival processed memories, harness choice materially affects results, and selected top humans achieve higher peak scores than evaluated agents (§§4.2–4.4).

## Problem and motivation
Improvement on familiar tasks may reflect pretrained knowledge rather than learning new rules from interaction. The authors identify this ambiguity, alongside inconsistent models and protocols across existing evaluations, as obstacles to comparing self-evolving agents (§1).

They target agents that must discover hidden rules through feedback and reuse experience across episodes without updating model weights.

## Approach and contributions
- **Benchmark:** Twenty manually designed text-game templates with novel or counterintuitive hidden rules, deterministic feedback, and automatic scoring. Ten repeat a fixed instance; ten change visible instances while preserving underlying rules (§3).
- **Controlled comparisons:** Evaluations of backbone models, experience-retention methods, and agent harnesses, including matched same-instance versus re-shuffled controls (§§4.1–4.6).
- **Human comparison and evidence archive:** College-student gameplay records, descriptive behavioral comparisons, per-game results, and qualitative rule-discovery annotations (§4.4; Appendices B.3, D).

The contribution is an evaluation framework and empirical study, rather than a new adaptation algorithm (§2).

## Evidence and main results
Unless specified otherwise, agent results aggregate all games using normalized scores, three independent trials, five-episode normal-game windows and ten-episode challenge-game windows; challenge games receive triple weight (§4.1; Appendix A.2). LS measures normalized score change per episode.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|

| Raw histories are competitive, not universally best | Across three backbones, MEMORY leads Mean/LG/LS at **44.5/+11.4/+3.04**, while Reflexion leads Max at **60.0**, versus MEMORY’s **57.6**. | Table 7 |
| Harness changes improve matched-backbone results | Opus 5: OpenCode → Claude Code raises Max **74.5 → 81.8**, Mean **53.8 → 62.3**, LG **21.3 → 27.1**, LS **5.13 → 6.59**. GPT-5.6-SOL: OpenCode → Codex raises Max **57.0 → 74.3**. | Table 2 |

These results establish benchmark-specific differences in achieved scores and learning trajectories. They do not isolate why a harness helps, prove that memory compression causes poorer learning, or demonstrate a population-wide human advantage. The authors explicitly describe behavioral comparisons and interaction cases as descriptive rather than causal (Appendices B.1.3, B.3.3).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Three trials limit precision of variability estimates; reported aggregate SDs are not confidence intervals (Appendix C).
- **Author-stated:** Rule annotations mix complete-rule discovery with successful execution, may extend beyond scoring windows, and omit Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra. High scores alone do not establish rule discovery (Appendix D).
- **Author-stated:** Some normalization references are estimated or only partially proved. Cost estimates depend on pricing and reconstructed caching; Astra’s billing basis differs and token records are incomplete (Appendices A.2.4, B.5).
- **Analyst inference:** Pinned rule seeds, deterministic text environments, and short evaluation windows limit conclusions about broader environments or long-term adaptation (§4.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Human Top-k selection and humans’ prohibition on code use complicate human–agent comparisons, especially where agents use programs (Appendix B.3).
- **Evidence inconsistency:** §4.4 says OpenCode–Opus 5.5 outperforms Human Top-1 in Model Auditor, but Table 73 reports Human Top-1 Max **70.0**, exceeding Gemini 3.5 Flash’s **75.0** only in the opposite direction; Opus 5.5’s **90.0** does support the stated comparison. The table therefore supports that specific claim, while causal explanations about human effort remain unverified (Appendix B.3.4).

## Open research questions
- **Can evidence-linked summaries retain raw-memory benefits at lower cost?** Compression-related failures are illustrative, not causal. Compare raw histories, summaries, and summaries linked to source interactions under matched token budgets (Appendix B.1.3).
- **Does deliberate exploration improve learning?** Human repetition and recovery statistics do not establish causation. Randomize exploration policies within one backbone and harness, measuring scores and verified rule discovery (Appendix B.3.3).
- **Do findings generalize across rule seeds and longer horizons?** Main results use pinned instances and short windows. Repeat evaluations over multiple rule seeds and longer episode sequences (§4.1).
- **Which harness components explain gains?** Matched backbones establish system differences but do not isolate components. Ablate action batching, context handling, and tool availability under matched budgets (§4.3; Appendix B.5).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported benchmark comparisons; **medium** in proposed mechanisms and broader generalization.

Material information unavailable in the supplied text includes complete implementation prompts, exact harness versions and reasoning configurations, full executable game definitions, verified downloadable release contents, multi-seed aggregate results, and causal component ablations.
