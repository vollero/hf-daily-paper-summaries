# Do LLMs Understand Sequential Structure? A Controlled Study of Inference and Generation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-09.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.04977) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04977)


## One-sentence takeaway
In controlled symbolic sequence tasks, the authors find that correct strategy identification and accurate action frequencies do not ensure faithful conditional generation, with higher-order dependencies remaining difficult even when transition rules are supplied (§§4–5).

## Problem and motivation
Behavioral simulators must reproduce how actions depend on history, not just how frequently actions occur. Different generating processes can produce similar marginal statistics, making plausible-looking behavior insufficient evidence of structural understanding. The authors address a gap in evaluations that conflate strategy recognition, distribution matching, and conditional rule execution (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Controlled diagnostic framework:** Two-player Rock–Paper–Scissors trajectories have known statistical or deterministic Markov strategies. Separate evaluations measure identification, marginal fidelity, and rule execution against generated histories (§§3.1–3.5).
- **Structural and execution controls:** Experiments vary opponent-only versus joint-state conditioning and first- versus second-order dependencies; teacher-forced next-step prediction helps distinguish local application failures from free-running execution failures (§§4.2, 4.4).
- **Stochastic follow-up:** One-player sequences over `{A,B,C}` vary dependency order and access to explicit transition probabilities. CCLG and weighted Jensen–Shannon divergence assess conditional generation, with a prefix-only empirical n-gram estimator providing a structured reference (§5; Appendix X).

These are evaluation contributions; the supplied text does not establish a new trained model or a public asset release.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Markov strategies are harder to identify | Across seven configurations, averaged over tested context lengths, Markov identification accuracy is lower for every model; six differences have *p* < .001, while GPT-5 has *p* = .095. | §4.1; Fig. 3 |
| More history does not reliably improve identification | Comparing 100–1000 observed RPS rounds, all seven models decline on non-Markov players and nearly all decline on Markov players. Closed-pool MLE achieves 100% exact-match identification at every tested length on the same trajectories. | §4.1; Appendix E, Fig. 11; Appendix F, Fig. 13 |
| Incorrect identification can preserve marginal fidelity | Among 282 incorrectly recovered strategies in the simulation analysis, 41.8% have marginal TV ≤ 0.02 and 62.1% have TV ≤ 0.10. The supplied passage does not specify the subset’s player-type composition. | §4.2 |
| Markov simulation has greater marginal error | In first-order opponent-only inference-and-simulation, Markov MSE is reported as approximately 8–9% higher overall and more than 100% higher in the correct-identity subset across context lengths. Continuations contain 1000 rounds, evaluated in 100-round windows. | §4.2; Appendix C; Fig. 6 caption |
| Local application and sustained execution differ | Under ground-truth histories, DeepSeek Reasoner and GPT-5 perform strongly on next-step prediction; DeepSeek Chat and GPT-5-mini still struggle. Numerical table entries are absent. | §4.2; Table 1 |
| Second-order dependencies reduce strict execution | For deepseek-reasoner, second-order rules lower strict rule match despite relatively high overlap and identification accuracy; joint-state conditioning alone does not reduce performance in the tested settings. | §4.4; Fig. 8 |
| Stochastic high-order generation deteriorates | With prefix length 2048 and orders 1, 2, 4, and 8, most models show sharply lower CCLG and higher WJS at order 8. Longer prefixes and explicit transition rules do not consistently remove degradation. | §5.3; Fig. 9; Appendices U–W |
| A structured prefix-only baseline remains competitive | The empirical n-gram estimator uses observed-prefix counts and lower-order backoff; at order 8 it reportedly has the best WJS among evaluated methods, without recovering the full transition table. | Appendix X; Figs. 33–34 |

The evidence supports separating recognition, marginal matching, and conditional execution in these tasks. Teacher forcing and explicit-rule conditions support the authors’ interpretation that failures extend beyond identification alone. They do not establish a specific internal mechanism or general failure across all sequential applications. MSE also requires care: Appendix O explains that marginal drift can occur through changed state visitation even when local rules remain valid.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** The predefined strategy pool excludes open-ended strategy discovery. Symbolic representations, prompts, schemas, parsing, and decoding settings may not be optimal, despite tested prompt variants (Limitations).
- **Analyst inference—generalization:** The complex RPS structural comparison uses only deepseek-reasoner, and both environments use small symbolic action spaces; broader model and application generalization remains untested (§4.4; §5.2).
- **Analyst inference—comparison controls:** Decoding and reasoning configurations differ across providers, limiting attribution of model differences solely to capability (Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference—prompt effects:** RPS generation prompts discourage periodicity and long runs, potentially influencing generated statistics; the supplied text does not isolate this constraint’s effect (Appendix A.4).
- **Evidence availability:** Many figures provide captions and narrative conclusions without numerical values; several tables lack entries. Figure references are inconsistent: §4.2 points to Fig. 7 for an MSE result, while Fig. 6’s caption identifies that plot.

## Open research questions

- **Does the diagnosis extend beyond closed strategy pools?** The authors explicitly leave open-ended discovery untested. A next experiment could introduce unseen strategies and score inferred transition behavior independently of identity labels.
- **How much degradation comes from dependency length versus sparse observations?** Higher-order states are described as sparse, and the baseline backs off under sparsity (§5.3; Appendix X). A next experiment could equalize state coverage across orders.
- **Can explicit state tracking improve execution?** Rule access alone is insufficient, but the study does not test external state maintenance (§5.3). A next experiment could compare direct generation with stepwise generation supplied with the current history state.
- **How sensitive are results to decoding and randomness instructions?** Settings differ and prompt optimality remains unresolved (Appendices A–B; Limitations). A next experiment could vary decoding settings and anti-repetition constraints within each model.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The qualitative findings are repeatedly supported in the supplied narrative, but much numerical evidence is unavailable.
