# Incidental information contaminates patient notes and disrupts clinical reasoning in large language models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-09.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.08585) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08585)


## One-sentence takeaway
In controlled paired experiments, incidental small talk and background speech contaminated generated clinical notes while general quality scores often changed little; attention-head interventions separately supported a preliminary link between distraction sensitivity and medical-question reasoning.

## Problem and motivation
Clinical documentation models must distinguish patient-relevant information from small talk and nearby conversations. Incorrect attribution can turn another person’s condition or a figurative remark into unsupported patient history, assessment, or planning.

The authors identify a gap between prior medical-examination distraction studies and clinical documentation reliability: conventional quality or hallucination checks may miss information that appears in the transcript but does not describe the patient. The study tests this gap across text documentation, audio transcription, and a separate reasoning probe (Introduction; Discussion).

## Approach and contributions
- **Paired documentation benchmarks:** MedDistractNotes adds bystander or nonliteral exchanges to clinical transcripts without changing patient information. MedDistractAudio overlays speech from separate mock consultations and traces contamination through transcription into notes (Fig. 1; Methods).
- **Contamination-specific evaluation:** Matched clean controls, target-specific judging, attribution classification, and comparison with general note-quality scores distinguish recording an aside from assigning it to the patient or using it clinically (Methods: Contamination judging; Attribution).
- **Mechanistic probe:** Contrastive causal head gating (CCHG) selects attention heads contributing to clean–distracted answer-probability differences. Clean-activation patching and suppression test their roles against size-matched random-head controls. The authors propose a **dual-encoding hypothesis**, rather than establishing a documentation mechanism (Fig. 4; Discussion).
- **Planned assets:** Benchmark inputs, generated notes, judgments, and construction/evaluation code are promised for release; the text does not confirm their availability. MedDistractQA is stated to be available (Data availability; Code availability).

The paired design supports effects of these specific insertions on model outputs. It does **not** establish real-world contamination prevalence, clinician acceptance, actual clinical actions, or patient harm. The mechanistic results concern examination questions; transfer to note generation remains untested. Transcript word leakage alone also does not establish a clinically consequential error.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Synthetic exchanges and audio overlays incompletely represent routine clinical conversation and acoustics. Short excerpts dominate the pooled text results and may inflate estimates relative to full visits (Discussion).
- **Author-stated:** Contamination and attribution rely on one LLM judge without clinician validation. Failure modes use automated rules; severity scores were not clinically validated (Discussion; Extended Data Fig. 2).
- **Author-stated:** Mechanistic testing excludes general-knowledge benchmarks, reverse patching, and note-generation interventions. CCHG can select generally useful reasoning heads by lowering clean-answer probability; selected fractions depend on its penalty parameter (Discussion; Methods: CCHG).
- **Analyst inference:** Clean-activation patching demonstrates an intervention effect under access to a clean counterpart, but does not establish a deployable safeguard. The exploratory learned correction changed held-out accuracy by at most 0.8 percentage points (Methods).
- **Statistical qualification:** Primary text confidence intervals resample note pairs without clustering all outputs from an encounter; encounter-level sensitivity analyses are referenced. Patching and suppression tests use uncorrected significance values, and cross-model comparisons are descriptive (Methods).

## Open research questions
- **Do clinicians detect these errors before signing?** Acceptance and harm were not measured. A blinded prospective review could compare detection and retained-error rates for clean and contaminated notes.
- **How frequent is contamination in routine full-length visits?** Synthetic perturbations and excerpt-heavy pooling limit external validity. Evaluate naturally occurring incidental speech in full encounters with clinician-adjudicated attribution.
- **Do CCHG-selected heads cause documentation errors?** Mechanistic transfer remains untested. Apply patching, reverse patching, and suppression during note generation while measuring contamination and clinical completeness.
- **Can safeguards preserve reasoning while improving attribution?** Prompting was inconsistent and suppression impaired clean accuracy. Test speaker/subject/relevance annotations or attribution-focused training jointly on documentation contamination and clean medical-QA performance.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in summarizing the reported controlled experiments; **medium** in the proposed mechanism and clinical generalization.
