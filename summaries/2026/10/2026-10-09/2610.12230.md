# From Prompting to Composing: A Spatial Canvas Interface for Poster Generation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-09.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.12230) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12230)


## One-sentence takeaway
Compo adapts image-editing models to a canvas combining spatial, semantic, identity, text, and pixel constraints, achieving stronger automated binding adherence than the tested baselines on a synthetic poster benchmark (Table 1).

## Problem and motivation
Poster design requires intentional placement of subjects, text, logos, and preserved visual assets. The authors argue that expressing these constraints through a single text prompt is indirect and ambiguous, while existing controllable generation methods offer restricted reference types or separate control modules. Their proposed gap is a unified interface connecting heterogeneous constraints to explicit spatial regions (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Spatial Canvas Interface:** A single canvas combines semantic regions, identity references, exact text locations, and preserved visual content. Accompanying Text Specifications describe individual elements, background, and style (Fig. 1; §3).
- **Compo adaptation:** LongCat-Image-Edit and Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 are adapted through LoRA supervised training, followed by DiffusionNFT reinforcement learning with text, instance-similarity, and layout rewards. The backbone architecture remains unchanged (§3.2; App. B).
- **Structured supervision and evaluation:** An automated pipeline generates posters, segments instances, processes references, and recognizes text to construct Compo-200K and a separate binding-focused benchmark (§3.1; App. A).
- **Two inference modes:** Users can construct the canvas directly, or a GPT-5.6 planner/verifier can select elements, plan regions, assign bindings, and resolve references from a high-level request (§3.3; App. B.3).

The contributions center on the unified interface, its supervision pipeline, and binding-specific adaptation and evaluation; the diffusion backbones, LoRA, and RL algorithm are adopted components.

## Evidence and main results
All quantitative comparisons below use the paper’s 500-sample synthetic benchmark, constructed without training-set overlap (App. A.2).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Highest reported aggregate adherence | Overall Avg. Score: Compo-LongCat **0.752**, Compo-Qwen **0.751**, versus **0.504** for HiDream-O1-Image, the highest-scoring baseline. Overall IoU: **0.885**, **0.886**, and **0.547**, respectively. | Table 1; §4.1 |
| Stronger text binding than tested baselines | Compo-LongCat reports NED **0.970**, sentence accuracy **0.940**, and text IoU **0.944**; CreatiDesign, the strongest baseline on these text metrics, reports **0.515**, **0.329**, and **0.670**. Higher NED means better fidelity under the paper’s definition. | Table 1; §4 |
| RL improves aggregate scores on both backbones | LongCat Avg. Score rises from **0.725** after supervised training to **0.752** after RL; Qwen rises from **0.726** to **0.751**. Gains are not universal: Qwen pixel SSIM falls from **0.785** to **0.782**. | Table 5; App. C.1 |
| Reward ablation shows a text–visual tradeoff | Removing the text reward lowers LongCat sentence accuracy from **0.940** to **0.778**, while semantic IoU increases from **0.828** to **0.884**. | Table 2; §4.2 |
| Combining strict and continuous text rewards helps exact correctness | Sentence-accuracy reward weight **0.75** yields sentence accuracy **0.940**, versus **0.916** with weight **0** and **0.938** with weight **1**. | Table 3; §4.2 |
| Both LoRA branches give the best aggregate supervised score | Before RL, Avg. Score is **0.725** when adapting both branches, **0.721** for image-only, and **0.722** for text-only adaptation. | Table 4; §4.2 |
| Unseen-theme generalization is claimed qualitatively | Authors report coherent examples at varied resolutions and on themes absent from training; no quantitative generalization comparison accompanies them. | Fig. 8; App. C.2 |

These results establish strong adherence under the authors’ automated evaluation protocol. Failed IoU matches receive zero scores, so similarity and text metrics also depend on successful localization (§4). The aggregate score averages heterogeneous metrics; it is not a direct measure of aesthetic quality. Visual-quality and generalization claims rely on qualitative examples, and the supplied text does not establish human preference, interface usability, or end-to-end agentic reliability.

## Limitations and validity risks
The authors provide no dedicated limitations discussion. They explicitly identify sparse exact-text rewards during early RL and reduce Qwen RL rollout resolution to alleviate memory overhead (§4.2; App. B.2).

**Analyst inferences grounded in the text:**

- **Shared synthetic distribution:** Training and evaluation follow the same generation pipeline, although samples do not overlap and benchmark instance counts are larger. Performance on human-designed posters or independently sourced compositions remains untested (App. A).
- **Evaluator dependence:** SAM-3 and Chandra-OCR-2 support annotation, RL rewards, and evaluation. Their errors could influence measured gains; no independent human audit of binding scores is reported (§4; App. B.2).
- **Restricted typography:** Data-generation prompts favor short English copy, horizontal text, and OCR-friendly presentation. Multilingual, rotated, dense, or unusual typography is not established (App. D.1–D.2).
- **Comparison constraints:** Dedicated poster systems receive only information supported by their native protocols. This limits attribution of the performance gap solely to model quality (§4.1).
- **Incomplete component isolation:** Reward and LoRA ablations are reported, but no controlled canvas-versus-text-only study, data-scale ablation, or quantitative agentic-mode evaluation is supplied (§4.2; App. B.3).
- **Reproducibility gaps:** The matching threshold, training duration, and evaluation sampling settings are unspecified. Sentence-accuracy aggregation is explicit for RL but less explicit for benchmark evaluation (§4; App. B).

## Open research questions
1. **Does the interface improve human design work?** No user study measures control, effort, or revision success. Next experiment: compare canvas and text-only workflows on matched poster briefs, measuring completion time and independently judged constraint satisfaction.
2. **Do gains transfer beyond the synthetic pipeline?** Unseen-theme evidence is qualitative. Next experiment: evaluate on independently annotated human-designed layouts, including multilingual and unconventional typography.
3. **How reliable is agentic generation end to end?** Planning and reference verification are described without stage-level success rates. Next experiment: score extraction, layout, reference identity, and final binding adherence separately on high-level requests.
4. **How sensitive are gains to automated evaluators?** Training and evaluation share detection and similarity tools. Next experiment: compare scores against blinded human annotations and vary the IoU matching threshold.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The method and benchmark results are well documented, but broader effectiveness remains uncertain.

Material missing information includes artifact release status and licenses, exact matching thresholds, training steps and costs, baseline sampling configurations, repeated-run uncertainty, independent annotation audits, quantitative aesthetic evaluation, user studies, and agentic-mode success rates.
