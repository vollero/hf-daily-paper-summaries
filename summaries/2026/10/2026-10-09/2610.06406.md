# What Did the Agent Actually Do? Evidence-Grounded Oversight for Long-Horizon Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-09.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.06406) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06406)


## One-sentence takeaway
EBG’s deterministic organization of source-linked behavioral evidence improves monitoring across most tested software-engineering settings, but decision interpretation and unnecessary alerts remain substantial obstacles (Table 1; Appendix E.5; Appendix G).

## Problem and motivation
Long-horizon agents make consequential choices whose evidence is scattered across code, messages, and tool results, making user oversight difficult. The authors identify a gap between evaluating task success or seeking clarification and identifying specific autonomous decisions, their implications, and the evidence users need to verify them—even when existing tests pass (Sections 1–3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Monitoring formulation:** Identify consequential decisions requiring user verification and connect them to evidence, covering both requirement–behavior alignment and awareness of autonomous choices (Sections 1, 3).
- **AgentMonBench:** Three software-engineering subsets: SpecGAP removes documented requirements; SilentSwap introduces semantic changes that preserve existing test outcomes; FeedbackTrace derives verification targets from later real-user feedback withheld from monitor input. Each subset contains 100 examples (Section 3; Table B1).
- **Evidence-Grounded Behavior Graph:** A training-free pipeline groups evidence into behaviors, scopes, and explicit relations. Repository views use task-guided ranking and graph expansion; trajectory views organize demand–action–response evidence by task while preserving event positions. Construction and presentation are deterministic; an LLM monitor makes the semantic judgments (Section 4; Appendix D).

The authors also demonstrate a Codex review harness in controlled research-task cases; this illustrates integration rather than establishing broad deployment effectiveness (Section 5.2.3; Appendix F).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Localization improves consistently in the main evaluation | Across eight monitors on the same benchmark instances, EBG exceeds Base on SpecGAP LF1, SilentSwap LS, and FeedbackTrace EHR; it also exceeds adapted RepoGraph on both repository localization metrics | Table 1; Section 5.1 |
| Identification gains are substantial for some monitors | Kimi K3 on SpecGAP: semantic F1 rises from **0.494 Base to 0.558 EBG**, and LF1 from **0.497 to 0.603**; semantic scores average two judges | Table 1 |
| Better retrieval does not guarantee better interpretation | DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash on SpecGAP: LF1 rises **0.459→0.469**, while semantic F1 falls **0.376→0.372**. GPT-5.6 Terra on FeedbackTrace: EHR rises **0.540→0.580**, while VPA falls **0.513→0.508** | Table 1 |
| Average localization gains persist at larger input sizes | High input-token tertiles, averaging Luna, Kimi K3, and GLM-5.3: reported absolute gains are **+0.078 LF1**, **+0.115 LS**, and **+0.108 EHR** over Base | Figure 5; Appendix E.3 |
| Components contribute, with some exceptions | Every applicable behavior, graph, and guidance ablation reduces its task’s representative metric—SF1, LS, or ESS—for Luna, Kimi K3, and GLM-5.3; some other metrics increase slightly | Table E4; Appendix E.2 |
| False alarms remain severe for some settings | Balanced disclosure sets contain **50 positive and 50 negative examples per component**. Luna with EBG reaches **94% TPR but 84% FPR** on FeedbackTrace; on SilentSwap its FPR increases **52%→58%** | Table E6; Appendix E.5 |
| Most audited failures occur with reference evidence visible | Under GLM-5.2 scoring for three monitors, judgment-stage failures account for **74.0–81.2%** of SpecGAP failures and **69.4–76.9%** of SilentSwap localization failures; all audited FeedbackTrace failures fall in this category | Table G1; Appendix G |
| Harness cases illustrate useful interventions | Five paired tasks, each run once with and once without the harness, show clarification, randomization correction, leakage detection, qualification of API verification, and recognition of unequal search budgets | Table 2; Appendix F.3 |

These results support the authors’ claim that evidence organization helps under the tested conditions. They do not establish reliable oversight, improved human decision-making, or generalization beyond the evaluated domains. Input-size comparisons use different examples grouped by length, so they do not isolate a causal effect of context length (Appendix E.3). Scores are descriptive; uncertainty intervals and statistical significance tests are not supplied (Appendix C.3).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ reported risks:** Evidence may be omitted during file selection or presentation, and monitors frequently fail despite seeing the reference evidence. The failure audit does not resolve which expansion, root-selection, or rendering rule causes presentation omissions; SilentSwap analysis covers localization failures only (Appendix G).
- **Judge sensitivity:** Semantic judgments differ between the two judges, including reversals in some monitor-specific EBG effects. Averaging judges reduces reliance on one evaluator but does not establish human agreement with the scores (Appendix E.1).
- **Analyst inference—restricted task structure:** SilentSwap monitors are told there are exactly five substitutions. FeedbackTrace retains sessions with one qualifying decision and requires one predicted point. This simplifies discovery compared with streams containing unknown numbers of decisions (Appendices B.4, D.5).
- **Analyst inference—limited isolation:** The graph ablation removes scopes and edges together, leaving their individual contributions unresolved. Harness comparisons change hooks, review skills, and evidence tools together, so they do not isolate EBG’s contribution (Appendices E.2, F).
- **Analyst inference—limited practical validation:** The five harness cases are controlled adaptations with one run per condition. They cannot establish repeatability, user-attention savings, or the quality of human verification (Appendix F.3).
- **Analyst inference—resource comparability:** Methods share budget caps, but actual token usage can differ. Matched caps therefore do not establish matched cost or latency (Appendix D.4).

## Open research questions
1. **How can interpretation improve when sufficient evidence is already visible?** This dominates the failure audit. A next experiment could hold evidence fixed while varying presentation and monitor reasoning procedures, as the authors suggest (Appendix G.4).
2. **Can disclosure be calibrated to realistic alert prevalence?** The supplemental evaluation uses balanced positive and negative sets and still finds high false-positive rates. Test natural execution streams with varying decision prevalence and measure missed decisions, false alerts, and user review time (Appendix E.5).
3. **Which graph structures actually drive gains?** Scopes and edges are ablated jointly. Compare scope-only views against views with typed edges while controlling evidence content and token budget (Appendix E.2).
4. **Does the harness improve human oversight reliably?** Existing evidence is limited to selected paired cases. Run repeated trials and a user study measuring verification accuracy, intervention quality, and attention cost (Appendix F.3).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the paper’s method description and reported benchmark results; **medium** in claims of practical oversight benefit.

Material information unavailable in the supplied text includes statistical uncertainty, repeated-run variability, measured token cost and latency, human-user outcomes, and evidence of generalization beyond software engineering and the selected research cases. Code and dataset release links are supplied, but their contents and reproducibility were not independently verified.
