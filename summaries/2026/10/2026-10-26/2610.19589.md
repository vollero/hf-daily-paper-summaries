# JockeyBench: Evaluating LLM Agents on Horse-Racing Decision-Making

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.19589) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19589)


## One-sentence takeaway
JockeyBench separates forecasting quality, revision gains, research costs, and retrospective betting value, finding useful predictive signals but negative mean capped-Kelly profit for every evaluated configuration on its shared historical window (§4).

## Problem and motivation
Repeated forecasting requires agents to acquire evidence, build predictors, and revise them as outcomes arrive. The authors argue that prediction scores alone cannot establish whether revision helps or whether forecasts produce value after information costs and decision risk. Horse racing supplies repeated events, structured historical records, and verifiable outcomes for studying these distinctions (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled replay benchmark:** A cleaned, linked Japanese racing archive exposes factual fields through priced tools, with historical availability cut-offs, pseudonymous identities, sealed forecasts, and delayed outcomes. Odds and expert ratings are withheld from agents (§3.1).
- **Two forecasting workflows:** **Program** executes an LLM-written predictor; **Adaptive Acquisition** executes an LLM-written evidence collector before a fresh per-race LLM forecast. Program revision is evaluated against the identical initial predictor held frozen (§§3.2–3.3).
- **Separated evaluation:** Delivery, probability quality, revision, synthetic net utility, betting returns, and risk are measured separately. Decision-subset diagnostics reveal differences that aggregate forecast summaries conceal (§§3.4, 4).

The contribution is primarily the benchmark and combined evaluation protocol, rather than a new forecasting model.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong forecasts need not improve through revision | Across 28 Program configurations, 138 matched pairs, and 324 races over ten days, seven revision gains and two declines survive Holm correction. The two highest-scoring configurations show zero revision gain. | Table 2; §4.1.1 |
| Best agent performance depends on reference strength | On the main window, GPT-6.1-Sol achieves mean smoothed log gain **0.525 nats** relative to uniform, versus **0.343** for support-fitted card/history R2, **0.608** for the broad-factual offline ensemble, and **0.757** for closing-market probabilities. These references have different training resources and inputs. | Tables 2–3; §A.4 |
| Large revision gains can leave a weak predictor | Nemotron-3-Ultra-550B improves by **0.359 nats**, with pointwise day-clustered 95% CI **[0.220, 0.498]**, but its revised mean remains **−0.083 nats**, below uniform. | Table 2 |
| Forecast signal does not establish betting profitability | Every Program configuration has negative mean capped-Kelly profit; **13 of 138** individual trajectories finish profitable. Accounts start at **JPY 100,000**, with stakes capped at **1% of day-start bankroll per race** and settlement at official dividends. | Figure 1; §§4.2, A.5.4, B.3 |
| Program leads the observed workflow comparison | Across four models on the same main window, Program has higher mean terminal betting P&L under both flat-WIN and capped-Kelly rules, lower research fees, and higher common net scores. Both workflows lose on average. | §4.2; Figure 3; §§B.3–B.4 |
| Decision selection changes the probability-bias picture | For DeepSeek Adaptive Acquisition, mean saved probability versus observed wins is **18.14% versus 21.25%** across all non-refunded top picks, but **17.83% versus 7.82%** among Kelly-selected bets, pooled across completed trajectories. | §4.2; §B.3.1 |
| Costs reverse one predictive-reward advantage | Per completed 324-race trajectory, DeepSeek Adaptive Acquisition earns approximately **1,000 more score-yen** gross reward than Program but incurs **4,551 more score-yen** research fees under the common tariff. | §4.2; §B.4 |

These results support separating prediction, adaptation, spending, and decision outcomes. They do not establish a general causal advantage for Program, a reproducible profitable strategy, or that selected-bet probability bias causes losses. Synthetic score-yen, provider charges, and betting P&L are distinct accounting quantities.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ scope limits:** Agent evaluation covers one shared historical window in structured Japanese racing. Additional periods evaluate reference predictors, not additional agent trajectories; cross-domain and temporal generalization remain untested (§B.1; Limitations).
- **Comparisons combine multiple changes:** Program revision includes feedback, additional analysis, data access, and debugging. Workflow comparisons also change the information interface; offline references vary training data, features, and fitting methods (§§4.1.1, B.2.1).
- **Small, unequal workflow cohorts:** Adaptive Acquisition has six, one, two, and one completed trajectories across the four models. Repeats share outcomes, and unequal counts affect smoothing of mean-path risk measures (§§4.1.2, A.3, A.5.5).
- **Retrospective financial validity:** The staking rule was selected retrospectively; original quote publication times were not independently verified. Risk statistics describe mean P&L paths rather than typical individual-account risk (§§A.5.4–A.5.5).
- **Cost and reproducibility limits:** Only one tariff is studied; Net is not strictly proper and excludes provider charges. Immutable model identifiers and detailed token accounting are incomplete, while full reproduction requires authorized archive access and additional artifacts (§§3.4, A.3, E).
- **Analyst inference:** With only ten day clusters, uncertainty about performance across future periods remains material despite corrected paired tests (§A.5.6).

## Open research questions
1. **Do revision gains persist across time?** Repeats reuse historical outcomes. Run matched revised/frozen agents across multiple disjoint seasons (§§5, A.5.5).
2. **Which revision components help?** Current controls do not isolate outcome feedback from debugging or additional research. Compare matched feedback, no-feedback revision, and debugging-only conditions (§B.2.1).
3. **Do acquisition policies respond usefully to prices?** Alternative tariffs remain unexplored. Randomize evidence and computation prices while holding models and race windows fixed (Limitations; §B.4).
4. **Can decision-conditioned calibration improve returns?** Selected-bet mean gaps are descriptive. Fit calibration only on earlier data, then prospectively compare frozen selection and staking rules on disjoint windows (§B.3.1).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported descriptive conclusions; causal and generalization claims remain limited.

The supplied text omits the bodies of several appendix tables, the complete field list, rendered prompts, and case-study code. It also lacks complete immutable model metadata and provider accounting. Public access to the underlying archive and full reproduction artifacts is not established (§§A.3, C, E; Ethics Statement).
