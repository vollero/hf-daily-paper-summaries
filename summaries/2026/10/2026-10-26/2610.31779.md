# FLORA: An Open Model Suite for Scaling Robotics Data Engines

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.31779) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31779)


## One-sentence takeaway
FLORA combines specialized video captioning and reward models for robotics annotation, reporting strong temporal localization and failure recognition, while its approximately 125× cost advantage over GPT-6 Astra rests on modeled deployment costs.

## Problem and motivation
Robot foundation models need timestamped action captions for pretraining, process rewards for post-training, and outcome labels for deployment monitoring. The authors identify a gap between systems covering individual annotation types and expensive general-purpose models that support several types at scale (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Integrated annotation suite:** A Qwen3.5-based segmenter, captioner, and reward model jointly cover timestamped intended actions, execution quality, episode outcomes, and user-defined rubric scores (§§4.1–4.3).
- **Temporal captioning pipeline:** Overlapping windows predict action fragments and continuation flags; stitching reconstructs intervals, which receive captions using before/target/after visual context. SFT is followed by GRPO for segmentation and captioning (§4.1; Appendix F).
- **Unified reward model:** One SFT-only model handles outcome, retrospective dense-quality, and rubric grading through different prompts. Dense labels distinguish normal, suboptimal, and failure steps (§§4.2–4.3; Appendix G).
- **Expanded evaluation:** DenseCaptionBench adds human, parallel-gripper, and dexterous-hand recordings with partial-credit caption scoring. Internal DenseRewardBench and RubricBench assess localized execution errors and rubric grading (§§2.1–2.2). The authors describe FLORA as open-sourced; the supplied text does not establish benchmark release availability.

## Evidence and main results

All results below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong WGO localization, weaker caption semantics | On 100 single-view episodes, equal-subset mean segmentation F1@0.75 is **50.3%**, versus Astra’s **46.0%**; joint F1 is **34.5% vs. 39.0%**, and gold-window caption accuracy **64.7% vs. 77.4%**. | §2.1; Table 2 |
| Human captioning leads; robot results trail Astra | DenseCaptionBench: Human100 segmentation/oracle-quality/joint scores are **54.6/66.5/37.4%**, leading displayed systems. Robot200 averages are **66.9/73.7/51.0%**, versus Astra’s **71.9/80.2/57.9%**. Temporal matching uses tIoU ≥0.5. | Table 3 |
| Pipeline ranking depends on backbone | On WGO, Refiner with Gemini 3.8 Flash reaches **36.4% joint F1**, above FLORA’s **34.5%**; FLORA retains higher segmentation F1, **50.3% vs. 42.0%**. | Appendix B.6; Table 9 |
| Better outcome-score accuracy | On 2,831 RoboRewardBench episodes scored 1–5, MAE×100 is **40.0**, versus Astra’s **55.4** and RoboReward’s **66.5**; the reported reduction versus Astra is **27.8%**. | §2.2; Table 4 |
| Strong reward ranking and progress ordering | RobometerBench OOD Kendall τa×100 is **72.0 vs. Astra’s 82.3** on 567 trajectories. FLORA’s ID/OOD VOC×100 is **98.9/99.6**, versus **98.9/99.7** for Astra. | Table 4; Appendix C |
| Higher failure recall with false alarms | Across 4,861 evaluated steps from 40 held-out bimanual episodes, macro F1 is **46.2% vs. Astra’s 40.7%**; failure recall is **53.3%**, while no baseline exceeds **11.7%**. About **11% of normal steps** are labeled failures. | §2.2; Table 5; Appendix C |
| Rubric grading remains behind Astra | On 40 teleoperated episodes across 10 tasks, pooled L1×100 is **6.5 vs. Astra’s 3.6**. FLORA uses nine-sample medoid aggregation and denser video sampling; baselines use single samples. | Table 5; Appendix C |
| Large conditional cost advantage | On a fixed 50-episode captioning subset, aggregate quality is **57.1% vs. 53.7%** for maximum-effort Astra. Estimated FLORA cost is **$0.7224/source-video hour**, versus Astra’s **$88.4922** known-usage subtotal: **122.49×**, summarized as approximately **125×**. | §2.3; Appendices D.1–D.2 |

These results support FLORA’s usefulness as an annotation system, particularly for temporal localization, partial-outcome scoring, and offline failure labeling. They do not establish uniform superiority across captioning and reward tasks, improved downstream robot policies, or measured serving economics. The claimed production scale of up to 200,000 video hours/day on 512 GB300 GPUs is reported in §1 without a corresponding measurement audit in the supplied text.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ evaluation caveats:** Systems differ in visual processing, prompts, reasoning, frame budgets, and recovery attempts. Specialized reward baselines retain published protocols; some evaluate different trajectory populations (Appendices B–C).
- **Incomplete measurements:** DenseCaptionBench robot tracks contain missing caption generation and unavailable judgments. Fixed-denominator scoring assigns no operational credit, so rankings are not fully observed quality orderings; some ordering changes on mutually observed episodes (Appendices B.4, E).
- **Judge and reference dependence:** DenseCaptionBench uses Astra as both judge and evaluated generator, creating acknowledged self-preference risk. Sampled review does not establish exact transition times or continuous visual coverage; some robot references are observed-operation proxies (Appendices B.1, B.3).
- **Limited interpretation of progress:** VOC rewards temporal ordering on successful trajectories; linear elapsed-time progress scores perfectly. It therefore does not demonstrate failure detection (Appendix C).
- **Cost uncertainty:** Serving rates are modeled, the production GPU-hour denominator is unverified, API usage accounting is incomplete, and quality comparisons use different reference snapshots. Deployment overheads are excluded (Appendix D).
- **Observed errors and scope:** FLORA can over-segment coarse events and confuse action phases or objects. Dense rewards use future evidence and are intended for offline use (Appendix E; §4.2).
- **Analyst inference:** The supplied results do not isolate gains from training data, GRPO, stitching, and context. API substitution diagnostics are informative but do not establish a workflow-only advantage (Appendix B.6).

## Open research questions
1. **Do better annotations improve robot learning?** The paper reports annotation benchmarks and use in GR00T training, but no controlled policy gains. A next experiment could compare policy training with FLORA, human, and API-generated labels under fixed data and training budgets.
2. **Which components drive the gains?** Component contributions remain unresolved. Ablating GRPO, temporal stitching, and neighboring context with a shared backbone and matched inputs would help isolate them.
3. **Can failure recall improve without excessive false alarms?** FLORA’s higher recall comes with normal-step misclassification. Evaluate calibration and decision thresholds on held-out embodiments and failure types.
4. **Does the cost advantage survive measured deployment?** The reported economics depend on serving assumptions. Measure end-to-end throughput, utilization, and complete costs for the evaluated checkpoints under matched reference versions and quality scoring.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text provides detailed methods, results, and explicit caveats, but incomplete measurements and unequal protocols constrain comparisons.

Material missing information includes training-set sizes and mixture proportions, complete optimization hyperparameters and compute budgets, release locations and licenses, benchmark release status, comprehensive train–test contamination checks, controlled component ablations, downstream policy results, and measured serving costs for the evaluated checkpoints.
