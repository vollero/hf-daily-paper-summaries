# Kolab: Benchmarking Human-Agent Collaboration in Long-Horizon Workflows

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.21109) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21109)


## One-sentence takeaway
Kolab benchmarks whether multimodal models can recognize and forecast professionals’ use of agent outputs and predict their collaboration ratings, finding weak forecasting and rating agreement despite richer workflow context (§§5.2–5.4).

## Problem and motivation
Professional collaboration involves changing plans, intermediate artifacts, and decisions made outside an agent’s chat interface. The authors argue that autonomous task benchmarks miss whether agents understand how humans subsequently use their contributions. Existing collaborative datasets also offer limited coverage of publicly available, self-defined professional workflows across domains (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Dataset:** Approximately 115 hours of recordings from 50 professionals across 40 occupations, covering 100 workflows and 508 steps. Released components include videos, chat logs, workflow documents, ratings, annotated graphs, and optional task files (§§1, 3).
- **Workflow representation and analysis:** Human-reviewed temporal graphs link requests, agent outputs, and subsequent Adopt, Modify, or Reject responses, producing 692 benchmark instances. Analyses characterize interaction burstiness, concurrent work, and output retention (§§4.1–4.3).
- **Benchmark:** Recognition of observed adoption behavior; forecasting before the human response, with varying history; and prediction of collaboration satisfaction and output quality. Experiments compare video, graph, and chat inputs and their combinations (§5.1).
- **Empirical findings:** Models exhibit label preferences and compressed rating predictions; additional context and modalities provide inconsistent benefits (§§5.2–5.4). The contribution is an evaluation resource and analysis, rather than a new collaborative agent.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Forecasting remains substantially weaker than recognition | Gemini 3.1 Pro video-only macro-F1: recognition **0.617 ± 0.007**, forecasting **0.391 ± 0.013**, on the standard 692-instance evaluation. | Tables 11–12; §5.1 |
| Forecasting rejection is particularly difficult | GPT-6 Astra video-only forecasting: macro-F1 **0.513 ± 0.003**, with Reject F1 **0.235 ± 0.070**. Its evaluation used fewer instances; the common forecasting set contains 349 valid outputs. | Table 12; §§5.1, A.11 |
| Longer history offers inconsistent benefits | On 114 last-step instances, no video-context improvement was statistically significant. Gemini 3.1 Pro chat forecasting improved by **0.12 F1** with three preceding steps (**p < 0.01**), but the gain did not persist with full-workflow history. | §5.2; Fig. 4 |
| Curated graphs can improve forecasting | Qwen3-VL-32B macro-F1 increased from **0.235 ± 0.010** with video to **0.370 ± 0.014** with video plus graph. | Table 12 |
| Video-only rating prediction poorly separates professional judgments | Gemini 3.1 Pro satisfaction MAE was **0.685 ± 0.006**, versus the reported mode baseline **0.682**; its Spearman correlation was **0.078 ± 0.057**. | §5.2; Table 13 |
| The mode-baseline conclusion needs a modality qualification | Although §5.2 says the mode baseline outperforms every model, multimodal results report lower MAE: Gemini Flash with all inputs achieved satisfaction MAE **0.589 ± 0.011**, below **0.682**; video plus graph achieved output-quality MAE **0.643 ± 0.003**, below **0.735**. | §5.2; Tables 13–14 |
| Forecasts show strong label preferences | With video, Gemini Flash predicted Adopt for **83.8%** of cases versus **65.9%** ground truth; Qwen-32B predicted Modify for **87.3%** versus **19.1%** ground truth. | §5.4; Fig. 7 |
| Concurrent work correlates with lower satisfaction | Workflow branching correlated negatively with collaboration ratings: **Spearman ρ = −0.24, p < 0.05**. | §4.3; Fig. 3b |

These results establish difficulties in interpreting recorded collaboration and predicting professional judgments. They do not demonstrate that improving these prediction metrics improves live collaboration or final deliverables. Rankings across models also require caution because evaluation subsets differ. The baseline finding supports a video-only conclusion more clearly than an across-modality conclusion.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ scope restriction:** Workflow analyses describe this sample; they are not population-level comparisons between professions (§4.3).
- **Protocol effects — analyst inference:** Required Claude use, advance planning, think-aloud recording, and encouraged interaction frequency may influence collaboration patterns relative to ordinary work (§A.2).
- **Unequal comparisons:** GPT-6 uses reduced evaluation sets. Claude recognition uses a balanced subset, while its rating subsets deliberately retain lower ratings. Common-instance comparison tables are referenced, but their numerical contents are absent (§§A.8, A.11).
- **Input differences:** GPT-6 and Claude receive sampled frames, whereas Gemini accepts native video. Model and input-processing effects therefore remain entangled (§A.7; analyst inference).
- **Annotation ambiguity:** Main-text definitions allow Reject for non-incorporation, while §A.4.1 requires explicit rejection. No inter-annotator agreement statistic is supplied.
- **Rating validity — analyst inference:** Professionals’ self-ratings establish perceived satisfaction and quality; the supplied experiments do not establish independent deliverable correctness.
- **Graph practicality:** Graphs required substantial manual reconstruction after frequent automatic extraction errors; their benefits do not establish a scalable deployment pipeline (§§A.5–A.5.1).
- **Reporting inconsistency:** The broad mode-baseline claim conflicts with lower multimodal MAEs in the appendix tables (§5.2; Tables 13–14).

## Open research questions
1. **Do better forecasts improve live collaboration?** The benchmark evaluates predictions, without testing forecast-driven interventions. A next experiment could randomize clarification or revision prompts triggered by predicted rejection and measure professional satisfaction and deliverable quality.
2. **Can automatic graphs retain the benefits of curated graphs?** Current graphs require extensive correction (§A.5). Compare automatic, corrected, and absent graphs on identical instances, measuring both prediction performance and annotation effort.
3. **How much do collection protocols shape observed behavior?** Required agent use and interaction encouragement constrain naturalism (§A.2). Compare matched tasks under unrestricted use and the original protocols.
4. **Can models distinguish poor experiences without relying on rating prevalence?** Compressed predictions and positive rating skew leave this unresolved (§§3, 5.4). Evaluate calibration and ranking on held-out professionals with separately reported performance across rating bands.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the dataset design and central findings, but comparison and reporting issues limit stronger conclusions.

Material missing information includes numerical contents of several summary and common-instance tables; actual evaluation prompt text and detailed inference settings despite appendix headings; frame-sampling density; annotation agreement; and independent assessments of deliverable correctness. The reported public release was not independently verified.
