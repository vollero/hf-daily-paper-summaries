# DirCast: Live Esports Directing and Commentary with a Single Streaming Video-Language Model

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.31734) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31734)


## One-sentence takeaway
DirCast jointly selects cameras and generates commentary from causal Dota 2 feeds, improving human-director agreement and automated commentary scores over the tested baselines while demonstrating bounded-memory streaming inference.

## Problem and motivation
Existing streaming commentators consume broadcasts whose shots have already been selected by human directors. The authors instead study **live broadcast production**: choosing what viewers see and commenting on that choice using only currently available and past information (§1).

This couples camera selection with language generation: missed action changes what the commentator can describe, and directing mistakes affect subsequent context. Processing every camera indefinitely also creates memory costs, while imbalanced human demonstrations encourage excessive holding and silence (§§1, 4).

## Approach and contributions
- **Task and dataset:** A collection of professional Dota 2 matches pairs ten replay-rendered hero cameras with inferred human camera choices, timestamped commentary, hero states, and events. Matches are separated into training, calibration, and test splits (§§3, 7).
- **Unified directing and commentary:** A Qwen3-VL-8B model scans low-resolution feeds, makes a hold-or-switch decision with a camera pointer, then reads the selected feed at higher resolution before generating commentary (§§4.1–4.2, 5.1).
- **Selective streaming memory:** Unselected low-resolution views expire quickly; aired views, events, and commentary remain within a bounded history. A finite horizon also removes older program context (§4.3).
- **Training and decoding adaptations:** Perturbed program histories expose the model to incorrect shots; event-anchored targets accept relevant alternative cameras; adaptive thresholds regulate cutting and speaking toward training-set human rates (§§4.4–4.6).

## Evidence and main results

Unless otherwise noted, results use four held-out matches comprising 496 clips; directing evaluation uses 11,283 strict live seconds (§5.1).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Best tested human-feed agreement | DirCast Top-1 **27.0%** and Any-visible **41.8%**, versus action rule **25.4% / 36.1%** and GPT-5 **13.5% / 24.8%**; DirCast averages three seeds. | Table 1 |
| Strong kill coverage, mixed fight coverage | Kill coverage **89.1%**, versus action rule **78.5%** and human director **50.3%**. Fight coverage **41.1%**, below action rule **62.4%**. DirCast cuts **6.7/min**, versus human **7.9/min**. | Table 1 |
| Higher commentary comparison score | Under the Proact-VL protocol with GPT-5 judging, DirCast wins **29.21%** against offline GPT-4o commentary, versus strongest baseline LiveCC-7B-Base **19.38%**. Baselines watch human broadcasts; DirCast selects its own shots. | Table 2 |
| Commentary gains do not cover every metric | DirCast TimeDiff **0.30 s** and PAUC **0.024**, versus best baseline values **0.34 s / 0.015**. Its speaking F1 **78.22** trails Proact-VL Qwen3 **83.21**. | Table 2 |
| Joint training improves both roles in the tested comparison | Joint versus separate role-specific models: Top-1 **27.00 / 25.17**, commentary win rate **29.21% / 24.36%**, memory **18.9 / 37.8 GB**. On identical human programs, joint commentary scores **27.84%** versus **25.67%**. | Table 3 |
| Directing benefits from architectural and training components | Removing hold-or-switch reduces Top-1 from **27.00** to **21.65**; removing state tokens or program rollouts gives **22.59 / 22.90**. Removing event targets increases Any-visible from **41.80** to **43.06**, showing a trade-off. | Table 4 |
| Bounded context supports measured real-time compute | Across eight **288-second** windows on one GPU, program memory with a **36-second** horizon uses **18.9 GB**, with mean/P95 compute **0.29/0.52 s per video second**; retaining every view uses **32.0 GB** and **0.60/1.05 s**. | Table 5 |

These results support feasibility and gains under the supplied evaluation. They do not establish overall broadcast superiority to human crews: feed agreement remains limited, the action rule covers more fight seconds, and commentary quality is evaluated by a model judge. The reported commentary win rate is against GPT-4o, not a direct head-to-head win rate against each streaming baseline.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Acknowledged evaluation deviation:** GPT-5 replaces the unavailable official GPT-5.1 judge, limiting direct comparability with the original Proact-VL protocol (§5.1).
- **Analyst inference—narrow generalization:** Evaluation covers professional Dota 2 with structured game information and fixed hero-following feeds. Transfer to other games or unavailable telemetry remains untested (§§3, 5.1).
- **Analyst inference—proxy quality:** Camera labels approximate human directing through selected or centrally visible heroes. Strict filtering improves reliability but retains only part of live footage; human agreement and event coverage measure different objectives (§§7.1, 7.3; Table 1).
- **Analyst inference—dependent samples:** Clips overlap, while reported significance uses bootstrap sampling over clips. Match-level uncertainty is not supplied (§5.2; Table 6).
- **Analyst inference—limited duration evidence:** Long-stream tests span several minutes, rather than complete matches. The bounded cache establishes computational control, but sustained narrative quality is less established (§5.4).
- **Reporting ambiguities:** Main-text footage hours and speech rates differ from appendix statistics; the appendix distinguishes overlapping clip counts from unique footage. Table 4 omits commentary win rates for two ablations, and no isolated adaptive-threshold ablation is shown (§§3, 5.2–5.4, 7.4).

## Open research questions
1. **Does the system maintain commentary coherence throughout complete matches?** Current extended tests use short continuous windows (§5.4). Next experiment: evaluate full matches with temporal quality ratings and references to events outside the retained horizon.
2. **How dependent are directing gains on reliable telemetry?** Removing state tokens hurts directing, but event-stream robustness is not isolated (Table 4). Next experiment: separately delay, corrupt, or remove states and events.
3. **Do viewers prefer its directing trade-offs?** Kill coverage improves while fight coverage trails the action rule (Table 1). Next experiment: blinded viewer comparisons measuring action visibility, shot continuity, and overall preference.
4. **How much do adaptive thresholds contribute?** Their rationale is described without a standalone ablation (§4.6). Next experiment: compare fixed and adaptive thresholds under matched average speaking and cutting rates.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text provides substantial methods, comparisons, and ablations, but broader validity and reproducibility remain uncertain.

Material missing information includes public code/data/weight availability, exact inference GPU, complete optimization and rollout settings, threshold parameters, operational kill-coverage details, end-to-end capture/rendering/audio latency, human preference evaluation, and match-level confidence intervals.
