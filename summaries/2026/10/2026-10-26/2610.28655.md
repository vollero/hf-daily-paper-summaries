# What Git Does Not Record: Provenance Legibility in Two AI-Assisted Research Repositories

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.28655) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28655)


## One-sentence takeaway
An audit of two research repositories shows that current AI-trace files and commit disclosures provide incomplete, query-dependent provenance evidence that cannot establish how much AI contributed (§§5–7).

## Problem and motivation
AI disclosure policies ask researchers to reconstruct how tools contributed, but do not ensure that the necessary evidence was recorded. The authors examine whether ordinary Git histories can support those disclosures, distinguishing stored change history from visible process records and reliable evidence of human review (§§1, 4).

The identified gap is measurement validity: prior repository studies count AI declarations, whereas this paper asks what those declarations and persistent agent artifacts actually establish (§2).

## Approach and contributions
The authors audit two environmental-science repositories created by one investigator: Project A has 466 commits and standing agent rules; Project B has 103 commits and a running agent scratchpad. They fix census revisions, inspect artifact histories across paths, count insertions and surviving lines, analyze blame cohorts and trailers, and compare artifacts with other text files in each repository (§3).

Distinct contributions are:

- **Provenance taxonomy:** separates recoverable, partial, absent, and misleading provenance fields (§4).
- **Legibility measurements:** quantify current-revision visibility, revision survival, and within-repository comparisons (§5).
- **Disclosure audit:** measures trailer-key coverage, agreement with artifact-edit proxies, and temporal variation (§6).
- **Capture requirements and evidence ledger:** derives requirements for recording acceptance events, explicit involvement fields, accessible histories, and rename-aware provenance; reports constructing row-level evidence supporting the measurements (§§1, 8).

These are observational findings and design requirements, not a tested provenance-capture system.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Current visibility differs sharply between artifacts | A retains 1,915 of 2,236 gross inserted lines (85.6%); B retains 335 of 1,537 (21.8%). Reinserted or rewritten lines count again in the denominator. | §5 |
| Both artifacts are unusually overwritten locally | History-only share ranks A at the reported 87th percentile among 174 comparison files and B at the 99th among 88; comparison files require at least 20 gross inserted lines, and both repository medians are 0%. | §5; Fig. 1 |
| Line retention overstates revision coverage | Under rename-following blame, 15/41 A cohorts and 18/41 B cohorts leave no surviving line. These cohorts differ from path-query revision counts. | §5; Fig. 2 |
| Path queries can obscure content age | Blame attributes 686/1,915 visible A lines (35.8%) to 26 commits absent from the path query, reaching back to 2025-09-01. | §5 |
| One trailer key misses other explicit declarations | A has AI-naming Co-Authored-By trailers on 66/466 commits (14.2%), versus any AI-naming trailer key on 121/466 (26.0%); the single-key audit misses 55/121 declarations (45.5%). | §6 |
| Trailer agreement with the activity proxy is low and case-dependent | Co-Authored-By appears on 1/39 B scratchpad-edit commits (2.6%) and 5/15 A rule-edit commits (33.3%). Artifact edits are an imperfect activity proxy. | §§3.1, 6 |
| Disclosure varies over time without an identified cause | A’s Co-Authored-By rate changes from 3/111 commits (2.7%) through December 2025 to 63/355 (17.7%) afterward, a reported 6.6-fold contrast across an inactive January. The boundary was selected retrospectively. | §6; Fig. 3 |
| Visible rules do not establish their origins or review history | Reconstruction assigns unknown origin evidence and unobserved human review to every examined rule; explanations for why rules exist reach inference only. | §7 |

The evidence establishes failures of visibility and disclosure interpretation in these cases. Deleted committed text remains recoverable from Git history; low current visibility is not data loss. Neither line retention nor trailer frequency measures the amount of AI-authored work, and the comparisons do not identify effects of tool choice or trace format.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Tool, artifact format, project, period, research phase, and usage intensity are confounded. Two repositories from one author support existence claims, not population estimates or causal comparisons (§§1, 9).
- **Author-stated:** Scratchpad edits can be human-made, while agent activity can occur without a corresponding scratchpad-edit commit. Proxy sensitivity is therefore not sensitivity against verified AI involvement (§3.1).
- **Author-stated:** The temporal boundary was chosen after inspecting results. Greater agent use or more agent-written commits could explain the disclosure increase; settings and commit-process histories are unavailable (§6).
- **Author-stated:** Qualitative coding was performed by the repository creator with knowledge of outcomes, without inter-rater agreement (§9).
- **Author-stated:** Line counts do not measure semantic information. Uncommitted edits, unmerged branches, and AI contributions outside the trace are excluded (§§5, 9).
- **Analyst inference:** The proposed capture requirements are motivated by observed gaps, but their effectiveness remains untested in the supplied study (§8).

## Open research questions

1. **Does trace format affect auditability independently of tool and user?** The cases confound these factors. A next experiment could hold tasks and tooling constant while comparing standing rules, mutable scratchpads, and append-only records across investigators.
2. **How accurately do trailers identify verified AI-assisted changes?** This paper has an artifact-edit proxy rather than event-level ground truth. A prospective study could record agent sessions, acceptance events, and commit creation, then measure trailer precision and sensitivity.
3. **Does exposing history improve provenance reconstruction?** The paper measures visibility, not auditor performance. An experiment could compare reconstruction accuracy using current files, ordinary Git history, and a rename-aware chronological interface (§§5, 8).
