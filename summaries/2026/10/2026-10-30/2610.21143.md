# CoSTA: Compressed Speech Token Adaptation for Efficient Spoken Dialogue Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.21143) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21143)


## One-sentence takeaway
CoSTA adapts spoken dialogue models to generate reversibly compressed speech tokens while retaining their tokenizer and decoder, achieving approximately 1.7× expanded speech-token generation speed with comparable or improved measured quality in Chinese and English (Table 1; §4.2).

## Problem and motivation
Spoken dialogue models typically generate the same speech tokens their synthesis decoder consumes. Long token sequences increase autoregressive decoding costs and consume interaction budgets. The authors argue that replacing the speech tokenizer with a lower-rate alternative can require substantial adaptation and still couples generation and synthesis representations (§1).

The identified gap is a way to shorten generation sequences in existing models while preserving their synthesis interface.

## Approach and contributions
- **Generation–synthesis decoupling:** Apply BPE to speech-token sequences, generate compressed units, and deterministically expand them into original tokens before synthesis. “Lossless” means reversible token segmentation; it does not guarantee identical responses from the adapted model (§§2–3).
- **Post-hoc adaptation:** Extend embeddings and the LM head using averages of constituent token rows; progressively adapt on compressed TTS targets; then adapt on speech-dialogue targets. Text tokens remain unchanged, and the original speech tokenizer and decoder are retained (§3).
- **Empirical characterization:** Measure an approximate log-linear compression trend across tokenizer configurations and corpus settings, then examine vocabulary-size trade-offs, interaction budgets, and adaptation to a second architecture (§§2, 4.4; Appendix D).

The claimed novelty is the combination of compatibility-preserving adaptation and token-space decoupling for existing large spoken dialogue models, rather than BPE itself (§5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Faster model-side generation | Across six benchmarks per language, CoSTA-ZH achieves 101.99 versus 58.74 expanded speech tokens/s for Step-Audio 2 mini (**1.736×**); CoSTA-EN achieves 99.05 versus 59.69 (**1.659×**). Generated-token compression is 66.81% and 70.17%, respectively. | Table 1; §4.2 |
| Lower full-response latency and higher throughput | Against the base model on AlpacaEval-zh/AlpacaEval, one A100 80GB, concurrency 1/4/8: latency falls **32.8–39.0% / 22.4–26.0%**, and throughput rises **51.6–59.2% / 25.8–38.6%**, for Chinese/English. | §4.2; Table 2 caption |
| Average automatic quality improves over matched fine-tuning | Chinese CoSTA-ZH versus Vanilla-FT-ZH: G-Eval **72.27 vs 69.88**, CER **1.51% vs 4.96%**, UTMOS **3.75 vs 3.77**. English CoSTA-EN versus Vanilla-FT-EN: **69.97 vs 66.59**, WER **2.50% vs 10.16%**, UTMOS **4.54 vs 4.46**. | Table 3 |
| Human judgments support quality preservation | Against base and matched fine-tuned models, reported win-or-tie rates span **86.1–96.1%** for fluency and **83.3–87.7%** for semantic quality; each language/metric/baseline comparison contains 180 paired ratings. | §4.2; Figure 5; Appendix C.6 |
| Larger vocabularies have diminishing returns | On AlpacaEval-zh, increasing added units from **80K to 160K** raises expanded generation speed from **103.03 to 104.18 tokens/s**, while G-Eval falls from **81.36 to 78.87**. | §4.4; Appendix C.9 |
| Compression trend persists on held-out speech | Across eight tokenizer configurations and **10K–320K** added units, log-linear fits achieve **R² 0.9807–0.9997**. Each tokenizer/corpus setting learns its own vocabulary. | §2; Appendices D.1–D.2 |
| Adaptation transfers to another architecture | On OpenS2S with its native 12.5-Hz tokenizer, speech-token steps fall **21.17% in Chinese** and **18.64% in English**; content and alignment improve over matched Vanilla-FT, with slightly lower average UTMOS. | §4.4; Appendix C.7 |

The evidence supports efficiency gains with favorable average quality under the tested adaptation and evaluation settings. Expanded-token speed measures original speech tokens represented per LLM decoding second; it is distinct from end-to-end latency. The results do not establish identical outputs, universal quality preservation, or vocabulary transfer across corpora. Appendix C.1 explicitly notes that improvements are not uniform across datasets and metrics.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Dialogue adaptation uses synthesized speech; alternative lossless segmentation methods remain unexplored; emotional dialogue, personalized styles, and diverse acoustic environments are outside the main evaluation (Limitations).
- **Analyst inference—generalization:** Adaptation uses a consistent response voice, and full-model experiments cover two languages and two architectures. Broader speaker, language, and architecture generalization remains unestablished (§4.1; Appendices B.1, C.7).
- **Analyst inference—measurement:** G-Eval depends on ASR transcription and an automated judge. Human evaluation covers 60 queries with six evaluators; the supplied text gives no uncertainty estimates or significance tests (Appendices B.2, C.6).
- **Scope constraint:** The budget stress test measures generation-format sustainability, explicitly not conversational coherence (§4.4).
- **Deployment cost:** CoSTA-ZH training costs approximately 79.5 A100 GPU-hours; timbre conversion and dialogue synthesis each additionally take approximately 1–2 days on eight A100s. No inference-volume break-even analysis is supplied (Appendix A.4).

## Open research questions
- **Does compression remain effective with varied real speakers and expressive speech?** Current adaptation standardizes voice and relies on synthetic dialogue. Next experiment: compare synthetic-only and mixed real/synthetic adaptation on held-out speakers, emotions, and noisy speech.
- **Can a learned BPE vocabulary transfer across domains or languages?** Compression curves use separately trained vocabularies. Next experiment: freeze a vocabulary from one setting and measure compression, dialogue quality, and runtime in another.
- **How should vocabulary size change with deployment hardware and workload?** The preferred size reflects tested A100 settings and an observed output-head overhead trade-off. Next experiment: sweep vocabulary size across hardware, concurrency, and response lengths while measuring quality and total latency.
- **Do budget savings improve coherent multi-turn conversation?** The stress test excludes coherence. Next experiment: evaluate connected multi-turn dialogues under matched token and time budgets.
