# Training Foundation Models with a Single Algorithm

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.40564) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40564)


## One-sentence takeaway
The authors formulate static-data training and suitable RL updates as shared likelihood fitting, reporting better reasoning, retention, and subsequent learning in tested settings, with benefits depending on data mixtures and compute budgets.

## Problem and motivation
Foundation-model pipelines typically separate pretraining, midtraining, supervised fine-tuning, and RL into distinct recipes and infrastructure. The authors argue that this obscures how data timing, reuse, and compute allocation jointly affect the final model. Their proposed shift is to keep the learning rule fixed while changing the training-data distribution (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Common formulation:** Extend the existing MaxRL likelihood interpretation to mixtures of static data and reward-conditioned generations, including arbitrary fixed generators. Staged training becomes a mixture that assigns weight to one source at a time (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Shared implementation:** Represent examples as tokens with nonnegative loss weights; use one token-normalized likelihood loss, buffer, and trainer. Generation runs asynchronously with periodic weight synchronization (§4).
- **Training-trajectory experiments:** Compare mixtures, schedules, reuse, compute allocation, retention, and subsequent adaptation in controlled puzzles and heterogeneous-data mathematical training (§5).

The contribution builds on MaxRL and TailRL rather than introducing their underlying objectives. With an older or different generator, fitting its target generally does **not** recover the current policy’s RL gradient (§3.2; Appendix F).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

| Best unified configurations improve final reasoning and size generalization | Controlled 124M model; fixed 20B-token pretraining and approximately 510M-token oracle corpus; pass@1 after 6,000 Base or 12,000 Extended RL updates, averaged over two seeds; compared with selected staged baselines. Exact gains are unavailable. | §5.1.1; Fig.4 |
| Compute advantage depends on budget | Observed pass@1/pass@16 frontiers include pretraining, midtraining, generation, and RL training. | Corpus composition can reduce coding accuracy loss | Removing designated code sources raises Coding pass@1 from **16.51% to 18.65%** under constant 0.5% mixing; little improvement occurs under full annealing. Scores average HumanEval+ and MBPP+ after mathematical RL. | §D.4; Fig.25; Table 18 |

| Implementation throughput is not an isolated algorithmic gain | Zero-mixing unified training records **3.7 seconds/update**, versus **28.8**, a reported **7.8×** throughput difference over 12,000 updates; systems use **24 versus 16 GPUs**, and asynchronous training can reuse responses. | §D.6 |

These results support benefits within the evaluated configurations, rather than universal superiority or predictive compute-optimality. Real-world experiments match optimizer updates, not total compute. Coverage measures the chance of obtaining a correct sample among multiple attempts; it does not establish that the model can identify that sample (§C.4).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Experiments are small-scale and mostly optimize one RL task at a time. No universally preferred mixture, predictive scaling law, or detailed optimization rule for changing mixtures is established (§7).
- **Author-stated:** Downstream adaptation still requires additional training; longer continual-learning sequences remain untested (§7).
- **Comparison qualification:** Real-world staged and unified implementations differ in asynchrony and baseline subtraction. Their direct comparison cannot isolate either factor (§D.6).
- **Measurement qualification:** Compute estimates omit several implementation costs; configured token fractions can differ from realized fractions as response lengths change (§§A.1–A.2).
- **Analyst inference:** The shared likelihood loss alone does not explain all gains: separately normalized losses sometimes outperform it under matched initialization and data mixtures (§C.9).
- **Evidence availability:** The supplied source is truncated, and many referenced tables contain captions without numerical cells. Complete coverage and independent assessment of all reported gains are therefore unavailable.

## Open research questions
- **Can mixture schedules be selected predictively?** The paper finds task-dependent tradeoffs but no general selection rule (§7). Next experiment: compare adaptive scheduling against tuned fixed schedules under matched total compute.
- **Do benefits persist at larger scales?** The authors explicitly identify small-scale evaluation and missing scaling laws (§7). Next experiment: repeat compute-frontier comparisons across model sizes and corpus budgets.
- **How much do staleness and baseline subtraction matter?** Existing implementations change both (§D.6). Next experiment: independently vary these factors while holding data mixtures, hardware, and fresh-rollout budgets fixed.
- **Does improved adaptation survive longer task sequences?** Current evidence covers separate continuations from mathematical training (§§D.7–D.8). Next experiment: evaluate repeated task transitions with retention measured after every transition.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The formulation and experimental direction are well supported by the supplied text, but quantitative completeness is limited.

Missing information includes many exact benchmark results, uncertainty estimates for the main gains, portions removed by truncation, complete hyperparameter tables, consumed-data staleness distributions, and confirmation of released code, checkpoints, or constructed datasets.
