# HairDeck: Animation-Ready Stylized Hair Cards from a Single Image

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.40599) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40599)


## One-sentence takeaway

HairDeck generates stylized hair cards and spring-bone rigs from one image using unordered sets of primitive tokens, achieving better geometry and faster card generation than the evaluated baselines on predominantly VRoid-based tests (Tables 1–4).

## Problem and motivation

Stylized game and avatar hair uses individually shaped ribbons whose geometry forms the visible hair, with spring-bone chains providing secondary motion. Artists normally construct and rig these elements manually. The authors identify a representation gap: strand generators target realistic hair, general image-to-3D systems produce fused surfaces, and CHARM generates stylized cards through lengthy autoregressive control-point sequences (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Card representation:** A VAE compresses each card’s variable-length control points into one continuous latent and decodes by arc length. Separate attributes preserve root position, root width direction and triangular or rhombic cross-section (§3.1).
- **Joint set generation:** A diffusion transformer without slot positional embeddings generates all cards together using flow matching; existence flags determine the card count. The novelty is its hair-specific representation and integration, building on prior set-generation methods (§§2–3.2).
- **Image conditioning:** A frozen image encoder conditions generation in a face-anchored head frame. Synthetic appearance augmentation and filtered, model-restyled training renders address drawing-style variation (§3.3; Appendix A).
- **Generated animation rig:** A second set transformer predicts spring-bone paths, joint counts and stiffness from card tokens. Fixed geometric skinning binds the cards, and an export procedure inserts the generated hair and rig into a VRM character (§3.4; Appendix A).

All results below are author-reported.

| Lower reported image failure rates | HairDeck: **3.5%** on rendered characters and **10.0%** on 40 in-the-wild images; baselines: **13.2–34.2%** and **17.5–77.5%**, respectively. Failure combines insufficient cards or scalp coverage; fused meshes use only scalp coverage. | Table 2; §4.1 |
| Card design improves orientation and smoothness | On point-cloud tests, removing root normals raises orientation error from **16.8° to 26.4°**; raw control-point tokens increase the median per-hairstyle maximum card turn from **14.2° to 21.9°**. Artist reference: **14.1°**. | Table 3 |
| Head frame improves placement rather than aligned shape | On 76 front-view test images, removing the head frame raises unaligned CD **0.0136 → 0.0189**, while aligned CD remains **0.0089**. | Table 7 |

The evidence supports improved geometry, card orientation and generation speed in the tested settings. The matched autoregressive comparison helps isolate the benefit of joint set generation, but does not show an orientation advantage. Aligned image metrics remove placement errors, and the strongest motion comparison uses artist cards rather than the complete image-to-animation pipeline.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Very short hair and realistic alpha-textured cards lie outside training coverage. Outputs include geometry and UVs but no textures; unusual spring-bone layouts can receive too few chains (§5).
- **Author-stated input failures:** Props, hats, horns and background elements retained by segmentation can become hair cards. Some training characters also encode accessories as cards (§5; Figure 23; Appendix F).
- **Analyst inference—limited generalization evidence:** Training and geometric ground truth come from CHARM’s VRoid-derived data. The in-the-wild evaluation reports coarse failure rates rather than reference 3D accuracy (§4.1).
- **Analyst inference—animation validity remains partial:** Simulation uses no colliders and fixed drag and gravity for generated chains. Full-pipeline motion correlations are absent, so collision behavior and motion fidelity of image-generated assets remain unestablished (Table 4; Appendix A).
- **Analyst inference—comparison limits:** Failure criteria differ between card generators and fused meshes. Timing ends before meshing, so it does not establish complete rigged-export latency (§4.1).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** The reported rendered-image failure percentage does not map directly to an integer count over the stated test set; aggregation or repeated-sampling details are not given. Statistical uncertainty and repeated-run variation are also unavailable (Table 2).

## Open research questions

1. **Can generation remain reliable when segmentation retains accessories?** Figure 23 documents this failure. A next experiment could compare current masks, manually corrected masks and accessory-aware masks on the same inputs.
2. **How closely does the complete image-to-rig pipeline reproduce artist motion?** Table 4 omits motion metrics for generated cards. Evaluate image-generated assets under the existing motion protocol, adding penetration measurements with colliders.
3. **Can the representation cover short hair and unusual chain layouts?** These are explicit limitations (§5). Add targeted training examples and evaluate held-out categories, separately varying training coverage and slot capacity.
4. **How accurate is the unseen geometry outside VRoid?** In-the-wild failure rates do not resolve 3D fidelity. Build an independently authored stylized-card test set with reference geometry and rigs, then measure reconstruction and motion without retraining.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** in the method description and reported benchmark comparisons; **medium** in broader generalization and end-to-end animation readiness.

Material missing information includes confirmed code/checkpoint availability, repeated-run uncertainty, the rendered-image failure-rate aggregation, quantitative 3D ground truth for in-the-wild inputs, complete export latency, and motion/collision evaluation for image-generated assets.
