# ABF-Bench: A Verifiable Benchmark for Agentic Blockchain Forensics

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.23656) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23656)


## One-sentence takeaway
ABF-Bench makes fund tracing an evidence-verified agent task, and its specialized harness beats general-purpose agents on reference-edge recall but finishes below breadth-first tracing despite acquiring evidence faster early in the query budget (§§4, 7.4–7.5).

## Problem and motivation
Blockchain investigations must follow funds across transfers, exchanges, and ledgers while recognizing where public evidence no longer establishes a continuation. The authors identify a gap in existing evaluations: comparing investigators requires measuring both recovered movements and the supporting records they acquire under controlled access costs (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Verifiable task formulation:** Solvers query a metered ledger environment and submit operations with record citations. A deterministic verifier assesses evidence independently of a grader that measures recovery of a hidden reference graph (§§3–4).
- **Benchmark construction:** A reusable pipeline combines within-ledger replay, cross-ledger linking, and human review. Conservative stopping rules exclude unresolved continuations, and every reference edge must have a verified, budget-retrievable witness (§5).
- **ABF agent harness:** Agent-maintained findings, leads, and notes support investigation, alongside report preview, context summarization, and five tracing/reporting heuristics. Evaluation compares it with general-purpose agents and fixed-order tracers (§§6–7).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Evaluated suite covers varied cross-chain tracing | 31 evaluated tasks, 2,044 reference edges, 20–107 edges per task, and query budgets of 98–510; derived from the Bybit incident across Ethereum and Bitcoin | Table 2; §§5.1, 7.3 |
| Specialized harness improves recall, but breadth-first tracing leads | First-round macro edge recall: ABF 0.75, Codex 0.47, Claude Code 0.10, breadth-first tracer 0.88. ABF and Codex share gpt-5.5 at high reasoning effort; missing submissions count as zero | §§7.3–7.4 |
| Harness acquires evidence faster early | At one-quarter of each task’s budget, mean reference-witness evidence recall is 39% for ABF versus 35% for the best-in-hindsight tracer; that comparator overtakes after 54% of budget | §7.5, H1; Fig. 4 discussion |
| Early stopping leaves substantial headroom | ABF spends 47% of its budget on average; what additional querying would recover is unmeasured | §§7.4–7.5 |
| Agents better recognize undisclosed custody | At reached custody points whose outflows were listed and where continuation remained possible, each agent stops at least three-quarters of the time; fixed-order tracers never do | §7.5, H2; Fig. 5 discussion |
| Retrieval discipline changes Claude Code’s score sharply | Unretrieved citations occur in 29/31 runs. Recall is 0.10 with the retrieval condition versus 0.54 when counting valid claims regardless of retrieval | §7.5, H3 |
| Larger and more branching tasks remain difficult | ABF recall falls from 0.82 to 0.69 between smallest and largest task thirds; recall correlates with maximum fan-out at Spearman ρ = −0.66 versus −0.41 with edge count | §7.4 |

These results support differences among the evaluated systems on this suite. The same-model ABF–Codex comparison supports an effect of the overall harness design, without isolating its components. Evidence recall measures retrieval of reference witnesses, whereas edge recall requires valid submitted edges with retrieved citations (§§4.4, 7.5). Neither metric establishes complete reconstruction of the underlying laundering activity.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** One incident limits generalization; public incident information may have entered model training; conservative rules score only a subset of flows; recovery using agents’ unused budgets remains unmeasured (§8).
- **Comparison scope:** ABF and Codex hold model and reasoning effort fixed, but Claude Code uses a different model. Cross-family differences therefore cannot isolate model, harness, and query policy effects (§7.3).
- **Analyst inference:** No component ablation is reported, so the separate benefits of memory, preview, compaction, and heuristics remain unresolved (§§6–7).
- **Evaluation stability:** Environment failures required repairs and reruns, two released tasks were excluded, and repeated runs showed substantial within-task recall variation (§§7.3–7.4).
- **Asset availability:** The introduction promises release upon publication, while later sections describe tasks as published; actual availability cannot be established from the supplied text alone.

## Open research questions
1. **Does the harness generalize across incidents?** Only one case is evaluated (§8). Test the unchanged harness on independently constructed incidents, including cases with reduced training-exposure risk.
2. **Would continued search close the tracer gap?** Agents stop early, but continuation gains are unmeasured (§7.5). Compare ordinary stopping with a budget-aware continuation policy under identical budgets.
3. **Which harness components drive improvement?** The same-model comparison bundles several changes (§6). Ablate memory, preview, and heuristics individually and jointly across repeated runs.
4. **Can verifier feedback improve agents through training?** The authors propose verifiable rewards but report no training experiment (§9). Train with operation verdicts and evaluate evidence-backed recall on held-out incidents.
