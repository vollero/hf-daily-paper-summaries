# Which Language Should a Skeleton Speak? Language Choices in Multilingual Reasoning

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.09607) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09607)


## One-sentence takeaway
English reasoning skeletons provide small average gains in multilingual mathematics, but language-specific benefits depend on the model, decoding protocol, and benchmark, with limited statistical support for alternative skeleton languages.

## Problem and motivation
Skeleton prompting supplies a short structural outline before an LLM generates detailed reasoning. The authors identify an underexplored design choice: the languages of the query, skeleton, and final reasoning can differ, yet prior approaches typically fix the skeleton to English. This matters for reasoning accuracy and for producing responses that users can inspect in their own language, particularly in low-resource settings (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled language exploration:** LASEF treats query, skeleton, and reasoning/answer languages as separate variables while fixing the skeleton format. It is a training-free empirical framework, not a learned language selector (§3).
- **Evaluation across conditions:** The study compares native-language and English-translated CoT, with and without skeletons, across model sizes and mathematical benchmarks. Alternative skeleton languages receive greedy, multi-rollout, and cross-benchmark checks (§4; Appendices G–K).
- **Analysis of heterogeneous effects:** Translation of English skeletons into other languages tests whether effects persist when intended semantic content is held fixed. The authors distinguish directionally consistent, evaluation-dependent, and asymmetric negative patterns (§5).
- **Evaluation asset:** The authors construct MGSM-LowResource through dual-model translation and verification: 34 languages, 250 problems each, totaling 8,500 samples. They state that resources are released (Appendix C, Table 6; abstract).

## Evidence and main results

All accuracy results below are conditioned on target-language-compliant outputs unless otherwise indicated; “pp” denotes percentage points.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Translation contributes much more than skeletons in the main low-resource setting | Qwen2.5-7B, greedy MGSM, mean over 19 languages: native CoT **33.99%**, English-query CoT **58.06%**, English-query CoT plus English skeleton **60.08%**; translation adds **24.07 pp**, skeleton adds **2.02 pp** | Table 2; §4.4 |
| English skeletons have small average gains with concentrated statistical support | Native-query setting above: mean **+2.38 pp**, positive in **13/19** languages. Only Kazakh and Uyghur retain significant improvements after FDR correction, each **pFDR=0.014**. Pooled paired items: **n=4,628**, **+2.27 pp**, **p=0.0014** | Appendix J |
| Alternative-language benefits can retain direction across decoding protocols | Qwen2.5-7B, native-query MGSM, non-English minus English skeleton EM: Basque–Korean **+6.32→+2.78 pp**, Armenian–Korean **+5.17→+4.61 pp**, Lao–Thai **+2.81→+3.18 pp**, from greedy to five-rollout sampling (**T=0.7**) | Figure 2 |
| Directional consistency does not establish significance | Those three pairs have greedy McNemar **p=0.072, 0.361, 0.401**, respectively; none reaches nominal significance | Appendix J |
| A large greedy gain can reverse under sampling | Maltese–Russian, same MGSM setting: **+8.62 pp** under greedy decoding, approximately **−0.24 pp** under five-rollout sampling; prose reports **−0.23 pp** | Figure 2; §4.5 |
| Translation ablation preserves some language effects | Greedy MGSM, translated versus English skeleton: Basque–Korean **+4.42 pp**, Armenian–Korean **+1.61 pp**, Lao–Thai **+0.40 pp**; Mongolian–Russian **−3.43 pp**, Uyghur–Chinese **−6.94 pp** | §5; Figure 3 |
| Cross-benchmark transfer is partial | Qwen2.5-7B, native queries, **35** non-English-versus-English skeleton cells: MGSM/MSVAMP rank correlation **ρ=0.383**, nominal **p=0.023**; signs agree in **23/35 (66%)**, binomial **p=0.090**. Basque–Korean becomes **0.00 pp**; Maltese–Russian becomes **−1.27 pp** on MSVAMP | Appendix K; Table 13 |
| Smaller models tend to benefit more, but this is not a scaling law | Mean English-skeleton effect under native queries: Qwen **+1.73→+1.00 pp** (7B→72B); Llama **+0.81→−0.43 pp** (8B→70B); Ministral **+1.29→+0.11 pp** (3B→14B), across each family’s reported benchmark/language settings | Appendices G–H |

The evidence supports context-dependent effects and a modest average English-skeleton benefit in the tested settings. It does not establish a universal language-selection rule or a causal role for linguistic typology. The translation ablation weakens a generation-quality-only explanation, but translation fidelity remains part of that control.

## Limitations and validity risks
**Author-stated:** Evaluation is confined to mathematics; fine-tuning and interactions between skeleton format and language remain unexplored. Full configuration exploration is computationally expensive and requires revalidation when tasks, benchmarks, or models change (Limitations). Cross-benchmark language-pair validation covers elementary arithmetic (Appendix K). Round-trip translation checks cannot guarantee grammatical quality or exclude model self-correction (Appendix C.3).

**Analyst inferences grounded in the text:**

- Language filtering makes reported accuracy conditional rather than an end-to-end deployment success rate. Restricting the main analysis to 19 sufficiently retained languages also limits conclusions about the least reliable languages (Appendices B, L).
- Selected positive pairs lack nominal statistical significance; six of 95 alternative-skeleton comparisons are nominally significant, but corrected results for those comparisons are not supplied (Appendix J).
- Typological categories are explicitly descriptive. They cannot establish that alignment causes improved reasoning (Appendix D).
- Reporting inconsistencies warrant caution: Uyghur–Chinese multi-rollout degradation is **−1.74 pp** in Figure 2 but **−1.12 pp** in prose. Skeleton examples also contain numbers and formulas despite stated exclusion constraints (Appendix A).

## Open research questions
- **Can language effects transfer to harder reasoning?** Cross-benchmark pair validation covers elementary arithmetic. Next experiment: preregister the same pairs on harder multilingual mathematics.
- **Does skeleton structure change the preferred language?** The fixed format isolates language but leaves this interaction open. Next experiment: cross several outline formats and abstraction levels with skeleton languages.
- **Can a cheap selector replace exhaustive exploration?** LASEF remains an expensive offline diagnostic. Next experiment: train a selector on measured effects and evaluate it on held-out models, languages, and benchmarks.
- **Do gains survive end-to-end evaluation and human translation checks?** Conditional filtering and round-trip verification leave these questions unresolved. Next experiment: report correctness jointly with language compliance and obtain native-speaker audits.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the descriptive findings, but statistical strength and generalization are limited.

Material information unavailable or unclear includes corrected significance results for all 95 alternative-language comparisons, multi-rollout uncertainty estimates, complete unfiltered accuracy tables promised in Appendix L, native-speaker translation validation, and detailed judge calibration. The stated resource release was not independently verified.
