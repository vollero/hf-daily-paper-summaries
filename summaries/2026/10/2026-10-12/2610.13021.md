# EmbodiedCLBench: Evaluating Continual Learning for Self-Evolving Embodied Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.13021) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13021)


## One-sentence takeaway
EmbodiedCLBench shows that basic-task experience improves agents’ success on unseen household-task compositions in an offline simulation benchmark, but additional experience does not consistently improve performance (§§4.2, 5.2).

## Problem and motivation
The authors address how to evaluate whether embodied agents accumulate experience and reuse it on unfamiliar tasks. They argue that existing continual-learning benchmarks lack embodied spatial and physical constraints, while embodied benchmarks rarely isolate experience transfer (§§1–2). Their proposed proxy is compositional generalization: learning simpler household operations before attempting unfamiliar combinations.

## Approach and contributions

- **Benchmark and protocol:** EmbodiedCLBench contains 168 basic and 100 advanced tasks derived from TongSim household interactions. Advanced tasks combine atomic actions from multiple basic-task categories (§4.1).
- **Offline evaluation asset:** Precomputed state-transition graphs pair first-person images with candidate actions. Agents select actions until reaching the goal or exhausting a budget; candidates include shortest-path actions, executable detours, and invalid actions (§§3.3–3.4). Generated descriptions and options undergo unanimous approval by three annotators (Appendix B).
- **Comparative findings:** Four harnesses and two backbone models are evaluated under zero-shot execution, full-trajectory in-context learning (ICL), and Markdown skill summaries. Further analyses examine composition complexity, experience quantity, action selection, and step budgets (§§4–5).

The contribution is principally a benchmark and evaluation design; ICL and skill summaries are evaluated approaches rather than newly introduced learning algorithms. The authors’ claim that this is the first such benchmark is not independently established by the supplied text.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Experience improves advanced-task completion | Main evaluation across four harnesses and two backbones: advanced-task SR rises from approximately 10% zero-shot to above 30% with ICL or skill summaries after two related basic tasks; testing allows three extra steps. Exact configuration-level values are unavailable. | §§4.1–4.2; Table 1 caption, missing table body |
| Transfer remains useful at higher complexity | Hermes/Qwen3.8-Flash, six atomic actions: SR is 4.0% zero-shot, 32.0% ICL, and 26.0% Skill Learning. Absolute SR declines as composition grows. | §5.1; Figure 4 |
| More experience does not yield consistent scaling | Hermes and OpenClaw with Qwen3.8-Flash: moving from zero to one basic-task sample raises advanced-task SR from 8.0%–10.0% to 28.0%–40.0%; none of the four curves improves consistently through six samples. | §5.2; Figure 5 |
| Experience shifts action choices toward executable options | Main-run decision records for all four harnesses with Qwen3.8-Flash: invalid-action selection decreases by a reported 13%–20%, while suboptimal selection increases by 3%–8%. The prose does not explicitly distinguish relative percentages from percentage-point changes. | §5.3; Figure 6 |
| Benefit persists under a strict budget | Hermes/Qwen3.8-Flash with zero extra testing steps: SR is 0.0% zero-shot, 15.0% ICL, and 7.0% Skill Learning. ICL remains above Skill Learning and zero-shot across the tested budgets. | §5.4; Figure 7 |

These results support experience-assisted transfer within the benchmark. They do not establish persistent lifelong learning, physical-world transfer, or why additional samples fail to help. The authors’ explanation that agents cannot extract sufficiently distinct information is an interpretation of the scaling curves, not an isolated causal result.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-identified limitations:** Performance remains far from saturation, and experience quantity does not produce consistent improvement (§6). The closed-source simulator motivates offline graph evaluation (§3.3).
- **Analyst inference—learning scope:** Retaining trajectories or summaries demonstrates contextual transfer; the protocol does not report delayed retention, forgetting, or learning across a sustained task stream (§§3.4, 4.1).
- **Analyst inference—ecological validity:** Deterministic graphs and supplied action options constrain the problem. Generalization to online simulation, unrestricted action generation, or physical robots remains untested (§§3.3–3.4).
- **Analyst inference—mechanism uncertainty:** No reported unrelated-experience control separates compositional skill transfer from learning the interface or action-validity conventions (§§4–5).
- **Analyst inference—shortcut risk:** Although Appendix B describes shortcut prevention, some example options explicitly name the next task objective. Whether such wording reduces reliance on images is untested (Tables 3–5).
- **Evidence limits:** Complexity and budget analyses use one harness/backbone pair; sample scaling uses two harnesses with one backbone (§§5.1–5.4). Statistical uncertainty is not reported in the supplied text.

## Open research questions

- **What causes the experience benefit?** Related-task exposure is compared with zero-shot execution, without an unrelated-experience control. Next experiment: compare related, unrelated, and interface-only experience under matched context budgets.
- **Why does additional experience plateau?** The scaling curves do not isolate sample redundancy or memory quality (§5.2). Next experiment: vary experience diversity and summary consolidation independently while holding context length fixed.
- **Does transfer persist over time?** The protocol tests advanced tasks with experience immediately available (§4.1). Next experiment: introduce a longer task stream, delayed tests, and retention measurements.
- **Does the benefit survive less constrained interaction?** Evaluation uses fixed graph states and candidate options (§3.4). Next experiment: compare the same compositions under graph evaluation and online simulation with freely generated actions.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The protocol and qualitative findings are clearly described, but numerical verification is limited by missing Table 1 contents and figure data.

Material omissions include the six category names from Table 2, prompt specifications under Appendix B.1, repeated-run counts and uncertainty estimates, detailed harness/API configurations, sample-selection rules for scaling, analysis-set sizes, and an explicit text-only or image-ablation comparison. Code and dataset availability is author-reported and was not verified.
