# Can AI Agents Learn Their Way to the Top? Evaluating Heuristic Learning in a Long-Running Game Agent Competition

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.12341) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12341)


## One-sentence takeaway
Fixed-weight coding agents can improve executable game policies through replay-driven revision, with the strongest configuration reaching first place on six of twelve frozen human-program ladders, but persistent plateaus and unequal compute limit broader conclusions (§4.1; Appendix D).

## Problem and motivation
The authors study whether AI agents can interpret unfamiliar game rules, implement strategies, and improve autonomous programs through limited competitive feedback. Existing evaluations differ in opponent populations, feedback access, and revision schedules; the identified gap is an agent-scheduled development benchmark against archived human programs with explicit interaction budgets (§§1, 5).

## Approach and contributions
- **AHL formalization:** An AI agent with fixed model weights revises executable policies, tools, tests, notes, and experiment records using match experience. The submitted program plays autonomously; the AI agent develops it between matches (§2; Algorithm 1).
- **AAArena benchmark:** Twelve competition games and 1,920 archived human programs, with frozen opponent ratings, selected-opponent matches, and full-pool evaluations. Scoring retains the highest officially evaluated Elo rather than the final revision (§§1, 3).
- **Empirical study:** Seven model–harness configurations, feedback and opponent-selection ablations, extended-budget continuations, replay-source comparisons, and analysis of champion policy composition (§4; Appendix E).

The contribution is the formalization, evaluation setting, and empirical evidence; heuristic learning itself is attributed to prior work (§§1, 5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strongest configuration tops half the ladders | Opus5.5 with Claude Code earns six rank-1 finishes across twelve games; no configuration tops the other six. Seven configurations use 128 opponent-test units and 16 full-pool evaluations; reported results are medians of three runs’ retained champions. | §4.1; Table 2 caption and accompanying text |
| Larger rule descriptions coincide with fewer first-place finishes | At least one configuration reaches rank 1 in five of the six smaller-description games, versus one of the six larger-description games, under the main evaluation setting. | §4.1; Table 2 discussion |
| Feedback-driven revisions can produce substantial gains and regressions | A GLM-5.3 Dorado trajectory improves from rank 41 to 2; concentrating upgrades yields rank 14→3 and +147.7 Elo. Other revisions regress. | §4.2.2; Figure 4; Table 3 discussion |
| More interaction does not consistently remove plateaus | Four GLM-5.3 continuations increase budgets from 128/16 to 384/48. Generals and LOTA retain unchanged champions; Miracle improves rank 26→19 and AntWar2 9→6; none reaches rank 1. | §4.3; Table 4 discussion |
| Feedback choices affect performance | On Pacman, AntWar, and Miracle, GLM-5.3 with dense feedback beats binary feedback in retained-champion Elo. Model-selected opponents beat random and top-four selection throughout; best tested batch sizes are respectively four, two, and four opponents under 128/16 budgets. | §4.4; Figures 6–8 |
| External replays support improvement, with mixed comparative results | With 128 external replay views and 16 own-policy evaluations, GLM-5.3 improves rank 10→1 on Pacman, 41→19 on AntWar, and 27→15 on Miracle. On-policy champions reach ranks 1, 5, and 26 respectively. | §4.5; Figures 9–10 |
| Equal feedback budgets allow unequal compute | Under the main 128/16 ceilings, reported total token use is 260M for GPT6-sol, 1,454M for Qwen 3.8, and 737M for Opus5.5 with Claude Code. | Appendix D |
| Champion policies mostly use rules and scoring | Equally averaging code shares across 84 retained champions, rule-based control plus heuristic scoring accounts for 79%, graph search 13%, and the remaining families 8%. Classification is automatic and approximate. | Appendix E |

These results establish competitive improvement against the specified archived pools. Rank 1 denotes first place under the rating protocol, not necessarily a direct win against the human leader or optimal play. Full-pool feedback is available during development, so results do not establish generalization to unseen opponents (Appendix B.3). The rule-size association is observational, and the external-replay comparison still includes feedback from evaluations of the learner’s own policy.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Model and harness differences are confounded: Opus5.5 uses Claude Code, while others use Codex (§4.1).
- **Author-stated:** Interaction ceilings do not equalize tokens, model calls, edits, or elapsed time; archived programs also include related versions from some entrants (Appendices B, D).
- **Author-stated:** AST and rule-atom counts measure representation-dependent specification size, not optimal-play difficulty. Policy-family classification is approximate and does not identify causal improvements (Appendices B.4, E).
- **Analyst inference:** Feedback ablations on one model and three games, and continuations of four existing runs, provide limited support for general conclusions about learning efficiency (§§4.3–4.5).
- **Analyst inference:** Selecting the best snapshot using development-visible pool evaluations may favor pool-specific adaptation; held-out evaluation is needed to assess transfer (Appendix B.3).

## Open research questions
- **Does AHL generalize to new opponents?** Frozen pools and visible evaluation feedback leave this unresolved. Next experiment: evaluate retained champions against held-out programs and changing populations (§6; Appendix B.3).
- **How much advantage comes from the model, harness, or compute?** Current comparisons vary these factors. Next experiment: cross models with common harnesses under matched token and interaction budgets (§4.1; Appendix D).
- **Can external replays reliably break plateaus?** Miracle benefits, but AntWar favors on-policy learning. Next experiment: repeat on-policy, off-policy, and mixed-replay conditions across games with uncertainty estimates (§4.5).
- **Do code revisions outperform parameter learning under equal resources?** A matched comparison is proposed but not reported. Next experiment: compare AHL, parameter-update learning, and hybrids with matched feedback and compute (§6).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the central findings and protocol, but omits numerical bodies of most result tables and actual plotted values. Material gaps include complete per-configuration scores, ablation effect sizes, run-level variance, ablation repetition counts, detailed match scheduling, original human-rating calibration, and a confirmed public release location for code, pools, and logs.
