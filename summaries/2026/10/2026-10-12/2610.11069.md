# Clinician use of language models diverges from how the models are evaluated

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.11069) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11069)


## One-sentence takeaway
In one institutional clinical assistant deployment, documentation and knowledge retrieval dominated requests, while 58 public benchmarks had a median task-distribution overlap of only 31%, demonstrating a workload mismatch rather than directly measuring deployed model performance.

## Problem and motivation
Clinical LLM readiness is often judged using examinations or curated cases. The authors ask whether these evaluations represent what clinicians actually request, including documentation, incomplete questions, and tasks requiring local information. They identify two gaps: insufficient measurement of benchmark representativeness and limited coverage of nurses and advanced practice providers (Introduction).

## Approach and contributions
- **Deployment characterization:** Retrospective analysis of the first clinician turn in 127,833 conversations from 6,342 clinicians across 35 specialties; 127,625 queries received valid annotations (Results; Table 1).
- **RCQ-Map:** A literature-grounded framework reporting 20 fields covering tasks, intent, context requirements, answerability, potential harm, and safest response. An LLM annotator was validated against seven clinicians on 100 queries (Methods: Development and clinician validation).
- **Benchmark comparison and reusable atlas:** The Clinical AI Benchmark Atlas (CABA) assembles 58 public benchmarks, with 10,659 annotated sampled items. The authors compare task distributions using one minus total variation distance and examine five benchmarks in greater detail (Methods: Clinical AI Benchmark Atlas; Fig. 5).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

| Many requests require additional information | 36.1% could not be answered well as posed (95% CI 34.8–37.5%); 22.2% lacked needed patient/local information. Clarification was the safest annotated route for 26.8%, retrieval for 12.0%. | Results: Answerability; Fig. 4 |
| Public benchmarks poorly match the task mix | Across 58 benchmarks, median overlap was 31% (IQR 21–39%), versus 55% for an evenly distributed ten-category workload. Median documentation/administration share was 0% (IQR 0–3%), versus 36.2% in deployment. | Fig. 5a; Methods: CABA |

The evidence establishes a task-composition mismatch for this deployment and the selected benchmarks. It does **not** establish deployed answer accuracy, actual harm, or how strongly benchmark scores predict clinical outcomes: assistant responses were not examined. The authors’ broader conclusion that benchmark scores reveal little about deployed performance is therefore an interpretation of the mismatch, rather than a directly tested predictive relationship.

## Limitations and validity risks
**Author-stated limitations:** One system and assistant may not represent other workflows; use of other tools was unobserved. First-turn extraction misses later requests. Benchmarks were author-selected, limited to public English text-only items, and reduced to single requests. The annotator was validated on clinical queries, not benchmark items. Harm, context, and response-route judgments had modest clinician agreement. Heavy users contributed much of the volume, although clinician clustering and sensitivity analyses addressed this concern (Discussion; Methods).

**Analyst inferences:** Task overlap alone cannot establish performance transfer or safety. The large difference between model-labeled and clinician-calibrated harm prevalence makes absolute risk estimates particularly uncertain. Restricted access to query text limits independent reproduction of annotations (Validation results; Data availability).

## Open research questions
- **Does benchmark overlap predict deployment performance?** Responses were not evaluated. A next experiment could compare benchmark scores with clinician-rated responses on representative deployment queries across several models.
- **How stable is the workload across institutions and interfaces?** Only one assistant was studied. Apply RCQ-Map across multiple systems, including chart-connected assistants, and compare role-stratified distributions.
- **Does clarification or retrieval improve outcomes?** Safest routes were annotated rather than experimentally tested. Prospectively compare direct answering with clarification and local-source retrieval on incomplete requests.
- **Do benchmark labels remain valid under independent review?** Human validation covered deployment queries only. Have clinicians independently annotate stratified benchmark samples and recompute distribution overlap.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported workload mismatch; **medium** in absolute harm and routing estimates.

Material missing information includes assistant responses and outcomes, the deployed assistant’s model configuration, full supplementary guidelines and distributions, and numerical sensitivity-analysis results. Query text is unavailable for independent inspection. The supplied Code availability section is empty, although the Discussion reports a code release. The Discussion also calls the 31% overlap an “average,” whereas the Results and Methods specify a **median**; this digest follows the latter.
