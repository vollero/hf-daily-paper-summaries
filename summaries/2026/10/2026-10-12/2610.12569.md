# SWE-Duel: Towards a Self-Scaling Arena for Coding Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.12569) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12569)


## One-sentence takeaway
SWE-Duel evaluates coding agents through executable, agent-authored bug-review duels, with preliminary evidence that newer task authors produce harder challenges, but uncertain rankings and imperfect test oracles limit stronger conclusions.

## Problem and motivation
The authors argue that static coding benchmarks become contaminated and saturated, while refreshed human-issue benchmarks provide newer tasks without necessarily increasing difficulty. Their proposed gap is an evaluation framework that generates both challenges and correctness tests during evaluation and lets participant capabilities determine difficulty (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Agent-authored challenges:** Red implements a new repository feature, deliberately embeds a bug, and writes sealed bug tests across separate context-isolated sessions. Validation requires visible tests to pass, sealed tests to distinguish buggy from feature-only code, and a fresh instance of the author to repair the task blind (§3.2).
- **Mirrored, execution-scored evaluation:** Blue reviews the PR without being told a bug exists. It wins only when existing, feature, and sealed bug tests all pass. Participants swap roles; missing authored tasks count as automatic Blue wins (§§3.3–3.4).
- **Reusable arena infrastructure and evidence:** The authors report an open toolkit, experiment dataset, leaderboard, tournament, and studies of task difficulty, oracle soundness, admission stability, harnesses, and newcomer onboarding (§1; §4; Reproducibility Statement).

Tasks are generated before tournament matches and reused within that tournament; freshness requires new banks across tournaments (§3; Appendix A).

The evidence supports an operational arena and a difficulty increase for the newest author in one family. It does not establish universal or indefinitely increasing difficulty, precise adjacent-model rankings, or semantic correctness beyond the test suites. Appendix H explicitly says adjacent rankings are not distinguishable at 95%; the differing headline repair percentages remain unresolved.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated:** Blind self-repair caps challenge difficulty at the author’s solving ability (§3.2). Small repositories and strong solver performance could still lead to saturation (Appendix L). The scaling study covers one family, one independent reference solver, small banks, and one solving draw per task; difficulty is not strictly monotonic across versions (Appendix K). Equal step budgets do not imply equal compute (Appendix D.1).

**Observed validity issues:** Sealed oracles reject some legitimate repairs (Appendix G). One task accounts for most of Nemotron’s Red wins and involves conflicting visible specification signals, making its apparent difficulty partly about resolving that conflict (Appendix O). Rankings depend materially on automatic-win policy and match-versus-turn aggregation (Appendices L–N).

**Analyst inference:** Fresh generation protects against prior exposure to those exact tasks, but does not demonstrate absence of repository familiarity; published banks also lose unseen-task status (Appendix A). The single successful newcomer placement does not establish onboarding accuracy for entrants near the leaderboard’s middle. Generated defect frequencies cannot be generalized to human software (Appendix P).

## Open research questions

- **Does scaling transfer across families and solvers?** Only one author ladder and one independent solver were tested. Repeat with several families, multiple fixed solvers, larger banks, and repeated solving runs.
- **Can harder tasks remain fairly solvable?** Blind self-repair limits difficulty. Compare blind admission with the authors’ proposed informed-repair gate, followed by independent blind review and semantic auditing (Appendix L).
- **How much do oracle errors affect rankings?** The audit covered sampled bug-test-only failures. Independently adjudicate a broader task sample, revise overspecified tests, and recompute rankings.
- **Does sparse onboarding reliably place competitive entrants?** Only one low-ranked entrant was demonstrated. Compare sampled and full schedules for several entrants spanning capability levels.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text describes the protocol and substantial supporting analyses, but many referenced tables contain captions without data rows, and worked code diffs are partially garbled.

Unavailable details include complete repository names and commit values, full participant statistics and intervals, exact prompt templates and size thresholds, harness versions, per-model API identifiers and dates, and complete artifact contents. The text also does not reconcile the 98.3% versus 98.7% solver figures or inconsistent ranking descriptions across sections.
