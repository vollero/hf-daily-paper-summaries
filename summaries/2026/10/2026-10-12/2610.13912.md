# RSI-Anything: Unlocking Recursive Self-Improvement with World Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.13912) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13912)


## One-sentence takeaway
RSI-Anything combines world-model-guided selection with feedback-driven, retained system updates and reports improvements across computational and scientific tasks, while sustained mutual improvement of policy and predictor remains unestablished (§§3, 5).

## Problem and motivation
External feedback requires costly executions or experiments, limiting exploration. World models can prioritize candidates, but policy changes can make their predictions unreliable; predictor changes can also alter policy learning signals. The authors identify a gap between accurate prediction and persistent improvement of the coupled policy–model system (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Prediction-guided improvement:** Domain-specific world models forecast candidate outcomes to guide external evaluation and, where enabled, learning-data selection. JEPA-Anything and its orthogonal predictive factorization are inherited foundations, rather than new contributions (§§2.2–2.3).
- **Update and retention protocol:** Feedback supports policy learning or persistent workflow revision. Predictor adaptation is conditional on reliability, sufficient data, and budget. Component checks and a joint compatibility check determine whether proposed versions replace the current pair (§§2.4–2.5).
- **Cross-domain demonstrations:** Experiments test different portions of this mechanism: fixed-policy predictor adaptation, successive policy versions, joint GUI updates, reaction search, and an immune-science workflow (§§3, 6). They do not uniformly evaluate the complete protocol.

## Evidence and main results

All findings below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Predictive selection improves code repair | On 200 CodeContests tasks across three seeds, fixed coder, four candidates per round, and at most three feedback rounds: hidden-test success was 29.2% versus 26.3% for the capacity-matched static verifier and 26.0% for random selection. Paired gain over verifier: +2.83 percentage points, 95% interval [+0.17, +5.50]. | §3.1; Fig. 3b; §6.2 |
| Better code predictions accompany the gain | Fixed world model versus static verifier: ranking accuracy 72.8% versus 66.5%; five-class Brier score 0.358 versus 0.488; online test-suite calls per task 3.71 versus 3.72, in the same CodeContests setting. | §3.1; Fig. 3c–d |
| Predictor adaptation adds repair success | Under the same CodeContests coder and candidate budget, adaptive world model reached 31.2% hidden-test success versus 29.2% fixed-model success, with 3.72 online test-suite calls per task. | §3.1; Fig. 3 |
| Additional code benchmarks favor predictive selection | Full-test success averaged across three seeds: 46.37% on APPS Introductory (1,000 problems) and 33.05% on LiveCodeBench v6 (1,055 problems), exceeding random selection and static verifier. Same coder, four candidates per round, at most three repair rounds; comparator rates are absent from the supplied prose. | §3.1; Fig. 3e–f; §§6.1–6.2 |
| Checkbox completion and action efficiency improve | MiniWoB++ success: 51% baseline, 79% adapted, 99% final. Baseline-to-final mean actions: 5.30→3.21, a 39.4% reduction. World-based control success: 49%, 96%, 98% across corresponding configurations. | §3.2; Fig. 4c, e |
| OSWorld gains are relative percentages | RSI-Anything success improved 18.4% relative to the initial agent and 5.5% relative to RLAnything; absolute success rates are not supplied. | §3.2; Fig. 4d; §6.1 |
| Successive laboratory policies improve | Five consecutive versions of one 4B policy scored 21.80%, 38.03%, 41.99%, 44.56%, and 46.01% on the same 200-task LabHorizon F200 Level-2 benchmark. Final score versus Actor–Simulator–Selector: 46.01% versus 45.32%; Level-1 accuracy: 0.700 versus 0.665. | §3.3; Fig. 5; §6.1 |
| Reaction search achieves higher endpoint yields | GC Suzuki–Miyaura screen, 20 experiments: best observed yield 93.0% versus Bayesian optimization’s 87.5%. Separate flow LC–MS screen, 40 experiments: mean best observed yield 96.15% versus 82.16%. | §3.4; Fig. 6 |
| Immune workflow produces a prospective hypothesis | Workflow evolution builds a predictor subsequently frozen during analysis; self-correction removes screened cytokine pairs and nominates SIRPα inhibition with IL-36γ-axis modulation for experimental testing. | §§3.5, 6.1 |

The evidence supports improvements in the reported settings, with particularly clear controls for code candidate budget and chemistry experiment count. Equal external-feedback usage does not establish equal total computation. The results also do not establish sustained joint policy–model improvement, reliable physical laboratory execution, or therapeutic efficacy (§5).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Joint multiround evaluation is needed to establish sustained mutual policy–model improvement. Laboratory results measure planning, chemistry covers two Suzuki–Miyaura screens, and the therapeutic hypothesis awaits testing (§5).
- **Author-stated:** Reliability assessments cover only observed candidate ranges; insufficient coverage cannot establish reliability (§2.4).
- **Analyst inference:** No reported ablation isolates the benefit of compatibility gating or conditional adaptation triggers. Their individual contributions therefore remain unclear (§§2.4–2.5, 3).
- **Analyst inference:** Laboratory progression follows one improvement process; uncertainty across independent runs is not established. GUI sample sizes, chemistry replication counts, and most result uncertainty estimates are unavailable (§§6.1–6.2).
- **Analyst inference:** The nearly comparable final checkbox control result limits attribution of the gain specifically to RSI-Anything’s added mechanisms (§3.2).

## Open research questions
- **Does joint improvement persist across many cycles?** The authors explicitly leave this open (§5). Run repeated joint-update experiments with held-out audits and matched feedback and compute budgets.
- **Which retention checks prevent regressions?** Their isolated effects are not reported. Ablate component checks, pair checks, and adaptation triggers while tracking task performance and prediction reliability.
- **Can reliability assessment detect blind spots from selective feedback?** Coverage limitations are acknowledged (§2.4). Compare utility-only selection with a declared exploration rule and audit predictions on independently sampled candidates.
- **Do scientific gains transfer to execution and broader settings?** Planning lacks equipment validation, chemistry is restricted to two screens, and the immune nomination is prospective (§5). Test laboratory plans on equipment, additional reaction families, and the nominated intervention experimentally.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly supports the reported settings and outcomes, but implementation and validity details are incomplete.

Material missing information includes table bodies for Tables 1–4; application-specific gate thresholds, update schedules, and training/adaptation splits; total compute costs; GUI evaluation counts and absolute OSWorld rates; chemistry replication and uncertainty; and quantitative immune-model evaluation, which is deferred to another paper. Resource contents and reproducibility are not established by the supplied links alone.
