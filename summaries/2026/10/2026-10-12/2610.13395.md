# ProgramGym: Scaling Training Environments for Long-Horizon Program Reproduction Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.13395) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13395)


## One-sentence takeaway
ProgramGym combines automated behavioral-test environment construction with a staged coding workflow, improving Qwen3.8-27B’s ProgramBench Tests Passed from 34.87% to 56.25% with fine-tuning and Program-Agent, while complete reproduction remains difficult (§6.2).

## Problem and motivation
Program reproduction requires agents to discover requirements from documentation and an executable, then implement observable behavior without reference source code or network access. The authors identify two gaps: scarce scalable environments with verifiable behavioral supervision, and workflows that fail to preserve exploration evidence and maintain buildable implementations over long development sessions (§§1, 3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Program-Env:** Converts repositories into reference executables, offline runtime sandboxes, documentation, and behavioral tests. Feedback repairs build failures; parallel module-level test generation uses coverage and static analysis. Four checks reject tests that fail on the reference, behave inconsistently, accept a dummy program, or contain weak assertions (§4; Appendix B).
- **Program-Agent:** Separates analysis, implementation, and review into fresh sessions connected by persistent artifacts. Implementation cannot access the reference executable; review compares immutable candidates against it. Independent compilation, bounded retries, and validated revisions provide recovery (§5; Appendix C).
- **Training assets and evaluation:** Constructs 1,648 environments and collects trajectories for supervised fine-tuning. Experiments distinguish training gains under a fixed scaffold from workflow gains under fixed model weights. Environments, construction code, and training artifacts are promised for release; availability is not established (§6; reproducibility statement).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Fine-tuning improves average behavioral recovery | On ProgramBench with Claude Code fixed, Qwen3.8-27B improves from **34.87% to 42.84% Tests Passed**, a **7.97 percentage-point** gain. Tests Passed is the macro-average of per-task pass rates. | §§6.1–6.2; Table 2 discussed in prose |
| Workflow improves both Qwen checkpoints | With model weights fixed, replacing Claude Code with Program-Agent at **K=5** raises Tests Passed from **34.87% to 55.71%** for base Qwen and **42.84% to 56.25%** for SFT. Both scaffolds have the stated limits of **8 CPU cores, 16 GB RAM, 30 GB storage, and 8 hours per task**. | §§6.1–6.2 |
| Reported flagship comparisons use different scaffolds | Qwen with Program-Agent scores **55.71%/56.25% Tests Passed**, versus Claude Opus 4.7 at **50.90%** and Gemini 3.5 Flash at **53.60%**, both using mini-SWE-agent. | §6.2 |
| Additional iterations improve SFT performance | Qwen3.8-27B-SFT with Program-Agent reaches **43.49%, 54.13%, and 56.25% Tests Passed** at **K=1, 3, and 5**, respectively, on ProgramBench. | §6.4; Figure 6 |
| Average gains do not imply near-complete solutions | With Claude Code, Qwen’s Almost Resolved rate—tasks passing at least **95%** of tests—falls from **3.50% to 1.50%** after SFT. Reported flagship mini-SWE-agent runs have **0.00% Resolved**. | §§6.1–6.2 |
| Environment construction is large and costly | **1,648 environments**, **665,512 validated tests**, mean core coverage **72.99%**, median **81.63%**, and total construction cost **136.77B tokens**. Coverage counts executable lines for C/C++/Rust and statement-weighted blocks for Go. | §6.3; Appendix B.4.2, B.5; Figure 17 |
| Training examples are context segments | **64,505 training trajectories** come from rollout requests across **1,648 tasks**; separate context segments count as trajectories. Fine-tuning runs for **two epochs**. | Appendix D.1 |

These results support improved **tested behavioral agreement** for the reported configurations. They do not establish semantic equivalence, consistent improvements in complete solutions, or model superiority independent of scaffold. The authors interpret coverage and validation as reliable supervision; analyst inference: neither code execution coverage nor rejection of a dummy program establishes discrimination against realistic, subtly incorrect implementations.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Finite tests establish agreement only on tested inputs (§3). Linux build failures, display/input-device requirements, and compilation or instrumentation timeouts exclude repositories; construction cost prevents processing the full candidate pool (Appendix A).
- **Author-stated:** The corpus is intentionally skewed toward low repository-complexity scores: **1,492 easy, 151 medium, and 5 hard** environments. This score measures code size and dependencies rather than reproduction difficulty (Appendix A).
- **Analyst inference:** Training evaluation covers one fine-tuned model family and ProgramBench. Broader transfer is not established (§6).
- **Analyst inference:** The supplied experiments do not separately ablate role isolation, handoff artifacts, review, and recovery, or compare test generation with and without each feedback component. Selected construction case studies provide narrower evidence than aggregate controlled comparisons (Appendix A, B.4.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Aggregate coverage mixes language-specific counting semantics, limiting direct cross-language interpretation (Appendix B.4.2).
- **Reproducibility risk:** Many tables appear only as captions, and release is prospective. MindForge is not compared because its artifacts were unavailable (Appendix A).

## Open research questions

1. **Which workflow components cause the gains?** The evaluation changes the scaffold as a bundle. Next experiment: ablate review, session isolation, persistent artifacts, and recovery under matched token and time budgets.
2. **How well do generated tests reject realistic faulty programs?** Dummy rejection and assertion linting do not measure this directly. Next experiment: evaluate mutation variants and independently constructed partial reproductions.
3. **Why does SFT improve average pass rate while reducing Almost Resolved?** The paper reports this divergence without isolating its cause. Next experiment: vary trajectory filtering and sampling, then compare paired task-level outcomes across fidelity thresholds.
4. **Do benefits extend to harder and excluded software?** Corpus filtering and complexity skew constrain evidence. Next experiment: construct a stratified harder corpus and evaluate transfer separately from repository size.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** Core methods and headline results are explicit, but the supplied text lacks full table contents, uncertainty estimates or repeated-run results, exact trajectory acceptance thresholds, the complete training hyperparameters, numerical output-token costs, component ablations, and confirmed artifact availability.
