# SAGE: Salient Factor Discovery and Generation with Visual Foundation Representations

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.39635) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39635)


## One-sentence takeaway
SAGE uses background/target labels to factorize frozen visual representations into decodable common and salient content, improving subtype discovery and reference-conditioned diversity while leaving residual information overlap and generation-quality tradeoffs.

## Problem and motivation
Contrastive analysis seeks image factors specific to a target group while separating content shared with a background group. The authors identify a gap: existing generative approaches reconstruct complex images poorly, while strong contrastive representations lack decoders. They aim to discover unnamed subtypes and generate examples from a reference image without subtype labels or text descriptions (§1).

## Approach and contributions
1. **Spatial factor discovery:** Trainable encoders split the frozen RAEv2/DINOv3 spatial latent into additive common and salient factors. Reconstruction, adversarial swaps, sparsity, image/latent consistency, and Cycle NCE encourage target-specific detail with limited shared-content leakage (§3.1).
2. **Discovery and manipulation:** The same factors support subtype probing, clustering, attribute removal, transfer, and interpolation. Evaluations cover digits, eyewear, and retinal diseases; eyewear analysis also identifies label errors and finer visual groups (§§4.2–4.5).
3. **Generation by example:** A separately trained, pretrained-initialized diffusion transformer uses a max-pooled salient reference representation to generate samples retaining its subtype while varying other content (§3.2).

The central methodological contribution is factorizing the full decodable spatial foundation latent and reusing its salient component for discovery, editing, and generation (§2).

These results support useful, selective representations and more varied generation in the tested settings. They do not establish complete disentanglement, automatic discovery of the number of subtypes, clinical validity, or uniformly better generation: raw conditioning has lower conditional gFID on both datasets.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Dataset labels underdetermine correlated-factor allocation. Digit identity remains probe-accessible in the common factor despite successful decoded removal (Appendix E; Table 7).
- **Author-stated:** Natural-image encoder/decoder priors limit biomedical reconstruction; OCT edits are qualitative, not clinical evidence (§4.5; Appendix E).
- **Analyst inference:** The same-backbone SepCLR comparison also changes pooling and evaluation dimensionality, so it does not isolate training objectives alone (Table 9).
- **Analyst inference:** Clustering assumes the evaluation class count; fine sunglasses styles lack independently annotated validation (Appendices B.5, A.4).
- **Analyst inference:** Reported clustering variability covers k-means seeds, not independent model training runs. Loss ablations are confined to Digits-ImageNet, and OCT lacks comparative baselines (Appendix B.5; Tables 3, 6).
- **Reproducibility boundary:** Code, models, and audited labels are promised upon publication; their availability is not established by the supplied text.

## Open research questions
- **Can SAGE separate target attributes from correlated nuisance differences?** Label-based allocation remains ambiguous; test controlled background/target confounds with independently labeled nuisance attributes (Appendix E).
- **How much of the SepCLR advantage comes from objectives versus readout?** Pooling and dimensions differ; compare both methods with matched pooling and representation sizes (Table 9).
- **Can subtype counts and finer styles be discovered reliably without evaluation labels?** Current k-means uses known counts; test label-free count selection and independently annotated eyewear styles (Appendices B.5, A.4).
- **Would biomedical pretraining improve OCT discovery and editing?** The authors identify inherited natural-image priors; replace the frozen autoencoder and compare patient-disjoint metrics plus blinded expert assessment (Appendix E).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported method and benchmark findings; broader generalization remains uncertain.

Material missing information includes independent-training uncertainty, matched-readout ablations, OCT baseline results, clinical assessment of edits, measured training time or cost, generation FID sample counts and subtype-classifier training details, and confirmation of released assets.
