# MetaKernelBench: Measuring GPU Kernel Knowledge Transfer Beyond Code

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.05014) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.05014)


## One-sentence takeaway
MetaKernelBench finds that natural-language experience transferred between GPU kernel DSLs can improve fresh attempts on the same problem, but benefits depend on the source’s standing relative to the target baseline and coexist with frequent regressions.

## Problem and motivation
Kernel benchmarks measure implementation correctness and speed, but do not measure whether an agent’s retained experience helps later attempts. The authors address this gap by changing the implementation DSL while holding the problem, workload, hardware, and verification contract fixed (§1). This tests reuse of optimization knowledge without transferring source code, while preserving the relevance of prior experience.

## Approach and contributions
- **Evaluation formulation:** Same-problem, cross-DSL knowledge transfer as a dimension complementary to implementation quality (§1).
- **Benchmark and protocol:** 74 fused-subgraph problems across six families, each paired in CuTe DSL and TIRx. Solo attempts produce natural-language skills; every available skill, including those from failed attempts, becomes the sole extra input to a fresh attempt by the same model in the other DSL. Source implementations and trajectories are withheld (§§2.1–2.3).
- **Empirical characterization:** Six models evaluated in both directions, with analyses of source quality, skill content, uptake, and harm (§4).

Target baseline and treatment each receive 40 minutes and at most 10 feedback submissions, with final evaluation on an NVIDIA B200 (§3.2). Valid implementations may mix eager PyTorch and DSL kernels, but must perform a required output-dependent step in the designated DSL (§2.1).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Transfer produces mixed outcomes | Across six models, both directions, and 74 problems, paired lift ranges from **−19.1% to +29.3%**; four models gain in both directions. Help rates are **21.6%–40.5%**, harm rates **16.2%–44.6%**. | §4.1; Table 1 and Figure 3 references |
| Source standing tracks transfer outcomes | Pooling models and directions: source above target solo tier yields **71.2% help / 8.1% harm**; same tier **25.7% / 21.8%**; below **10.6% / 53.8%**. Tiers are failure, passing below 2×, and passing at least 2× eager-reference speedup. | §4.2; Figure 4 |
| Aggregate gains can depend on one family | GPT-5.6 Sol’s CuTe→TIRx lift is **8.7%** overall, **99.1%** on 10 Quantization problems, and **−1.1%** on the other 64. | §4.1 |
| Unsupported conclusions associate with harm | “Unsupported closure” is **7–17 percentage points** more prevalent in harmed than helped comparisons for five of six models, averaged across directions. Labels describe stated claims rather than independently verifying them. | §4.3; Figure 5; Appendix C.1 |
| Failure mechanisms differ across models | Search truncation and unverified adoption account for **65%–86%** of assigned harm causes for the three larger models; faulty execution, toolchain friction, and budget/artifact issues account for **63% and 94%** for GLM-5.3-Flash and Qwen3.8-Flash. | §4.4; Appendix C.2 |
| Treatment outcomes track source speed more closely | Across **841** comparisons with transferred skills, Spearman correlation with source speedup is **0.35** for solo and **0.51** for treatment; nonpassing attempts receive a **0.05×** floor before ranking. | Appendix B.2 |

“Paired lift” is a composite score, not a pure runtime improvement: jointly passing runtime ratios are clipped to [¼, 4], failure-to-pass transitions score 4, pass-to-failure transitions score ¼, and joint failures or skipped treatments score 1. Help requires a ratio ≥1.25; harm requires ≤0.8 (§3.3).

The evidence supports beneficial and harmful reuse within this controlled setting. Content associations and selected trajectories do not isolate the causal effect of particular statements (§4.3), and results do not establish generalization beyond the same problem.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** One hardware platform, two DSLs, six models, one scaffold, one skill directive, and one budget; no evaluation of new problems/workloads or accumulated skills (Appendix E.1).
- **Author-stated attribution limit:** Same-model transfer jointly measures skill distillation and utilization. Hybrid implementations also prevent paired lift from separating eager-PyTorch advice from kernel strategy transfer (§2.2; Appendix E.1).
- **Author-stated cost caveat:** Matched target budgets exclude the additional source attempt needed to produce the skill (§2.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Single-attempt comparisons leave run-to-run variability uncertain; repeated-run intervals are not supplied (§4.1).
- **Analyst inference:** LLM annotations and precedence-based assignment of one harm cause limit mechanistic certainty; annotator agreement is unavailable in the supplied text (Appendix C).
- **Scoring sensitivity:** Missing skills count as neutral, including 43 Qwen3.8-Flash treatments; pooled outcomes can hide opposing family effects (§4.1).

## Open research questions
- **Can distillation and utilization be separated?** Same-model transfer conflates them. Run a crossed source-model/target-model experiment with fixed skills (Appendix E.2).
- **Does removing unsupported closure reduce harm?** Current evidence is associative. Randomize original skills versus versions with individual unsupported prohibitions removed (§4.3; Appendix E.2).
- **Is transfer worthwhile under equal total compute?** Source generation adds cost. Compare source-plus-treatment against solo search receiving the combined budget (§2.2).
- **Does knowledge survive problem changes or repeated transfer?** Neither is tested. Transfer skills to related family members and chain successive rounds, tracking correctness, performance, and claim propagation (Appendix E.2).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the protocol and reported findings; **medium** in mechanistic interpretations.

Material gaps include full numerical cells for Tables 1–4, repeated-run uncertainty, annotation models and agreement, complete prompts and task catalog contents, and a confirmed public benchmark/code/trajectory release location.
