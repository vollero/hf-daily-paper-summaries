# Reflex: Visually Grounded Reactive Humanoid Control

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.12772) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12772)


## One-sentence takeaway
Reflex separates humanoid catching into motor learning, object-dynamics learning, and visual grounding, achieving 85.7% simulated catch success and 65% real-robot success at 2.5 m, with long-range perception and retention remaining weaknesses (§§V-B, VI).

## Problem and motivation
Catching a thrown box requires predicting its trajectory, coordinating whole-body interception, absorbing impact, and retaining it while balanced. Moving onboard cameras, latency, and near-contact occlusion complicate perception. The authors identify a gap in combining these capabilities on a real humanoid using ordinary objects and onboard vision rather than external sensing or markers (§I).

## Approach and contributions
- **Three-stage learning framework:** PPO first learns state-based catching with human motion tracking and adversarial motion priors. Action matching then initializes an object-dynamics encoder and controller, which PPO jointly refines (§§IV-A–B).
- **Compact perception–control interface:** An eight-dimensional learned latent connects delayed privileged observations to whole-body control. A temporal RGB-D encoder subsequently predicts that latent while the controller stays fixed (§§IV-B–C).
- **Visual grounding and hardware evaluation:** A heatmap localizer, geometric depth features, and camera ego-motion feed a GRU. Experiments isolate temporal inputs, initialization, latency, appearance, and retention, followed by real Unitree G1 trials (§§IV-C, V).

The contribution is the integration and training decomposition; PPO, motion priors, latent regression, and data aggregation are acknowledged building blocks.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Visual input largely preserves simulated catching | Reflex catches 3290/3840 throws (85.7%), versus 87.9% with privileged latent input to the same decoder; six controllers, 640 held-out throws each, reference simulation setting. | §§V-A–B; Table VII |
| Real catching declines with distance | G1 catch success is 65%, 40%, and 15% at 2.5, 5, and 7 m; 20 regular-box trials per condition. Human reference: 85.0%, 68.3%, and 28.3%, pooled over three people and 60 trials per distance. | §V-B; Table II |
| Temporal history and ego-motion matter | Across five policies per variant, single-frame input loses 35.6/39.8 percentage points on centered/0.6 m-offset throws; removing ego-motion loses 10.4/22.6 points. | §V-E; Table V |
| Action matching improves initialization | BC initialization improves mean visual catch success by 5.2 points over position/velocity weight mapping; gain holds in five of six controller pairs. | §V-D; Table VII |
| Long-range visual training coverage matters | At simulated 5 m, visual success is 43.0% versus 79.7% privileged. Expanding Stage 3 data to 2.5–7 m raises visual success to 75.8% at 5 m and 36.7% at 7 m; hardware transfer is untested. | §§V-E, VI |
| Reaching a box does not ensure retention | Small/large-box visual catch rates are 25.5%/39.7%, versus interception rates of 58.1%/86.1%; one checkpoint, 640 throws per condition, geometry-specific sustained-hold criterion. | §§V-A, V-C; Table I |
| Learned temporal filtering has no clear advantage over tuned filtering | With the same localizer and latent decoder, five additional controllers yield a visual-model margin of +0.3 ± 5.2 points across centered and 0.6 m-offset conditions. | §V-E |

These results support effective visual substitution for privileged input within the tested setting. They do not establish broad reactive-skill generalization or a consistent advantage of learned temporal filtering over a tuned Kalman filter. Catch success also does not establish settled posture: torso excursion varies substantially across controllers (§V-C).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Evaluation covers one task, with only 20 real trials per condition and confidence intervals spanning up to approximately ±20 points. Long-range visual grounding is the main weakness (§VI).
- **Author-stated:** The controller is effectively size-blind. Rendering omits motion blur, exposure dynamics, and structured depth artifacts (§§IV-C, V-C, VI).
- **Measurement limitation:** Hardware latency was measured for the detector-based pipeline, not the deployed visual-latent pipeline; several robustness and delay comparisons use single training seeds (§§V-A, V-D, VI).
- **Comparison caveat:** Full-state regression must predict orientation, angular velocity, and size that the geometric visual features barely expose, so its poorer performance does not isolate latent supervision alone (§V-E).
- **Analyst inference:** The evidence supports this decomposition for catching, but does not establish that it is necessary or superior to a jointly trained pixel-based controller; no such direct comparison is reported.

## Open research questions
- **Can broader visual training improve hardware range?** Expanded throw-distance data improve simulation but remain untested on hardware. Next experiment: compare original and expanded visual encoders on matched real throws (§VI).
- **Can size-aware control improve retention?** Size is poorly represented in the latent, and nonreference boxes are often intercepted without being held. Next experiment: add explicit geometry conditioning and evaluate interception, retention, and torso stability (§§V-C–D).
- **How robust is the complete visual pipeline to real latency and image artifacts?** Runtime measurements cover another perception pipeline, while rendering omits relevant artifacts. Next experiment: measure visual-latent latency and test matched delay, blur, exposure, and depth perturbations (§§IV-C, V-F, VI).
- **Does the decomposition transfer to another reactive skill?** Only box catching is evaluated. Next experiment: train an avoidance or handoff task using the same staged interface and compare training cost and success (§VII).
