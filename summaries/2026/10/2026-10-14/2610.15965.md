# Scaling Repeated-Data Pretraining: Batch Size, Regularization, and GPU-Time Tradeoffs

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.15965) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15965)


## One-sentence takeaway

On the studied dense language models, jointly tuning batch size, repetitions, and regularization improves repeated-data pretraining, while deadline-based comparisons show that using more GPUs through larger batches can worsen validation loss (§§3–5).

## Problem and motivation

When unique training text is finite, additional compute requires repeated exposure to the same data, increasing overfitting risk. The authors identify two gaps: prior repeated-data studies largely fixed batch size, and aggregate compute budgets obscure the difference between GPU availability and sequential training time (§§1–2).

The practical question is how to choose batch size, training duration, and regularization when fresh data are scarce and training must meet a deadline.

## Approach and contributions

1. **Joint batch and repetition tuning.** The study tunes learning rate, weight decay, and stopping epoch separately for each batch size, finding an interior batch optimum rather than a consistent advantage for either extreme (§3).
2. **A repetition-aware recipe.** Controlled ablations evaluate document repacking each epoch, embedding tying, and positive embedding decay. The contribution is evidence for their combination in repeated-data training; the ingredients themselves already exist (§4; Appendix C.1).
3. **Separate GPU and time constraints.** The authors construct frontiers from evaluated training configurations and separately model latency-oriented parallelism. The latter systems results are simulations, not end-to-end measurements (§5; Appendices D–F).

Experiments use dense decoder-only Transformers, DCLM-Baseline text, AdamW, and a warmup–stable–decay schedule (§3.1).

## Evidence and main results

Here, **B** denotes global batch size in sequences and **E** denotes passes over the unique-data pool. Validation CE uses the fixed, document-disjoint 100M-token DCLM-Baseline split.

These results support recipe and batch tuning within the measured setting. The proposed gradient-noise regularization explanation is consistent with training–validation gaps, but is not causally established (§3.2). Downstream results broadly support intermediate batches, although accuracy is noisier than BPB (Appendix B.3).

The GPU frontiers use **exactly** the indicated GPU count with fixed microbatch size. **Analyst inference:** worse results at a higher exact count do not establish that a larger available GPU budget is harmful when unused GPUs are allowed (Appendix D.3).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** measured models span 153M–1.5B nominal parameters and data pools span 0.333B–3B tokens. Transfer to larger models, other corpora, optimizers, schedules, context lengths, and MoE architectures remains unvalidated (§6).
- **Systems evidence:** latency-oriented parallelism is simulated; deadlines in the main frontiers use optimizer-step equivalents rather than full deployment measurements (§5; Appendices D–F).
- **Incomplete saturation:** the 153M model on the 3B-token pool was still improving when stopped at E640, so its endpoint is not a demonstrated optimum (Appendix D.1).
- **Analyst inference:** pool-resampling robustness does not establish robustness to independent initialization seeds. Tying also changes parameter counts, so recipe comparisons do not hold trainable parameter count constant (Appendices A.1, B.1).
- **Reproducibility:** extensive configurations are supplied, but code and exact commands are deferred until acceptance. Downstream metrics can disagree with CE, notably for DiLoCo (Reproducibility Statement; Appendix C.2).

## Open research questions

- **Does the optimum transfer to larger dense models and MoE?** The authors explicitly leave this unresolved (§6). Next experiment: repeat joint batch/regularization sweeps at larger scales and with matched dense/MoE settings.
- **Can large batches recover small-batch generalization?** Noise injection was unstable and local updates gave mixed CE versus downstream results (Appendix C.2). Next experiment: compare tuned local-update methods under matched wall-clock and GPU budgets.
- **Do latency-first gains survive real systems overhead?** Current evidence is simulated (Appendix D.2). Next experiment: benchmark fixed-global-batch configurations end to end, including communication and scheduling.
- **Which checkpoint-selection metric best predicts downstream utility?** Downstream evaluation is noisy and sometimes conflicts with CE (Appendices B.3, C.2). Next experiment: compare CE-, BPB-, and accuracy-selected checkpoints across independent runs.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The numerical recipe findings are well documented within the measured scope; large-scale and wall-clock conclusions are less established.

Material missing information includes released experiment code and commands, end-to-end latency validation, broad initialization-seed replication, and target-scale or MoE validation. Several plotted downstream and GPU-frontier values are not numerically tabulated in the supplied text.
