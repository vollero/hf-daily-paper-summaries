# Let the Mind Conceive, Let the Hand Achieve: Following Multimodal Instructions for Long-Horizon Robotic Manipulation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.18352) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18352)


## One-sentence takeaway
Orchestra combines explicit subtask orchestration, spatial grounding, visual memory, and learned progress detection to improve sustained manipulation, achieving 7.350 average consecutive subtasks versus HarnessVLA’s 2.986 on RMBench-Compose with environment-provided grounding (§5.3).

## Problem and motivation
Long-horizon robots must preserve instruction order, select the intended objects and destinations, and recognize when to advance as the scene changes. The authors argue that strong local action policies and memory mechanisms alone do not reliably keep these requirements aligned with the active subtask (§1). Their central question is whether policies trained on atomic instructions and short demonstrations can execute longer, complete instructions.

## Approach and contributions
1. **Shared-context orchestration:** Orchestra decomposes a complete instruction into ordered subtasks and maintains the active goal, completed-task records, recent observations, and spatial grounding. Grounding and local histories reset at transitions (§§3.1–3.2).
2. **Adapted policy and separate evaluator:** A π₀.₅-based policy combines spatial prompts with temporally fused visual history. An independently trained evaluator predicts demonstration-derived progress categories; its terminal category triggers advancement. Plan termination and externally measured task success remain distinct (§§3.3–3.4; Appendix B).
3. **RMBench-Compose:** Five adapted RMBench environments support state-feasible atomic-skill compositions, configurable order and length, spatial annotations, and external success predicates. Experiments test composition beyond atomic-training horizons (§4).

The contribution is the coordinated system and its benchmark, rather than the underlying orchestration, pointing, or segmentation models.

## Evidence and main results

ASL counts consecutive subtasks completed in order. SSR counts successful attempted subtasks and excludes unreached ones. Cross-environment averages are unweighted (Appendix D.2).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Higher mean RMBench success | Across nine tasks, Orchestra reports 68.22% versus Mem-0’s 42.00%, a 26.22 percentage-point gain. Comparison mixes official baseline results with author-run evaluations. | §5.2; Table 1 referenced; Appendix D.3 |
| Harness improves composition with the same base policy | On RMBench-Compose, π₀.₅ versus Orchestra using π₀.₅: Atomic SR remains 85.0%; SSR rises from 47.36% to 70.78%; ASL rises from 0.932 to 2.884. | §5.3; Fig. 3 |
| Complete system improves sustained execution | Five-environment means: 95.2% Atomic SR, 86.69% SSR, and 7.350 ASL; HarnessVLA achieves 2.986 ASL. Evaluation uses 100 episodes per environment/configuration and environment-provided grounding. | §§5.1, 5.3; Fig. 3; Appendix D.1 |
| Components contribute under simulation conditions | Mean ASL falls from 7.350 to 1.502 without orchestration, 2.500 without visual memory, and 5.814 without grounding. The no-orchestration policy is retrained on complete instructions and corresponding demonstrations. | §5.4; Fig. 4; Appendix E.2 |
| Oracle transitions yield similar aggregate performance | Simulator-state transitions achieve 7.374 mean ASL versus 7.350 with learned transitions. This compares rollout performance, not detection accuracy. | §5.4; Fig. 4 |
| Adapted policy improves hardware execution | With the same harness, complete Orchestra versus the base-policy variant improves mean SSR from 72.77% to 82.09% and ASL from 2.20 to 2.75, a 25.0% increase. Four tasks use 20 matched instructions each per configuration and predicted/tracked grounding. | §5.5; Fig. 5; Appendix D.2 |
| Action execution dominates recorded failures | Action execution accounts for 87.7% of 65 recorded hardware failure samples; the sample includes subtask/task failures and interrupted trials excluded from evaluation rollouts. | Appendix E.4; Fig. 13 |

These results support improved composition within the evaluated settings. The harness-only configuration remains below HarnessVLA in mean ASL, so the full advantage cannot be attributed to orchestration alone (§5.3). Similar oracle-transition ASL does not establish equivalent completion detection, and aggregate gains do not establish reliable completion of entire instructions.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-reported scope:** Simulation uses reference grounding, and performance remains task-dependent. The conclusion calls for longer hardware sequences with predicted grounding (§6). Hardware instructions contain only three to five subtasks (§5.5).
- **Author-reported failures:** Planning can omit or duplicate objects; grounding can select incorrect entities; completion detection can lag; grasping and placement remain unreliable (Appendix E.4).
- **Analyst inference—comparison limits:** Mixed baseline sources and privileged simulation grounding limit attribution of gains to architecture alone. The supplied text does not establish matched spatial information across all baselines (Appendix D.3).
- **Analyst inference—measurement limits:** One evaluation length per simulation environment cannot isolate length effects from task difficulty. SSR excludes unreached subtasks, so it does not measure whole-sequence reliability (Appendices D.1–D.2).
- **Analyst inference—uncertainty and selection:** No confidence intervals or repeated-training-seed results are supplied. Excluded interrupted hardware trials and the mixed failure sample prevent interpreting failure-category shares as rollout failure probabilities (Appendix E.4).

## Open research questions
1. **How much advantage survives imperfect grounding?** Simulation supplies environment masks. Compare predicted versus reference grounding on identical composed episodes, including controlled localization and tracking errors.
2. **How does reliability scale with sequence length?** Each simulation environment uses one length, while hardware sequences are shorter. Sweep instruction length within each environment while holding atomic skills and object distributions fixed.
3. **Can completion supervision generalize beyond demonstration timing?** Progress targets derive from temporal position, and delayed transitions occur on hardware. Compare temporal labels with state-based completion labels under varied execution speeds and pauses.
4. **Can plan validation prevent instruction omissions?** Recorded plans duplicate or omit entities. Test coverage and duplicate checks against the existing planner using identical instructions and scenes.
