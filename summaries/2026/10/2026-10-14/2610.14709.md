# Vela: User Behavior as Supervision Sparks LLM Intelligence

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.14709) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14709)


## One-sentence takeaway
Vela converts real user histories into behavior-grounded training tasks, improving a Qwen base model on user-related and general benchmarks, although the experiments do not isolate behavioral supervision from teacher-generated trajectory effects.

## Problem and motivation
The authors target LLMs’ ability to infer individual preferences, intentions, and future actions from long behavior histories. They argue that static text and synthetic training data inadequately capture evolving individual behavior, while prior behavior-based training focuses on particular applications rather than broader transfer (§1).

The practical challenge is turning heterogeneous platform logs into diverse tasks with usable supervision.

## Approach and contributions
- **Behavior-grounded task construction:** Split histories into visible past and hidden future. Instantiate 91 templates covering six behavior categories. Predictive questions use future events as ground truth; summarization and attribution questions use evidence-grounded rubrics (§§2.1–2.2).
- **Training-data pipeline:** Retain intermediate-difficulty tasks, generate four trajectories per generator, and accept the highest-scoring trajectory only when it exceeds the filtering-stage mean. This produces approximately 75,000 teacher-generated Vela-Instruct samples and 9,375 self-generated Vela-RFT samples (§2.3).
- **Evaluation and analysis:** Introduce VelaBench with held-out users, evaluate transfer to user-related and general benchmarks, and study data scale, difficulty filtering, and subsequent GSPO reinforcement learning (§§2.4–4.2).

The proposed contribution is the broad behavior-supervision framework and its empirical evaluation; the paper also explicitly relies on LLM-generated questions, rubrics, and trajectories.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong in-domain improvement | Vela-Instruct fine-tuning raises VelaBench’s aggregate set-F1/rubric score from **51.5 to 63.0**, **+11.5 points**, on 982 held-out-user tasks. | Table 1; §§2.4, 3.2 |
| Uneven user-related transfer | Four-benchmark mean rises **41.3→47.5**, **+6.2 points**. AdRecommendation rubric score rises **51.1→62.3**; PurchaseIntent recall@20 rises only **43.8→44.0**. | Table 1; §2.4 |
| General benchmark gains | Five-benchmark mean rises **54.2→58.2**, **+4.0 points**; all five reported scores improve. IFBench gains **6.7 points** and CL-bench Life gains **5.6**. This mean combines different benchmark metrics. | Table 1; §3.3 |
| Self-generated training gives smaller gains | Vela-RFT yields **+2.0 points** on VelaBench and **+1.6** on the user-related OOD mean; general OOD mean is unchanged. It uses 9,375 samples versus approximately 75,000 for Vela-Instruct. | §2.3; Table 1 |
| Scaling is not uniformly monotonic | From **4.7K→75K** samples, VelaBench rises **59.2→63.0**. User-related OOD mean peaks at **47.6** with 37.5K, then reaches **47.5**; general OOD mean peaks at **59.0** with 18.8K, then reaches **58.2**. | Figure 5; §3.4 |
| SFT initialization improves downstream RL | With the same GSPO setup, initialization from the **18.8K-sample** Vela checkpoint versus the base yields **66.2 vs 58.5** on VelaBench and **37.6 vs 29.9** on ProfileSummary: **+7.7 points** each. | Figure 6; §4.1; Appendix E |
| Filtering improves user-related OOD scores | At **37.5K** final samples per variant, difficulty filtering adds **1.1 points** to the user-related OOD mean; VelaBench remains **62.4** for both variants. | Table 2; §4.2 |

These results support gains for the tested training pipeline and base model. They do not establish that behavioral supervision alone causes the gains: teacher trajectories, task diversity, and selection are bundled together. The authors’ broader-intelligence interpretation rests on transfer across the selected benchmarks, rather than a separately measured intelligence construct.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Training uses one social media platform; the conclusion calls for extension to more tasks and scenarios (§§2.1, 6). There is no dedicated limitations section.
- **Analyst inference—teacher/judge dependence:** Qwen3.8 generates Vela-Instruct trajectories, composes rubric tasks, and scores several evaluations. No human-agreement or alternative-judge validation is reported (§§2.2–2.4).
- **Analyst inference—causal attribution:** No matched nonbehavior training control or predictive-versus-rubric ablation isolates the source of transfer. Teacher-versus-self-generated comparisons also differ substantially in data volume (§2.3).
- **Analyst inference—generalization and uncertainty:** Only one base model is fine-tuned. No confidence intervals or repeated-run variability are reported; small improvements therefore have unclear robustness (Table 1).
- **Analyst inference—reproducibility:** Templates, prompts, and code are promised, but raw-data and model-weight release are not established. Platform identity and user-population details are unavailable (p. 10).

## Open research questions
1. **What produces general transfer?** Behavioral grounding and teacher distillation are confounded. Compare matched-budget behavior-grounded and nonbehavior tasks using the same teacher and selection procedure.
2. **Do gains survive independent scoring?** Several outcomes rely on the training pipeline’s scorer. Re-score blinded outputs with human annotators and alternative judges, including agreement measurements.
3. **Does transfer extend across platforms and populations?** Single-platform training leaves this unresolved. Evaluate on a separate platform with user-disjoint histories and independently constructed tasks.
4. **What scales beyond in-domain performance?** OOD averages plateau or decline at larger tested scales. Run repeated, token-budget-controlled scaling experiments and report per-task uncertainty.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly documents the pipeline and reported comparisons, but broader causal claims remain uncertain.

Material missing information includes statistical uncertainty, judge–human agreement, matched-budget supervision controls, detailed population characteristics, SFT compute costs, and confirmed availability of data and model weights. Some Chinese appendix text is corrupted, limiting inspection of benchmark examples and translated prompts.
