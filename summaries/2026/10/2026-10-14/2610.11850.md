# VEDJE: Video-Efficient Discriminative Joint Encoder for Scalable Video-Text Retrieval

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.11850) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11850)


## One-sentence takeaway
VEDJE improves video–text retrieval through joint reranking of compact, reusable frame-indexed caches, with a fourfold cache reduction costing only 0.2 percentage points of text-to-video R@1 in its MSR-VTT VideoPrism setting (§4.3).

## Problem and motivation
Independent video and text encoders retrieve candidates cheaply but lack joint token interaction for distinguishing similar scenes with different events. Joint verification is more expressive, yet video encoding and long visual sequences make it expensive. The authors target a query-independent cache that preserves evidence across sampled times while keeping storage and online scoring small (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Frame-indexed compression:** A shared transformer decoder compresses each sampled frame’s frozen patch features into a small token group. Ordered concatenation preserves separate groups across time, extending the existing cached image-reranking recipe to video (§§2, 3.2).
- **Training-only feature-change prediction:** Compressed tokens predict differences between frozen features at sampled times. The predictor is discarded after training, adding no retrieval-time computation (§3.3).
- **Compact joint scoring and evaluation:** A roughly 33M-parameter encoder jointly reads text and cached tokens, then incorporates an embedded first-stage relevance score. Matched controls evaluate cache organization, auxiliary supervision, scoring, storage, and complete-query latency (§§3.4–4.7).

Training combines matching, contrastive alignment, masked-language modeling, and feature-change losses at equal weights; the visual encoder remains frozen (§3.5).

## Evidence and main results

All results below are author-reported; recall values are percentages.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Gains survive first-stage fine-tuning | MSR-VTT, fine-tuned VideoCLIP-XL, 64-token cache: T2V R@1 **56.2→59.8**; V2T **55.1→58.8** | §4.2; Table 15 |
| Improvements extend across benchmarks | With VideoPrism and zero-shot VideoCLIP-XL, R@1 improves in both directions on all four datasets; gains vary substantially by setting | Table 15 |
| Compression has direction-dependent costs | MSR-VTT/VideoPrism with feature-change supervision: **48→12 KiB**, T2V R@1 **54.6→54.4**, V2T **54.6→53.1**; three-seed means | §4.3; Table 3 |
| Separate frame groups outperform pooling | MSR-VTT/VideoPrism, matched 64-token caches and scorer: before auxiliary supervision, separate groups improve T2V and V2T R@1 by **2.0 points each** over attention pooling | §4.4; Table 4 |
| Auxiliary supervision especially helps compact T2V caches | MSR-VTT/VideoPrism: feature-change supervision adds **0.6** T2V R@1 points at 64 tokens and **1.9** at 16 tokens | §4.5; Table 3 |
| Joint scoring improves use of the same cache | MSR-VTT/VideoPrism, 20 candidates, 64-token cache: joint encoder **54.6** versus trained MaxSim **51.9** T2V R@1, with matched data, schedule, and prior input | §4.6; Table 5 |
| Measured online overhead is modest in the tested setup | MSR-VTT/VideoPrism T2V, one L40S, query batch 1, host-cache I/O, 20 candidates: **5.79 ms** complete query versus approximately **4.3 ms** first stage alone; medians over 200 runs after 30 warmups | §4.7; Table 6; §A.6 |

The matched controls support benefits from cache organization, auxiliary training, and joint scoring under the tested conditions. They do not establish that the model explicitly understands motion or temporal order. Cross-system accuracy/parameter comparisons retain different training protocols, and million-video storage figures are extrapolations rather than measured deployments (§A.6; Appendix B).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Caches are query-independent; changing the backbone or compressor requires re-indexing. Retrieval stages are trained separately. English-captioned web-video benchmarks do not establish multilingual performance (§5).
- **Analyst inference:** Reranking cannot recover relevant items absent from the first-stage candidate set. Increasing candidate count does not consistently improve accuracy (§3.1; §A.9).
- **Observed qualification:** Feature-change benefits depend on prediction offset and metric. Some tested offsets lower R@1, and adding the default auxiliary loss lowers T2V R@5 in the loss-stack experiment (§§A.13–A.14).
- **Measurement scope:** Latency uses one GPU and batch size; cache residency affects results. Payload figures exclude metadata and the separate first-stage index (§§A.2, A.6).
- **Evidence availability:** Many retrieved tables contain captions without data rows. The code-release subsection is empty, leaving released artifacts unclear (§A.5).

## Open research questions
- **Does the cache preserve temporal ordering or mainly event coverage?** Frame-group ablations do not isolate order sensitivity. Next experiment: reverse or shuffle cache groups and evaluate order-sensitive queries.
- **Can adaptive cache access improve difficult queries?** The fixed query-independent cache cannot selectively expand evidence (§5). Next experiment: compare selective expansion with fixed caches under matched storage and latency budgets.
- **Do the gains transfer beyond English web videos?** Current evaluation leaves that scope open (§5). Next experiment: repeat matched first-stage comparisons on multilingual captions and a distinct video domain.

## Confidence and missing information
