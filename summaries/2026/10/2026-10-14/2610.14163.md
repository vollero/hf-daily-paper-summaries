# AutoEnvScaling: Automating the Data Flywheel with Terminal Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.14163) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14163)


## One-sentence takeaway
AutoEnvScaling lets terminal agents build and revise solver-guided RL environments, improving held-out terminal-task performance over static training pools and enabling bounded self-improvement after externally supplied cold-start training (§§4.2–4.3).

## Problem and motivation
Terminal-agent RL needs executable environments with reliable rewards, but human environment design limits supply and must be repeated as solvers improve. The authors identify a gap in existing generation methods: researchers typically predetermine the generation workflow, even when tasks receive execution feedback. They propose delegating environment diagnosis, construction, testing, and curriculum adaptation to an agent (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Agentic environment construction:** A proposer reads solver trajectories and verifier outputs, then builds complete Harbor tasks containing instructions, a container environment, a reference solution, and tests. It can execute and repair tasks using child sandboxes and restricted web access (§3.1).
- **Adaptive training curriculum:** External admission checks require successful construction, a passing reference solution, a failing do-nothing solution, task review, and solver-calibrated difficulty with reward variation. The pool is refreshed as the solver improves (§§3.2–3.3; Appendix G.4).
- **Proposer–solver co-evolution:** Fixed-weight proposers improve their persistent harness; an open-weight shared policy instead receives separate proposer and solver RL rewards. The shared-policy experiment tests whether a model can improve through environments it generates itself (§3.3; §4.3).
- **Extensions:** The framework generates environments across several agentic domains and supplies clarification-training tasks for HiL-Bench. Cross-domain generation is demonstrated, but downstream training gains across those domains are not (§4.5; Appendix C).

These results support improved executable-task generation and useful curricula under the reported settings. They do not establish independently verified task correctness, equal-total-compute superiority, sustained recursive improvement, or downstream training benefits across all generated domains. The clarification comparison also changes training-set size.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated verification limits:** Reference and do-nothing checks cover little of the possible behavior. Generated tests can miss wrong solutions, reject valid alternatives, or share errors with the reference solution. Task review uses the proposer’s own model (§3.2; Appendix A.1; G.4).
- **Author-stated scope limits:** Longer-run improvement is unestablished, Terminal-Bench 4.0 performance remains low, and cross-domain results concern generation rather than training gains (Appendix A.1).
- **Contamination limits:** Benchmark-page blocking and description-level overlap checks do not rule out paraphrased copies or other contamination (Appendix F.2).
- **Analyst inference—resource attribution:** Comparisons match solver updates rather than total compute or elapsed time. Faster rollout collection alone therefore does not establish lower end-to-end training cost (Appendix G.1).
- **Analyst inference—autonomy boundary:** Shared-policy improvement depends on externally generated cold-start trajectories and fixed human-defined admission rules; it demonstrates a bounded self-improvement loop (§4.3; Appendix H).
- **Analyst inference—comparison confounds:** HiL-Bench task quantity differs between training conditions. Cross-domain model comparisons also differ in harnesses, task choices, and budgets (§4.5; Appendix C.2).

## Open research questions
1. **Does improvement persist over longer runs?** The authors explicitly leave this unresolved. Extend shared-policy training across multiple seeds and track held-out performance, environment diversity, and validity (Appendix A.1).
2. **How reliable are accepted verifiers?** Admission does not establish correctness. Audit sampled tasks independently using incorrect, partial, shortcut, and alternative valid solutions (Appendices A.1–A.2).
3. **Does adaptive generation help under equal total compute?** Current comparisons match solver updates. Compare adaptive and static pools with generation, calibration, proposer training, and solver training included in one compute budget (Appendix G.1).
4. **Do generated tasks improve other agentic domains?** Cross-domain training remains untested. Train matched agents on generated versus static domain pools and evaluate held-out task completion (Appendix C; A.2).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied narrative supports the main findings, but many referenced tables contain captions without their numerical bodies. Material gaps include complete per-model generation costs and sample counts, reported confidence-interval values, full sandbox and training configuration tables, total end-to-end compute costs, independent task-quality audits, and verified contents of linked code or model artifacts.
