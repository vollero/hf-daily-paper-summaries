# MixLID: Token-Level Language Identification in Mixed-Language Text

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.16601) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16601)


## One-sentence takeaway
MixLID combines monolingual supervision with code-switched and loanword-substituted training data to identify token languages across 111 language–script combinations, outperforming the tested baselines on the evaluated mixed-language subsets (Sections 2–3).

## Problem and motivation
A single language label cannot identify language changes within a sentence or document. The authors target the gap between broad language coverage and accurate token-level identification, where annotated code-switching data are scarce. They motivate this capability through multilingual corpus curation and checking the language of generated text; neither downstream application is directly evaluated (Section 1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Two-stage token classifier:** MixLID fine-tunes mmBERT-small with a 114-class head: 111 language–script labels plus number, punctuation, and symbol labels. Both stages use subword-level cross-entropy (Sections 2.2, 3.1).
- **Monolingual-to-mixed training recipe:** Stage 1 uses approximately 123.4M OpenLID-v3 examples, propagating sentence labels to words with labeling heuristics. Stage 2 continues training on approximately 634K examples from concatenated ACS data and augmented Smol/Belebele texts (Sections 2.1, 3.1).
- **Loanword-based augmentation:** Wiktionary borrowing relationships support substitutions between English words and their loanwords. Eligible matches are independently replaced with 70% probability, receiving the target-language label (Appendix B.4).
- **Reusable model and comparative evidence:** The authors report a publicly available [MixLID checkpoint](https://huggingface.co/nlpai-lab/MixLID), alongside sentence-level, token-level, and training-stage evaluations (Abstract; Sections 3–4).

The claimed methodological distinction is this combination of staged training and loanword-based augmentation; token classification and synthetic multilingual training themselves have precedents acknowledged in Appendix A.

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Stronger ACS token identification | Across 11 evaluated pairs, mean accuracy / macro F1: **93.2 / 88.8%** for MixLID versus **71.6 / 53.9%** for Polyglot Tagger, the strongest baseline on these averages | Table 3; Appendix C.2 |
| Stronger CodeMixBench token identification | Across German–English, Spanish–English, and Chinese–English, mean accuracy / macro F1: **94.5 / 87.3%** versus Polyglot Tagger’s **85.6 / 64.1%** | Table 3 |
| Gains across evaluated pairs | MixLID exceeds CLD3, MaskLID, and Polyglot Tagger in both token metrics on every listed pair in both benchmarks | Table 3 |
| Broad sentence-level advantage claimed | Authors report higher accuracy and macro F1 than all eight baselines on FLORES+, WiLI-2018, UDHR, and CommonLID, using each baseline’s shared language–script coverage; numerical table cells are unavailable | Section 3.2; Table 1 caption |
| Advantage on a common language subset | Across 36 languages shared by all models and all four sentence benchmarks, mean F1 is **97.38%**, **0.99 percentage points** above GlotLID-v3; MixLID ranks highest on 26 languages | Section 4.1; Figure 1 discussion |
| Stages appear complementary | Sequential training beats either stage alone on ACS and CodeMixBench; it also exceeds monolingual-only training on sentence benchmarks except WiLI-2018. Exact scores are unavailable | Section 4.2; Table 4 caption |

Token scores are computed per example, averaged within language pairs, then averaged equally across pairs. Evaluation uses the same eligible tokens and shared pair coverage for all compared models (Appendix C.3).

These results support superiority on the reported eligible-token settings. They do not establish performance across all supported language combinations, ambiguous tokens, or downstream applications. Sentence-level comparisons use different language intersections for different baselines, so those comparisons do not share one universal evaluation population.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope limits:** Coverage is restricted to 111 labels, and mixed-language evaluation covers relatively few combinations (Limitations).
- **Author-stated synthetic-data risk:** Dictionary substitutions and automatic labels may introduce unnatural expressions or labeling errors. Augmented examples do not represent any particular community’s language practices (Limitations; Ethical Considerations).
- **Concrete augmentation constraints:** No contextual sense disambiguation or inflected-form inference is performed; unchanged examples are retained (Appendix B.4).
- **Analyst inference—component attribution:** The supplied stage comparison does not isolate loanword substitution from ACS concatenation, so their individual contributions remain uncertain (Section 4.2).
- **Analyst inference—evaluation validity:** ACS uses automatic reference labels, and evaluation excludes mixed or ambiguous reference tokens. Reported scores therefore do not resolve performance on linguistically disputed cases (Appendices C.2–C.3).
- **Analyst inference—reproducibility:** Training uses ACS Corpus data while evaluation uses the ACS Benchmark test split, but the supplied text does not explicitly document their overlap controls. This is an unresolved detail, not evidence of leakage.

## Open research questions
- **Which mixed-language component drives the improvement?** Stage-level comparisons do not separate augmentation sources. Train matched variants with ACS only, substitutions only, and both.
- **Does performance transfer to more non-English combinations?** The listed evaluation is predominantly English-paired. Test held-out non-English pairs with human-annotated token labels.
- **How should contextual loanwords and ambiguous tokens be labeled?** Substitution lacks sense disambiguation, while ambiguous references are excluded. Evaluate a human-adjudicated challenge set that explicitly records ambiguity.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The method and token-level results are clearly described, but the supplied retrieval omits the contents of Tables 1, 2, 4, 5, and 6. Missing material includes exact sentence-level and ablation scores, detailed data composition, the full language inventory, and training hyperparameters. Explicit train–test overlap controls, uncertainty estimates, and separate augmentation-component ablations are also unavailable.
