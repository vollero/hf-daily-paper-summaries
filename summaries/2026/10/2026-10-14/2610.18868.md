# Tev: Scaling Long-Horizon Agentic Environments with Dense Progress Rewards

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.18868) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18868)


## One-sentence takeaway
Tev combines procedurally generated tasks with verifiable progress rewards to improve long-horizon terminal agents, raising Tev-27B’s ALE-CM best-of-five solve rate from 0.17 to 0.28 while retaining substantial gaps on harder benchmarks (Table 2; §4.2).

## Problem and motivation
The authors identify two coupled bottlenecks: long-horizon training environments are difficult to construct and verify, and terminal-only rewards provide little learning signal when agents rarely finish. In group-relative RL, groups of entirely unsuccessful rollouts receive identical rewards and contribute zero advantage (§§1, 3.1).

Their proposed gap is between large, comparatively short-horizon training corpora and small, expert-authored long-horizon evaluation sets. This is the authors’ characterization of prior work, rather than an independently verified literature conclusion (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Latent-world-first synthesis:** Construct a known-correct hidden world, compile its workflow into a dependency DAG, render files, and apply declared invertible corruptions. Prompts, verification, and progress scoring derive from the same world to reduce inconsistencies between artifacts (§2.1).
- **Tev-Gym:** A collection of 1,170 verified environments across seven computing-and-math domains, with seeded generation, adjustable difficulty, and qualification checks for correctness and reward exploitation (§§1–2).
- **Progress-potential reward shaping (PPRS):** Score prerequisite-gated intermediate workspace states with a potential \(\Phi\). Reward potential changes per turn plus successful submission; the total is \(1[\mathrm{pass}]+0.25\Phi(s_T)\). Training uses return-to-go and a group baseline at each turn index (§3; Appendix C.1).
- **Trained agents:** Apply the recipe to obtain Tev-9B, Tev-27B, and Tev-320B, evaluating transfer across terminal benchmarks (§4).

The contribution is the combination of world construction, intermediate verification, and training—not backward construction or potential-based shaping alone (§5). PPRS deliberately changes the objective rather than preserving classical shaping’s policy invariance (Appendix B).

## Evidence and main results

All results below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Tev-Gym is harder and longer under the fixed corpus evaluator | Gemini 3.6 Flash: median rollout time 12.4 minutes and P@1 29%, versus SWE-Smith’s 2.0 minutes and 47%. | Table 1 |
| Tev-27B improves ALE-CM completion | On 18 tasks with five rollouts each, Qwen3.8-27B → Tev-27B: P@1 0.12 → 0.16; P@5 0.17 → 0.28; S@1 0.33 → 0.40. Both use Vanillux2. | Table 2; Appendix C.2 |
| Frontier parity is metric-specific | ALE-CM P@5 is 0.28 for Tev-27B, GPT-6 Astra, and Claude Opus 5; their P@1 scores are 0.16, 0.28, and 0.28, respectively. Frontier agents use native harnesses. | Table 2 |
| Gains transfer to harder terminal subsets | Qwen3.8-27B → Tev-27B P@1: 0.09 → 0.14 on TB 3.0-CM, and 0.04 → 0.10 on TB 4.0-CM; subsets contain 22 and 18 tasks, with five trials per task. | Table 3; Appendix C.2 |
| The recipe also benefits a larger base | GLM-5.3-Flash → Tev-320B P@1: 0.20 → 0.27 on TB 3.0-CM and 0.29 → 0.37 on TB 4.0-CM; P@5 on the latter remains 0.56. | Table 3 |
| Mid-horizon performance improves | On TB 2.1’s 89 tasks, with five trials and Terminus-2 for both models, Qwen3.8-27B → Tev-27B P@1 rises 0.764 → 0.794. | Table 4; Appendix C.2 |
| Reward ablation supports PPRS | In the 9B ALE-CM experiment, PPRS versus terminal-only training: S@1 0.29 versus 0.25, P@5 0.11 versus 0.06, and mean training cost 9.0 versus 16.7 H100 GPU-hours per step. | Table 5 |
| Progress can distinguish unsuccessful rollouts | Across 24 groups covering two checkpoints and 12 warehouse seeds, all 192 rollouts receive zero terminal reward, but every group has nonzero potential variance. | Appendix B |

These results support improved completion and more informative rewards in the tested settings. They do not establish general frontier parity, transfer beyond terminal workflows, or robust gains across independent training seeds. The warehouse probe demonstrates reward variance within one environment family, not across the entire corpus.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** The study focuses on synthetic terminal environments; new workflows require additional component authoring and validation, and leading frontier agents remain stronger on the hardest benchmarks (§6).
- **Author-stated objective trade-off:** PPRS can change the ranking of stochastic policies by rewarding partial progress. Its weight is fixed rather than swept (Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference—evaluation scope:** Evaluation uses small domain subsets selected partly through Claude judgments and runtime compatibility. Subset/full-benchmark correlations support comparative relevance but do not establish full-benchmark transfer (Appendix C.2).
- **Analyst inference—comparison controls:** Native frontier harnesses differ from Vanillux2, so frontier comparisons measure complete agent configurations rather than isolated model capability (Appendix C.2).
- **Analyst inference—causal attribution:** Detailed ablations are limited to the smaller model; model size and training-corpus scale also change together. Their separate contributions are therefore unresolved (§§4.1, 4.3).
- **Analyst inference—uncertainty:** The supplied results provide no confidence intervals or independent training-seed variation. Lexical decontamination checks do not alone exclude structural similarity to targeted benchmark workflows (§4.1; Table 11).

## Open research questions

1. **Does the method transfer beyond synthetic terminal tasks?** Current training scope leaves this unresolved (§6). A next experiment could train on additional latent-world workflows and evaluate on held-out real or interactive environments.
2. **Which reward components matter at larger scales?** Detailed reward ablations use the smaller model (§4.3). Repeat them at larger scales under matched compute and report both submission success and partial progress.
3. **How sensitive is success to the shaping weight?** The paper fixes the weight despite acknowledging possible stochastic-policy preference changes (Appendix B). Sweep it and compare terminal pass rates, final potentials, and submission behavior.
4. **Does transfer survive stronger structural separation?** Training targets ALE-related workflows, while reported overlap checks emphasize lexical similarity (§4.1). Hold out entire workflow families or domains during generator construction and evaluate transfer.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the method and reported comparisons, but the source was truncated; this analysis does not cover the complete paper.

Material missing information includes much of Appendix A’s generator, qualification, and structural decontamination detail; confirmed asset release locations and licenses; statistical uncertainty; independent training replications; and full-benchmark results for the trained models.
