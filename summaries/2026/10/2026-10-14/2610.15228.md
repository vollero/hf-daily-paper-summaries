# Words to World: Benchmarking LLMs in Interactive Physical Environments

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.15228) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15228)


## One-sentence takeaway
W2W-Bench evaluates complete model–harness systems across 11 interactive task families, finding task-dependent strengths and persistent failures in spatial precision, timing, coordination, and recovery, although its documented settings largely use simulation or recorded data (§§2–3; Appendix C).

## Problem and motivation
Tool access does not ensure that an agent gathers useful observations, understands environmental changes, or verifies whether actions worked. The authors identify a gap between static or digital evaluations and evaluation of complete model–harness systems across heterogeneous device-grounded tasks (§§1, 4).

This matters because physical interaction requires repeated observation, planning, execution, and correction under spatial, temporal, and resource constraints.

## Approach and contributions
- **Capability framework:** Organize tasks around 3D spatial understanding, temporal understanding, low-level execution, and high-level planning, while acknowledging dependencies between them (§1).
- **Benchmark and evaluation pipeline:** Introduce 11 task families with tool-mediated interaction, recorded trajectories, task-specific outcome metrics, and process diagnostics. An independent evaluator withholds ground truth and scoring rules during episodes (§2; Appendices A–D).
- **Comparative diagnosis:** Evaluate multiple model–harness configurations and analyze view selection, observation timing, dual-arm coordination, and anomaly recovery (§3).

The contribution is principally a benchmark and comparative analysis; the paper does not introduce a new trained model or control algorithm.

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Best overall configuration differs from task leaders | Claude Code × Opus 5 has the lowest reported overall average rank, **4.05**, versus **4.95** for Pi × GPT-6 Astra and **5.14** for Codex × GPT-6 Astra; lower is better. | Table 1 |
| Harness choice changes the same model’s results | GPT-6 Astra obtains **77.91% versus 62.68%** mean reconstruction F-score at **10 mm** with Pi versus Codex, but **10% versus 50%** strict LEGO Building success. These tasks use different metrics. | Table 1; Appendix D |
| Qwen’s strengths do not transfer uniformly across tasks | With Pi, Qwen3.8 Max obtains **80%** object-recognition success versus Astra’s **75%**, but **0%** Functional LEGO success versus Astra’s **80%**. Recognition requires a correct label and an informative acquired view. | Table 2; Appendix D |
| Temporal requirements remain difficult | Among **15** SimuHome tasks containing temporal criteria, Claude Code × Opus 5 satisfies all temporal criteria on **10/15 (66.7%)**, versus **8/15 (53.3%)** for each of Codex × Astra and Codex × Sol. | §3.2.2 |
| Adaptive observation timing beats one fixed-window setting | For Codex × GPT-5.6 Sol, autonomous timing uses **6.40 future hours** with **56.67% Exact P@1**, versus **9.00 hours** and **53.33%** under a fixed contiguous window. | §3.2.2; Table 3 |
| Dual-arm invocation often fails | Across the reported configurations, dual-arm control is invoked in **15/50 cases**; **7/15 invoked cases (46.7%)** fail, including **4/7** failures attributed to insufficient coordination. | Table 4 |

These results establish configuration-dependent performance on the sampled benchmark cases. They do not establish a universally superior harness, isolate causal mechanisms, or demonstrate reliable transfer to deployed physical hardware. Average rank also combines tasks with different scoring definitions.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-reported capability failures:** The authors describe missed spatial misalignments, exploration that does not adapt adequately to occlusion, unmet scheduled outcomes, coordination errors, and incomplete anomaly recovery (§3.2).
- **Analyst inference—physical deployment validity:** Despite the abstract’s real-world framing, Appendix C documents simulated scenes, robots, homes, and laboratories, alongside recorded documents and clinical data. The supplied text does not clearly identify hardware-based evaluation results.
- **Analyst inference—comparison uncertainty:** Evaluation uses small case sets (§2.3; Appendix C), while main-result uncertainty intervals and repeated-run variability are unavailable. Model coverage differs across harnesses, and matched seeds, prompts, budgets, and configuration controls are not fully specified.
- **Reporting inconsistencies:** The autonomous health result is **53.33%** in Table 1 but **56.67%** in Table 3. Table 5’s anomaly-category percentages total **60%** for Codex and **110%** for Claude Code, leaving category coverage or overlap unclear.
- **Aggregation ambiguity:** The text and Appendix D say tabletop games are reported separately, but Table 1 supplies one “H/R/P” value per configuration without clearly explaining its aggregation.
- **Limited mechanistic evidence:** The timing ablation tests one model–harness configuration against one fixed duration. The failure analyses do not isolate whether errors originate in perception, model reasoning, tool design, or harness behavior (§3.2).

## Open research questions
- **Do the findings transfer to hardware?** Documented evaluations largely use simulation or recorded data. A next experiment could repeat matched scanning and LEGO tasks on physical devices and compare failures with simulation.
- **Which harness features cause performance differences?** Configuration rankings show associations without component-level isolation. A next experiment could hold the model, seeds, tools, and budgets fixed while varying memory, scheduling, or verification behavior.
- **Does adaptive timing generalize?** The fixed-window comparison covers one configuration. A next experiment could test several models and multiple fixed durations under equal observation budgets.
- **Can explicit state verification improve recovery?** Reported failures include stale coordinates and mistaken recovery judgments. A next experiment could require fresh observations and state reconciliation after detected disturbances, measuring strict recovery success and added interaction cost.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the benchmark structure and reported comparisons, but inconsistencies weaken some quantitative interpretations.

Material missing information includes public code/data availability, complete prompts and interaction budgets, matched-case controls, repeated-run uncertainty, several scoring thresholds, separate tabletop results, reconciled health and anomaly tables, and clearly identified physical-hardware experiments.
