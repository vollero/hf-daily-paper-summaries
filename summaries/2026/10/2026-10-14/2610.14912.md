# Probable but Not Valid: A Psychometric Audit of Decision Models as Synthetic Survey Respondents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.14912) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14912)


## One-sentence takeaway
On one Lithuanian survey benchmark, decision-model probabilities approximate population response distributions better than rounded answers but fail to beat population frequencies at predicting individuals, while newer generative releases show no reliable improvement over predecessors (§§5.5, 5.9–5.10).

## Problem and motivation
Synthetic survey responses can appear plausible while distorting variance, correlations, reliability and relationships between constructs. The authors ask whether decision models—which directly return probabilities over rating categories—or newer generative LLMs overcome these problems. Their identified gap is evaluation beyond group averages: full psychometric structure, properly scored individual forecasts and comparisons across model releases (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Comparative audit:** Evaluate 14 decision models and 47 generative LLMs using the same 100 real profiles and 68 survey items, against a reference of 263 Lithuanian employees. The benchmark is inherited from an earlier audit (§§3–4).
- **Distribution and structure analysis:** Compare rounded expectations, most-probable categories and independent sampling; examine correlations, factor structure, response styles, profile removal, repeated requests and demographic swaps (§§4–5).
- **Individual forecasting:** Score probability vectors using ranked probability score (RPS) and log score against population frequencies and a profile regression trained on the other 163 respondents; test cross-fitted recalibration (§5.9).
- **Evaluation corrections and reference checks:** Correct reverse-item alignment and omitted baseline distribution scores, compare successive releases within product lines, and use a second human sample to expose limitations of the composite Psychometric Similarity Score (PSS) (§5.1; §5.10; Appendix B).

These results support the authors’ distinction between matching population marginals, preserving psychometric structure and forecasting individuals. They do not establish general human equivalence or isolate output format as the cause of class differences. Population-based forecast references also have access to same-survey human responses unavailable to the language models (§§4, 7).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope:** One language, country, non-probability employee sample and self-report instrument set; the change-engagement scale is less validated than the other instruments (§§3, 6–7).
- **Confounded interface comparison:** Model classes differ in prompting language, API route, sampling and answer dependence. Even the two GPT-6 Luna endpoints cannot be verified to share weights (§§4, 5.3).
- **Statistical uncertainty:** Small samples make mediation and demographic components unstable; selecting the best among many models inflates its apparent performance. Many added analyses were exploratory and unregistered (§§4, 7).
- **Version comparisons:** Older models were not queried again; some comparisons change model tier or provider, and all generative evaluations use minimal reasoning (§§5.10, 7).
- **Reproducibility and validity gaps:** Memorisation and measurement invariance were not tested. Code and raw outputs are available on request, rather than through a supplied public repository (§7; Data and code availability).
- **Analyst inference:** Composite-score gains can obscure failures important to a particular survey use; the readout and reference-sample results demonstrate this risk (§§5.1–5.4).

## Open research questions
- **Can richer personal information improve individual forecasts?** Coarse profiles provide little predictive benefit. Add anchor-item answers or interviews and test held-out RPS against population frequencies (§7).
- **Does the interface cause weak factor structure?** Current class comparisons are confounded. Hold model, language and routing fixed while comparing separate-item, sequential and joint-item elicitation (§7).
- **How much human calibration data is needed?** Shrinkage removes most forecast loss without demonstrating positive decision-model skill. Vary calibration sample size and compare against a copula trained on the same data (§§5.9, 7).
- **Do the findings generalise?** Replicate on larger, multilingual probability samples, measuring both psychometric structure and separation from independent human samples (§7).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported benchmark findings; generalisation remains limited.
