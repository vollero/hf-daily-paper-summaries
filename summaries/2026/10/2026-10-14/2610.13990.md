# DoSA: Dots Sparse Attention

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.13990) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13990)


## One-sentence takeaway
DoSA uses certified score bounds to skip full sparse-attention indexer scoring while preserving exact Top-K under stated numerical and storage conditions, reporting up to 1.94× lower time to first token relative to SGLang (§§2, 4.3; Appendix C).

## Problem and motivation
Sparse attention reduces the keys attended to, but its indexer can still score every key using every head. At approximately 1M tokens, indexing accounts for 73.0% of GLM-5.2 prefill GPU time and 90.8% for DeepSeek-V3.2 under the authors’ SGLang profiling setup (§1, Figure 1; Appendix D.1).

The authors identify a gap between systems that reduce score writeback or selection overhead while retaining full scoring, and approximate screening that can exclude true winners. Their objective is to avoid unnecessary full-head scoring with a certificate that preserves the original selection rule (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Signed-score certificate:** Exact scores from a seed subset establish a lower bound on the Top-K threshold. Selected heads are evaluated directly; nonnegative projections and residual bounds cover omitted positive heads, while an activation-informed aggregate bounds omitted negative contributions. Keys whose upper bounds fall below the threshold are rejected (§§2.2–2.5).
- **Correctness and efficiency analysis:** The authors prove exact selection under valid bounds, original-score refinement, and lossless candidate handling. They relate pruning effectiveness to certificate slack, seed quality, selection margins, and retained-head cost (Appendices A–C).
- **GPU implementation:** Query grouping, shared key loads, survivor compaction, and filtered score writeback translate rejection into reduced computation and traffic. Decoding retains full FP8 scoring and accelerates selection using recent selected keys as threshold witnesses (§3).

The general threshold-pruning principle is established prior work; the claimed novelty is its certificate and execution strategy for the signed ReLU indexer (§5).

## Evidence and main results

All measurements below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Faster complete Indexer across the microbenchmark grid | GLM-5.2, single GPU, English/Chinese/code, 128K–approximately 1M contexts, 4K–32K query chunks: fastest in all 60 configurations; 1.12–2.74× over SGLang and 1.17–1.69× over LiteTopK; includes query/key preparation. | §4.2, Figure 4 |
| Substantial end-to-end gains | At approximately 1M tokens with TP8: GLM-5.2 TTFT speedups of 1.86× over SGLang and 1.26× over LiteTopK; DeepSeek-V3.2 achieves 1.94× and 1.50×, respectively. Prefill chunks are 32K and 16K, respectively. | §§4.1, 4.3, Figure 8 |
| Most screened pairs are rejected | GLM-5.2 certificate analysis: domain-average pruning of 97.2%–97.5%; above 95% in 20 of 21 layers in each domain. | §4.2; Figure 6 caption, although prose points to Figure 7 |
| Screening and sparse writeback provide complementary savings | LOFT SciFact example, 16K queries/896K keys, averaged across 21 layers: latency reductions versus Base are 37.4% for screening, 23.4% for sparse writeback, and 47.3% combined, equivalent to 1.90×. | §4.4, Figure 7 |
| Negative aggregation is critical in the tested workload | GLM-5.2, final 4K queries per input/layer: removing it increases mean survivor rate from 2.65% to 95.06% and geometric-mean Indexer time to 9.51× DoSA, including recovery. | Appendix D.2, Figure 9 |
| Decoding benefits are smaller | With approximately 1M total cached tokens, decoding Indexer speedups over SGLang are 1.06×, 1.11×, and 1.26× at request lengths of 256K, 512K, and approximately 1M. Gains come from selection rather than skipped full scoring. | §§3, 4.2; Figure 5 caption |
| Exactness is conditional | Same Top-K IDs require valid numerical upper bounds, original-score refinement, no lost candidates, and a common total tie order; otherwise the guarantee permits a valid tied Top-K set. | Appendix A.2, Theorem 1; Appendix C |

The evidence supports computational savings in the evaluated settings. It does not establish improved task accuracy, identical generated text under different tied selections, or reliable reasoning beyond trained context lengths (§6; Appendix D.1).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ limitations:** Every key is still scanned. Short contexts, weak seeds, or loose bounds can make screening overhead outweigh savings (§6; Appendix B.3).
- **Conditional numerical guarantee:** Real-arithmetic proofs alone do not establish FP8 correctness. Conservative error allowances, upward rounding, valid key summaries, and full-domain recovery are required; overflow candidates cannot be dropped (Appendix C).
- **Evaluation scope:** Main microbenchmarks use GLM-5.2, while end-to-end evaluation covers two models. DeepSeek measurements beyond its declared context establish computational scaling, not task quality (§4; Appendix D.1).
- **Reproducibility concern:** The layer-wise retrieval matrix mixes original measurements with five remeasured cells after a capacity repair. That repair adds 9.94 GiB of workspace at approximately 1M-key capacity; the full matrix was not rerun (Appendix D.4).
- **Analyst inference:** Evidence for positive-head projection’s practical contribution is limited in this workload: it is needed in only 0.42% of evaluated query-layer rows, and removing it has a small aggregate effect (Appendix D.2).

## Open research questions
1. **Can block certificates avoid reading individual keys?** DoSA still scans every key, and block bounds remain future work (§6). Test compact block summaries against per-key screening, measuring key traffic, latency, and exactness.
2. **How should head budgets and seeds adapt to difficult queries?** The analysis provides a logical-work tradeoff but excludes planning, padding, and recovery costs (Appendix B.3). Compare adaptive policies with current choices using complete-Indexer latency and survivor counts.
3. **How robust are the gains under heavy candidate overflow or frequent numerical recovery?** Correct recovery is specified, but its frequency across workloads is not reported (Appendix C.3). Stress-test tied scores and loose certificates while measuring memory, recovery frequency, and Top-K agreement.
4. **Can training improve certificate tightness without harming task quality?** Slack regularization is proposed but not evaluated (§6). Train matched indexers with and without it, comparing accuracy, pruning, and total inference cost.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text provides detailed methods, proofs, numerical conditions, and experimental summaries. Material gaps include exact GPU models, absolute latency tables, uncertainty estimates for headline speedups, complete seed/planner configuration, broad empirical Top-K agreement tests, and confirmed public implementation availability. Some figure references are inconsistent, and the capacity-repaired layer matrix was only partially remeasured.
