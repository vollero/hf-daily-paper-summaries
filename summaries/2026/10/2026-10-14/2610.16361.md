# HexaBee-4B: Six-Language Translation with Multilingual Answering and Bidirectional Supervision

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.16361) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16361)


## One-sentence takeaway

HexaBee-4B’s three-stage adaptation improves direction-macro chrF++ over Gemma 3 4B IT and 12B IT on a synthetic six-language test set, but prompt differences and missing ablations prevent attribution to its shared-answer supervision design (§§5–7).

## Problem and motivation

Translation requires preserving source meaning while following a requested target language. The authors argue that ordinary multilingual answering need not teach transfer between languages when questions and answers always share a language. They investigate how cross-language answering and translations of the same answer texts can support a compact translator (§1).

The gap concerns the organization of supervision: how to connect language-controlled answering with translation, rather than simply combining monolingual instruction data (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Three-stage adaptation:** Continue pretraining Gemma 3 4B IT on Wikipedia, continue the same LoRA adapter with joint answering/translation supervision, merge it, then train and merge a fresh translation adapter (§4; Figure 1).
- **Shared-answer supervision:** A question and an answer text in one language can both map to the same target-language answer. Both tasks use response-token negative log-likelihood; there is no separate alignment loss (§3).
- **Constructed training mixture:** Phase 2 contains 19,458,332 answering records—including 5,759,806 cross-language records—and 10,695,072 answer-text translations. Phase 3 uses 658,274 forward/reverse examples derived from synthetic aligned pairs (§§4.2–4.3).
- **System and evaluation assets:** The paper links code, model checkpoints, adapters, datasets, prompts, stored outputs, and evaluation artifacts, and reports all translation directions among Arabic, Chinese, English, Hebrew, Persian, and Spanish (§§1, 4–5; Appendix F).

The claimed contribution is this particular combination and its evaluation; the paper acknowledges precedents for its component methods (§2).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Higher overall chrF++ | On 36,572 synthetic test examples across 30 directions, direction-macro chrF++ is **73.75**, versus **57.11** for Gemma 3 4B IT and **66.83** for 12B IT; each uses its archived prompt. | §6.1; Table 2 |
| Other aggregate metrics agree | Same full-test direction-macro setting: HexaBee versus 4B IT achieves BLEU **62.03 vs 40.70**, TER **30.05 vs 51.40** (lower is better), and COMET ×100 **89.86 vs 85.88**. Authors report the best aggregate score on all five metrics. | §6.1; Table 2 |
| Broad directional gains, with a metric exception | chrF++ improves over both baselines in every evaluated direction. Chinese→Hebrew TER is worse: **90.09**, versus **88.40** for 4B IT and **78.13** for 12B IT. | §6.2; Figure 10 |
| Model judging supports the ranking | On 300 matched items, reference-assisted holistic means are **92.92 / 80.32 / 91.73**, and reference-free means **94.10 / 81.43 / 91.97**, for HexaBee / 4B IT / 12B IT respectively. Scores are on a model-defined 0–100 scale. | §§5.3, 6.3; Table 2 |

These results support better performance for the **recorded complete system on this synthetic distribution**. They do not establish human-assessed translation quality, out-of-domain generalization, or which training component caused the gains. Judge scores and scaled COMET values are not accuracy percentages. Numerical confidence intervals referenced in Table 3 are absent from the supplied text.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ stated evaluation limits:** Sources and references are synthetic; the judge comes from the same teacher-service family. Teacher-like wording may be favored. No human-authored benchmark or human assessment is available (Limitations).
- **Authors’ stated comparison limits:** Inference prompts differ. The 4B baseline has 31 empty outputs, with context-limit failures appearing in logs. No controlled component ablation or intermediate-checkpoint evaluation separates training effects from prompting and failure handling (§7; Appendix B.4).
- **Authors’ stated data risks:** Coverage is uneven; exports retain empty fields and 1,961 conflicting answer-language directives. Generated answer equivalence lacks exhaustive human verification (Appendices B.1, C).
- **Authors’ stated validity limits:** Record splitting permits topic overlap; two whitespace-normalized test sources match training sources. Semantic duplication and pretraining exposure remain unresolved. COMET truncates long texts, and judge sampling is narrow (Limitations; Appendices D.2, F).
- **Analyst inference:** Translation gains do not establish improved multilingual answering, because held-out QA evaluation is unavailable (Limitations).

## Open research questions

1. **Does shared-answer QA add value beyond translation supervision?** Attribution remains open without mixture ablations (§7). Compare QA-plus-MT and MT-only intermediate training while holding training budget, final corpus, and inference prompts fixed.
2. **Do gains transfer to human-authored text?** Evaluation is entirely synthetic (§5.1). Test identical prompts on a human-authored multilingual benchmark and collect blinded bilingual assessments.
3. **How much does prompting affect the comparison?** Archived prompts differ (§5.1). Run a crossed model-by-prompt evaluation with matched context limits and report translation quality alongside generation failures.
4. **Does the intermediate stage improve answering itself?** No held-out QA results are provided (Limitations). Evaluate same-language and cross-language QA across intermediate and final checkpoints, including target-language adherence.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied narrative supports the reported system-level results and their caveats, but many tables appear only as captions.

Material unavailable information includes numerical paired confidence intervals, complete directional/rubric tables, Table 10’s executed training settings, and Table 25’s truncation counts. The paper also lacks component ablations, human evaluation, repeated-run variability, and held-out QA results; the synthetic split seed and immutable translation-service revision are unrecoverable (Appendix B).
