# Beyond Prompt Count: How Data Shapes Transfer in On-Policy Distillation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.37377) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37377)


## One-sentence takeaway
In the studied on-policy distillation settings, very few prompts can support strong transfer, but their effectiveness depends on the teacher–student pair and target task, while targeted selection offers no consistent advantage over random sampling.

## Problem and motivation
On-policy distillation (OPD) trains students using teacher feedback on student-generated responses. Because the same prompt produces changing trajectories during training, prompt count alone cannot characterize the supervision received. The authors investigate a gap between **prompt sufficiency** and **prompt interchangeability**: how quantity, source, teacher choice, and selection affect transfer, including harmful outcomes and recovery (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
The study uses a fixed reverse-KL OPD procedure with sampled-token teacher feedback; task rewards do not enter the training loss (§2.1; Appendix B.2). Its contributions are empirical:

- **Data requirements and preferences:** prompt-count sweeps, fixed-count source comparisons, and teacher swaps holding student initialization and prompt supports fixed (§§3–4).
- **Transfer diagnostics and recovery:** parameter and prediction alignment, generation analysis, hidden-state replacement, and continued training after unfavorable transfer (§§5.1–5.3).
- **Selection assessment:** comparisons of random sampling against difficulty, length, diversity, disagreement, and gradient-based selectors, plus mixed-source filtering and screening costs (§5.4).

The evidence supports pair- and task-dependent prompt utility and recoverability in the tested settings. It does not establish universal few-prompt sufficiency, compute savings from smaller supports, or a causal reasoning mechanism. Functional alignment measures prediction changes on frozen prefixes rather than benchmark improvement.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-qualified budget limits:** fixed prompt counts do not fix tokens or compute. Code recovery uses additional updates; Math continuation matches updates but not token budgets (§2.2; Appendix E.5).
- **Teacher-swap confounding:** Math-RL and Code-RL differ in training history as well as RL data, so their comparison does not isolate training-domain effects (Appendix B.1).
- **Restricted diagnostics:** functional measurements use initial-student prefixes and conditional top-token distributions; aggregate weighting favors longer trajectories. Hidden-state intervention uses a small probe and requires an evaluation-time donor (Appendices E.1–E.5).
- **Analyst inference—uncertainty and generalization:** support-draw replication with a fixed training seed cannot establish full training variability. Results within the studied model families do not establish generality beyond them (§2.3; Appendices E.4, F.1).
- **Mixture confounds:** Mix3-to-Mix5 changes prompt membership alongside domain allocation; the four-prompt control changes repetition/exposure (Appendices B.3, F.2).

## Open research questions

- **Can adaptive selection reliably beat random sampling?** Static selectors show inconsistent gains. Test selection that tracks the evolving student, with screening included in matched total compute budgets (Appendix H).
- **What determines recovery and its limits?** Support switching works in selected settings, but exposure and switching time remain unresolved. Sweep switch times and source ratios under matched token budgets (Appendix H).
- **When does trajectory reuse preserve transfer?** Preliminary replay results vary by model pair. Compare adaptive replay schedules against fresh-only OPD at matched wall-clock and teacher-scoring budgets (Appendix G).
