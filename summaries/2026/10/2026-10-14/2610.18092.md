# LifeSearch: Benchmarking Deep Research Agents for Real-World Life and Productivity

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.18092) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18092)


## One-sentence takeaway
LifeSearch evaluates autonomous web-research reports on expert-refined Chinese and English life/work tasks, finding that the strongest tested agent satisfies 63.44% of weighted rubric requirements under a shared harness (Table 4).

## Problem and motivation
The authors argue that existing deep-research benchmarks emphasize academic or synthetic tasks and insufficiently test practical life and professional information needs. Useful reports must satisfy detailed source, numerical, temporal, and coverage requirements—not merely provide plausible summaries (§§1–2). Their claimed gap is a bilingual, expert-annotated benchmark targeting these practical deliverables; the asserted “first” status is an author claim rather than independently established here.

## Approach and contributions
- **Benchmark construction:** LifeSearch contains 203 tasks—102 Chinese and 101 English—across seven domains, with 4,014 expert-written rubrics. Crowd-worker query seeds undergo filtering, operational-requirement removal, expert refinement, and manual selection (§§3.1–3.3).
- **Grounded evaluation:** Self-contained Pass/Fail rubrics specify factual targets, sources, dates, units, and tolerances where relevant. Annotator weights determine within-task satisfaction scores, which are averaged equally across tasks (§§3.3–3.4).
- **Empirical validation and comparison:** The study selects an LLM judge against human labels, compares 12 agents in a shared ReAct-style harness, and checks aggregate rankings against human report rankings (§§3.5, 4).

The contribution is a benchmark and evaluation study; the paper does not introduce a new research-agent architecture or training method.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Leading agents leave substantial requirements unmet | Across all 203 tasks, GPT-5.6 Sol scores **63.44 ± 0.27%**, versus Claude Opus 5 at **56.10 ± 0.28%**; mean ± sample SD across three independent runs. Scores measure weighted rubric satisfaction. | Table 4; §§3.4, 4.1 |
| Overall leadership does not imply consistent task completion | GPT-5.6 Sol’s three-run mean falls below **50%** on **41/203 tasks (20.2%)**; no evaluated system exceeds **70%** in any domain. | §4.2 |
| Selected judge agrees imperfectly with human labels | Gemini 3.1 Pro, low reasoning and temperature zero, achieves **84.25% mean accuracy** and **98.8% self-consistency** across three grading runs on **402 labels from 20 reports**. Human mean pairwise Cohen’s κ is **0.715** before disagreement resolution. | §3.5; Table 3 |
| Aggregate rankings align with human rankings on a subset | For **seven systems and 210 reports across 30 tasks**, ranking correlations are **Spearman’s ρ=0.89** and **Kendall’s τ=0.81**; the top four systems have identical ordering. | §4.5; Table 5 |
| Similar generation volume can yield different scores | Claude Opus 5 generates **48.9k** output tokens per task and scores **56.10%**; GLM-5.3 generates **49.3k** and scores **49.19%**. Tokens cover the entire execution. | Table 4; §4.2 |
| Effort and report length correlate with scores | Across **12 systems**, iteration count correlates with score at **Spearman’s ρ=0.76**, and final-report character length at **ρ=0.60**. | §4.3 |

These results establish comparative performance within the reported harness and support—but do not fully validate—the grading protocol. Scores are requirement-satisfaction measures, not probabilities of successful real-world decisions. Cross-model correlations do not establish that more searching or longer reports cause better performance.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-noted boundaries:** Domain comparisons are descriptive, particularly for the nine-task everyday-facts subset. Iterations include actions beyond search, preventing causal interpretation of search effort (§§4.2–4.3).
- **Harness dependence:** Clarification requests receive no replies and can terminate tasks (§4.4). **Analyst inference:** rankings may differ in interactive use or under other tools and budgets.
- **Limited judge validation:** Judge selection uses one response per task on a small task subset; human ranking validation covers only seven systems (§§3.5, 4.5). **Analyst inference:** agreement may not generalize uniformly across models, languages, or rubric types.
- **Construction effects:** Experts enrich seeds with capability requirements, and quality control tightens overly easy tasks (§§3.2–3.3). **Analyst inference:** difficulty and realism reflect these editorial choices, rather than an untouched distribution of user requests.
- **Incomplete reproducibility evidence:** The supplied text references prompt templates, but their actual bodies are absent. It claims open-sourcing without providing a repository or artifact link.

## Open research questions
- **Does additional search causally improve scores?** Cross-model effort correlations cannot answer this (§4.3). A next experiment could vary search budgets within the same model while holding tools and prompts fixed.
- **How robust are rankings to grading choices?** Human agreement covers limited subsets (§§3.5, 4.5). Regrade a stratified sample with independent experts, alternative judges, and uniform versus annotated weights.
- **How much does interaction change practical performance?** Clarification is penalized by the autonomous setting (§4.4). Compare autonomous runs with standardized, scripted clarification replies.
- **Can temporal controls reduce cutoff violations?** The paper identifies violations but tests no remedy (§4.4). Compare baseline agents with explicit date checks and historical-source retrieval.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported benchmark design and descriptive results; **medium** in broader real-world generalization.

Material missing information includes the full task/rubric release and numerical weights, actual prompt bodies, exact execution limits and evaluation dates, language-specific results, failure-mode frequencies, and uncertainty estimates for human-ranking correlations.
