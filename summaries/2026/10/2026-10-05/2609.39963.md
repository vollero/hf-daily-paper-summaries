# Diptych: Scoped, AI-Interpreted Comparison for Reference Listening in Music Production

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.39963) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39963)


## One-sentence takeaway
Diptych combines user-selected audio comparison scopes with inspectable features and AI interpretations, helping musicians report additional differences and clearer next steps without increasing aggregate expert support for their existing judgments (§§5.2–5.4).

## Problem and motivation
Reference listening requires musicians to choose comparable passages, remember their differences, and articulate actionable observations. The authors identify a gap in whole-track comparison tools and AI feedback: an accurate description can still be unhelpful when it addresses the wrong passages or ignores the user’s creative goals (§1). Diptych makes comparison scope an explicit user choice.

## Approach and contributions
- **Scoped comparison interface:** Whole-track comparison and independently selected structural segments share feature tables, playback controls, timelines, and AI interpretations. Segments need not match in timestamp, duration, or structural label (§§3.1–3.3).
- **Structured analysis and interpretation:** The pipeline extracts 59 features across eight categories, computes deterministic differences, and supplies those differences plus a feature glossary to an LLM. Figure 2 names Gemma-4-31B-Dense via vLLM; §3.4 describes schema-constrained decoding and low-temperature generation. Chroma-based novelty detection and heuristic labels provide navigation landmarks.
- **Empirical account and design guidance:** A before-and-after musician study with source-blinded expert ratings examines judgment changes, selective acceptance, usability, and unsupported interpretations. The contribution is interaction design and evaluation rather than a newly trained audio model (§1; §§4–6).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Existing judgments gained no aggregate expert support | In 38 matched judgments from 12 musicians across four pairs, full support from both assigned experts changed from 24/38 (63.2%) before use to 23/38 (60.5%) afterward; at least partial support remained 32/38 (84.2%). | §5.2, p. 23 |
| Additional differences were mostly supported, often partially | Seven musicians added 10 judgments after system use: nine received at least partial support from both experts, including four fully supported by both; one was unsupported by both. | §5.2, pp. 23–24 |
| Confidence increases did not consistently track support | Among 35 judgments with complete confidence ratings, confidence increased for 15; seven remained below full support from both experts and one lost full support from one expert. | §5.3, p. 24 |
| Usability and perceived usefulness were favorable | For 12 respondents, mean SUS was 74.79 (SD 9.26; range 62.5–97.5); 11 rated the system somewhat or very helpful, and all 12 reported clearer next steps. | §5.4, pp. 24–26; Figs. 5, 7–8 |
| Verification and autonomy concerns remained | Eight of 12 found evidence sufficient to verify statements; four reported insufficiently supported claims stated too confidently; two reported pressure to match the reference against their judgment. | §5.4, p. 27; Fig. 8 |
| Interpretations sometimes exceeded audible evidence | Experts flagged unheard shuffle feel, key changes, emotional qualities, and vocal descriptions in instrumental audio; participants also reported a false vocal reading and failed rhythm-intensity reading. | §6.3, pp. 28–29 |

These descriptive results support perceived usefulness and the discovery of additional, often partially supported differences. They do not establish improved judgment accuracy, better finished mixes, or an isolated benefit from the LLM. The authors explicitly evaluate exposure to the complete system, and expert support is a perceptual reference rather than absolute ground truth (§§4.1, 7).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Expert ratings depend on interpretation of musical context, claim wording, and evidential sufficiency; lack of unanimous full support does not necessarily mean a judgment is wrong (§7).
- **Author-stated:** Feature accuracy and usefulness vary. Features were selected from literature rather than formative producer research, and their individual contributions were not measured (§7).
- **Analyst inference:** The fixed before-and-after order leaves repeated listening and system exposure entangled. No component ablation separates playback/navigation, features, and AI interpretation (§4.1).
- **Analyst inference:** A single session with 12 musicians, four assigned pairs, and no actual DAW edits limits conclusions about sustained professional use or production outcomes (§§4.1–4.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Technical fluency and confidence may obscure weak evidence, consistent with the reported confidence/support mismatch and unsupported descriptions (§§5.3, 6.3–6.4).

## Open research questions
1. **Which components provide the benefit?** The complete-system study cannot isolate them. A counterbalanced comparison of A/B playback, scoped feature inspection, and added AI interpretation could measure incremental effects on discovery and expert support.
2. **Can evidence-linked wording reduce unsupported claims?** The authors propose sentence-level grounding but do not evaluate it (§6.3). Compare current verdicts with feature-linked, uncertainty-aware outputs, including instrumental tracks.
3. **Which features deserve interface space?** Feature utility was not measured (§7). Combine producer interviews with feature-subset experiments measuring verifiability, task usefulness, and information burden.
4. **Does clearer guidance improve actual production decisions?** Participants proposed next steps without editing their own music (§4.1). A longitudinal DAW study could examine chosen edits, creative intent, and independently assessed outcomes.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported descriptive findings; causal and broader-use conclusions remain limited.

The supplied text does not establish track identities or genre coverage, public code/data availability, exact prompts and decoding settings, complete feature formulas, inter-rater agreement statistics, or analyzed interaction-log results. It references supplementary questionnaire wording that is not included.
