# Multilingual GSM-Symbolic: What determines capability transfer across languages?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.03367) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03367)


## One-sentence takeaway
On matched mathematical word problems across 15 languages, the authors find that larger models and enabled reasoning narrow resource-related performance gaps, while typological gaps remain harder to reduce and individual model failures remain difficult to predict (§4; Table 3).

## Problem and motivation
Multilingual benchmark scores often confound language with problem difficulty, formatting, or cultural knowledge, while static datasets risk contamination and saturation. The authors identify a gap in jointly estimating how model characteristics, language characteristics, and their interactions predict cross-lingual performance. Mathematics provides a setting where underlying operations and answers can remain comparable across languages (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Extensible benchmark:** Starting from GSM-Symbolic, the authors release a multilingual parser, generation code, and 100 templates translated and localized into 15 human-validated languages. Twenty instances per template yield 30,000 question–answer pairs. Native-speaker review, model-assisted error analysis, and automated checks validate the templates (§3.1; Fig. 1).
- **Joint explanatory analysis:** A crossed-effects logistic model relates exact-answer correctness to model size, reasoning, Common Crawl resource level, typological distance from English, relative tokenizer fertility, and interactions, accounting for family, model, language, and template variation (§§3.2–3.4).
- **Performance forecasting:** Leave-one-language-out evaluation tests predictions for otherwise evaluated models; additional target-language templates provide limited-data calibration (§4.2; Appendix F).
- **Reusable evidence:** The authors report releasing versioned data, code, tests, and per-sample response logs, with EuroEval and Inspect AI integrations (Reproducibility Statement, p. 11).

## Evidence and main results

Here, **pp** means percentage points. Continuous-predictor coefficients are log-odds per predictor standard deviation; reasoning compares enabled with disabled.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Size, resources, and reasoning predict higher correctness; distance predicts lower correctness | Equation 1: size β=1.768, resource β=0.773, reasoning β=0.669, distance β=−0.253; all p<0.001. Relative fertility is nonsignificant: β=−0.041, p=0.195. | Table 3; Appendix C |
| Size and reasoning narrow the resource gap | Resource×size β=−0.265 and resource×reasoning β=−0.200, both p<0.001. Distance×size is nonsignificant (β=0.024, p=0.319); the small distance×reasoning effect is not robust to ablations. | Table 3; §4.1.1 |
| The size interaction survives removal of saturation | Restricting to models below 90% English accuracy leaves no evaluated cell above 90%; resource×size remains β=−0.188, p=1.4×10⁻⁵. | Appendix I.3; Table 7 |
| Held-out-language forecasting works better for averages than individual failures | Language-mean MAE=3.17 pp; model-in-language MAE=5.98 pp. With 10 target-language templates, each instantiated 20 times, these become 2.38 and 4.19 pp over three draws per language. | Appendix F; Table 5 |
| Language features explain substantially more language variation than model-specific transfer variation | Against a size-only baseline, authors report 92% reduction in between-language variance versus 23% in model×language variance. | §4.2 |
| Symbolic instances are harder than original items | Across 705 model–language pairs, symbolic exact-answer accuracy averages 3.14 pp lower; the gap is uncorrelated with model accuracy (r=−0.02). | §4.3; Fig. 10 |
| Resource effects grow deeper into solutions | Stage-specific fits excluding reasoning give resource β=0.299 for operand reads, 0.420 for intermediates, and 0.712 for final answers, all p<0.001. | Appendix I.2; Table 6 |
| More templates matter more than more instances | At 100 templates, model–language accuracy has a 2.29 pp standard error; doubling instances from 10 to 20 reduces standard error by only 3%. | Appendices A.4; Table 2 |

The evidence supports predictive associations within this benchmark and model suite. It does not establish that changing training resources or linguistic structure causes the estimated effects. Forecasting holds out languages while retaining model observations from other languages; it does not test unseen models. Stage analysis measures whether gold numbers appear in responses, which provides indirect evidence about reasoning stages.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Only grade-school mathematics, 100 templates, and 15 validated languages are analyzed. Results may differ across domains, base models, or MoE architectures (Limitations).
- **Confounded language features:** Typological distance correlates strongly with raw fertility; using relative fertility leaves the distance coefficient potentially capturing both structure and tokenization. Common Crawl counts imperfectly represent usable, model-specific training data (Limitations).
- **Residual model failures:** Language features explain only a minority of model×language variation, including large forecasting failures for Apertus in English and some models in Estonian (§4.2).
- **Specification sensitivity:** Adding template×language effects improves fit while broadly preserving conclusions. Variance-explained figures differ between the main analysis and Appendix A.1. Saturation-free reasoning interactions cannot be assessed reliably because too few paired models remain (Appendices A.1, I.3).
- **Measurement and contamination:** Unparseable final answers count as incorrect. Stage extraction ignores numbers written as words and counts numeric presence anywhere in a response. Template memorization remains possible despite fresh symbolic instances (§3.5; Appendix I.1; Ethics Statement).
- **Analyst inference—reporting consistency:** Some numerical results conflict across sections: size β=1.88 in §4.1 versus 1.768 in Table 3; symbolic-penalty tests and stage coefficients also differ between main text and appendices. Evaluation counts vary across analyses without a fully reconciled explanation. Detailed tables are used above where available.

## Open research questions
- **What causes model-specific language failures?** Large residual transfer variation remains (§4.2). A next experiment could add measured per-language training exposure and post-training composition, then test held-out predictions.
- **Can linguistic structure be separated from tokenization?** Their correlation limits interpretation (Limitations). Controlled tokenizer comparisons with matched training data could test their independent contributions.
- **Do the resource-gap interactions generalize?** Other domains and architectures are outside the study’s scope. Repeat matched-item evaluations across tasks, base models, and MoEs.
- **How much is the apparent solution-stage gap a measurement effect?** Numeric occurrence is an indirect proxy (Appendix I.1). Human-labeled multilingual traces and extraction supporting number words could validate the stage findings.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text includes methods, detailed results, and robustness analyses, but internal discrepancies limit confidence in exact figures.

Material unresolved information includes reconciled analysis counts and conflicting estimates, complete decoding and token-budget settings, the precise fitting procedure for target-language calibration, and model-specific training exposure. Released code, data, and logs are described but were not inspected.
