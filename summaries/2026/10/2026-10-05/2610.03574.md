# HyperBrowseComp: A Multilingual and Multimodal Stress Test for Web-Browsing Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.03574) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03574)


## One-sentence takeaway
HyperBrowseComp tests difficult evidence discovery with 423 human-authored questions across 13 languages, and the strongest evaluated model–harness configuration answers only 31.68% correctly (Table 2).

## Problem and motivation
The authors identify a gap between browsing benchmarks that separately emphasize persistent search, multilingual retrieval, or multimodal evidence. They target searches requiring agents to interpret implicit clues, discover relevant sources and modalities, and connect obscure evidence across the open web (§§1–3.1).

The intended difficulty comes from locating evidence rather than producing long answers: each question requests a short, publicly verifiable factual answer. Native-language authorship aims to preserve local terminology, cultural context, and distinct information environments (§3.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Benchmark construction:** Questions are independently authored in their languages, with reference answers, aliases, source URLs, exact evidence locations, and solution traces. Separate validators check correctness, accessibility, and uniqueness; Appendix A describes a final core-team validation stage (§3.2; Appendix A.1).
- **Difficulty screening:** Seven models answer without internet access. The authors exclude questions solved by multiple models, plus selected single-model successes requiring highly specific answers, retaining 423 of 500 candidates. This is a screening heuristic, not proof of memorization or browsing necessity (§3.2; Appendix C.1).
- **Comparative evaluation:** Five models are tested with native search; subsets additionally use Exa retrieval or the existing OWL multi-agent framework. Human evaluation and descriptive failure analyses contextualize accuracy and resource use (§4).

Approximately 64.3% of questions are classified as requiring non-text interpretation along their documented evidence routes; multimodal evidence is not mandatory for every item (§3.2).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

| Human and model successes differ on a small sample | On 30 questions across Indonesian, Thai, and Vietnamese: humans solve 15/30, Gemini 3.7 Flash 13/30, with eight jointly correct; human mean elapsed time is 89.50 minutes including give-ups | §4.3; Table 3 |
| Aggregate scores show limited judge sensitivity | Across eight complete retrieval configurations and 3,384 judgments, alternative graders change aggregate accuracy by at most 0.80 percentage points | Appendix B.4; Table 6 |

The results establish difficulty for the tested systems and substantial sensitivity to retrieval integration. They do not isolate whether failures originate in retrieval, access, perception, or reasoning. The authors’ interpretation that native search and models are closely co-adapted is plausible, but the comparisons do not establish that mechanism causally. Judge agreement measures scoring consistency, not independent verification of answer correctness.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Deliberate stress-test construction differs from everyday search; live-web changes impair exact reproducibility; language coverage is incomplete; computational costs restrict model and harness coverage (limitations, pp. 9–10).
- **Author-stated:** Language splits contain different questions and difficulty levels, so their scores do not isolate language ability (§4.4; Figure 7).
- **Analyst inference:** Harness comparisons combine differences in tools, budgets, temperature, retries, and modality access. They therefore support configuration-level comparisons more directly than isolated model or search-tool rankings (Tables 4–5).
- **Analyst inference:** One evaluation epoch provides no estimate of run-to-run variability (Table 4).
- **Analyst inference:** The small, selected human evaluation cannot establish broad human–model parity. Timing guidance is also ambiguous: Appendix A.2 requests approximately one hour per question, while recorded means exceed that duration.
- **Annotation boundary:** Modality labels describe documented routes without opening linked sources during automated classification; they do not rule out alternative text-only solutions (Appendix C.1; Figure 6).

## Open research questions
- **Which stage causes failure?** Existing analyses do not distinguish retrieval from interpretation or reasoning (§4.4). Next experiment: provide verified source URLs, then decisive evidence excerpts, and measure the accuracy recovered at each stage.
- **Which harness is best under equal resources?** Current configurations have different capabilities and budgets. Next experiment: hold the model fixed and compare retrieval systems under matched token, time, and tool-call limits.
- **How much do language differences reflect question difficulty?** Independent language-specific question sets confound this comparison. Next experiment: construct matched evidence-search tasks across languages alongside the native questions.
- **Would more reliable OWL execution improve accuracy?** Terminal failures materially affect its score (§4.2). Next experiment: rerun failed items with corrected execution handling while preserving research budgets, reporting overall and completed-attempt accuracy.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for benchmark design and reported configuration-level results; **medium** for explanations of failure mechanisms.

Material missing information includes full resolved prompts and tool schemas, final evaluated API identifiers, complete execution/retry records, item-level evidence and traces, detailed human participant counts, monetary cost accounting, and repeated-run uncertainty. Several artifacts are promised for release, but their contents and availability cannot be established from the supplied text.
