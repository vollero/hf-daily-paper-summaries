# Receiver-Conditioned Latent Communication gives 94% CacheBack

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.32046) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32046)


## One-sentence takeaway
CacheBack uses the receiver’s question to select sender-state positions without training, improving reported accuracy and median completion latency over same-size text agents on two long-context QA settings, though several accuracy confidence intervals include zero.

## Problem and motivation
Full-cache latent communication accumulates every sender’s context at the receiver, undermining the memory and context benefits of distributing documents across agents. Text handoffs are compact but require autoregressive generation and can omit needed evidence. The authors identify message-content selection—conditioned on what the receiver needs—as a gap distinct from message representation (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Receiver-conditioned protocol:** A receiver supplies a query that guides what information the sender communicates. Experiments use the task question after sender computation (§3).
- **CacheBack selector:** A training-free attention rule boosts positions supported strongly by particular query tokens, correcting dilution from averaging attention across the whole query. Budgeted selection retains source spans, the first position, and all current latent steps (§3.1; Appendix C).
- **Evaluation across architectures and topologies:** Selected positions are transmitted as continuous rows and prefilled again at the receiver. Comparisons cover parallel fan-in and sequential chains, with text baselines receiving the same information need (§4).

The method adopts existing latent-step generation; its central contribution is selecting message content rather than inventing latent communication itself (§2).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| “94%” denotes position removal | At 16× compression, authors report removing 94% of sender positions and outperforming same-size text in accuracy and latency across tested families and benchmark topologies; it is not an accuracy score. | §4, p. 6 |
| FanOutQA gains across four families | Selected Qwen/Ministral/Gemma/Nemotron settings improve strict accuracy by **14.7/20.7/7.3/11.3 pp**, with **3.2×/8.0×/3.0×/1.3×** median TTEOA speedups over same-size text. Each uses 50 questions, three receiver draws, and concurrent eight-H100 serving. | Table 1; Tables 6–8 |
| Strongest highlighted Qwen result | At 4× compression, Qwen 3 8B reaches **55.3% versus 40.7%** strict accuracy; median TTEOA is **192 versus 612 s**. Accuracy-gain 95% CI: **[1.3, 28.0] pp**. | Tables 1, 8 |
| Receiver query materially affects selection | Replacing the task query with a fixed unrelated query for Qwen 3 8B at 16× reduces message recall from **91% to 65%** and strict accuracy from **46% to 23%**. | §4.2; Figure 7 |
| LongBench relative budgets improve reported means | On 50 Easy questions, Qwen 3 8B at relative 4× achieves **54.0% versus 39.3%** accuracy and **237 versus 884 s** median TTEOA; Nemotron at relative 8× achieves **48.0% versus 38.7%** and **133 versus 329 s**. Gain CIs are **[0.0, 29.3]** and **[-5.3, 23.4] pp**, respectively. | Tables 2, 9 |
| Compression eventually harms accuracy | On LongBench Easy with Qwen, increasing relative compression from 16× to 128× changes median TTEOA from **165 to 143 s**, while accuracy falls from **44.7% to 32.7%**. | §4.3, p. 9 |
| Selection is not universally better than sending all rows | Gemma’s full-row FanOutQA control reaches **62%** strict accuracy versus **59%** at CacheBack 4×, but takes **363 versus 201 s** median TTEOA; these appendix accuracies are rounded. | Table 7 |

The evidence supports improved measured tradeoffs in the tested workloads and a causal role for the receiver query under the unrelated-query control. It does not establish universal accuracy gains: FanOutQA confidence intervals for Gemma and Nemotron include zero, as do most selected LongBench intervals (Tables 8–9). Latency measures include batch queueing and configuration-specific GPU allocation, so they do not isolate communication overhead alone.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope:** Queries are issued once, equal the task question, and use predetermined budgets. Adaptive queries, repeated requests, and serving many receivers from one sender state are unevaluated (§5).
- **Authors’ stated selector limits:** CacheBack uses a fixed scoring rule; hybrid models contribute only global-attention layers. The experiments do not establish the best selector (§5).
- **Analyst inference—restricted generalization:** End-to-end evidence covers selected long-context QA panels; LongBench is restricted to Easy questions after text-message truncation problems. Tool use and repository exploration are discussed but not evaluated (§§4.2–4.3, 5; Appendix F.3).
- **Analyst inference—comparison sensitivity:** Text lengths are not matched to latent budgets, GPU allocations differ by channel, and some LongBench text reports still reach the ceiling. Results therefore compare complete configured systems (§4.1; Appendix F).
- **Measurement and reproducibility:** FanOutQA references were audited and modified, although gains persist using original references. Receiver sampling fixes sender messages, and authors note output and timing variability under concurrent serving (Appendices E.3, F.2; p. 10).
- **Representation boundary:** Experiments use selected continuous rows with fresh prefill, rather than direct selected-KV transfer. Positional mismatch for direct KV transfer is unevaluated (Appendix C.3).

## Open research questions
1. **Can adaptive requests recover omitted evidence efficiently?** Single-query, fixed-budget evaluation leaves this open (§5). Compare adaptive follow-up queries against fixed budgets at matched total latency.
2. **How well does inherited evidence survive longer chains?** Only four-reader chains are evaluated, and discarded positions cannot return (§4.3; Appendix C.2). Vary chain length and measure evidence retention by originating document segment.
3. **Do gains persist outside concurrent node-local serving?** Latency includes queueing, and network or prefill costs may narrow the advantage (§5; Appendix F). Repeat comparisons across concurrency levels and bandwidth constraints.
4. **Does the attention correction generalize beyond QA?** Parameters were selected on a small FanOutQA development set; broader agent tasks remain untested (Appendix D.2; §5). Evaluate fixed CacheBack parameters on evidence-heavy tool workflows against mean-query attention and text handoffs.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text documents methods, controls, settings, and uncertainty thoroughly, but accuracy estimates are limited by small panels and sometimes broad intervals.

Unavailable evidence includes independently verified repository contents, evaluations on LongBench Hard or actual tool workflows, adaptive-query results, direct selected-KV transfer results, and repeated full-pipeline sampling that varies sender messages as well as receiver generation.
