# Self-Supervised Scaling of Terminal Environments for Scientific Domains

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.02710) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02710)


## One-sentence takeaway
Reusing executable workflows as hidden behavioral targets produces verified terminal-agent training trajectories that improve reported benchmark means, although unseen-workflow generalization and scientific-task transfer remain untested (§§3, 4.5, 5).

## Problem and motivation
Scientific terminal tasks require reference behavior and domain-specific verification: an executable program or plausible artifact can still be semantically wrong. The authors identify repeated engineering of separate solutions and validators as a scaling bottleneck, alongside limited scientific coverage in existing agent-training data (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Workflow-derived reconstruction tasks:** Execute an existing workflow on typed inputs, expose public input–output examples, and ask an agent to build an editable replacement without source access. Hidden inputs test behavioral agreement. Each workflow supports multiple variants (§§3.1–3.2).
- **SWR and semantic verification:** Construct a collection spanning scientific and supporting software domains. Acceptance combines execution/schema checks, domain-specific output comparison, and anti-shortcut checks; verifier reliability is assessed through calibration tests and an independent post-freeze audit (§§3.3, 4.2–4.3; Appendix I).
- **Verified-trajectory SFT:** Retain successful interactions, exclude evaluation task identities, and oversample trajectories for reconstruction-only supervised fine-tuning. Compare training budgets and alternative corpora with matched assistant-loss tokens (§§3.4, 4.5).

The distinctive contribution is deriving reference behavior and verification targets from the same reusable executable workflow; domain-specific comparison rules still require engineering (§5).

These results support training value under the reported workflow collection and sampling procedure. They do not establish recovery of the original implementation, generalization beyond finite hidden cases, superiority attributable specifically to verification, or improved end-to-end scientific performance (§2; Appendices A and F; §5).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** SWR100 holds out task identities, not entire workflows. Sibling variants share mechanisms and may reuse public examples; scientific coverage is incomplete and difficulty uneven (Appendices A–B).
- **Author-stated confounding:** Larger training-entry budgets include repeated trajectories. Matched-token controls leave task composition, trajectory diversity, and verification entangled (Appendix F).
- **Author-stated verifier limits:** Zero replay success follows task selection and covers only the predefined replay controls. Independent audit errors include numerical-boundary and geometric-equivalence cases (§4.2; Appendix I).
- **Author-stated statistical limits:** SWR100 uses one trial per model–task pair, leaving small differences among low-success models unresolved (Appendix H).
- **Analyst inference:** The demonstrated construction scale does not quantify engineering efficiency: workflow wrapping and semantic comparators remain necessary, but their construction costs are not reported.

## Open research questions
1. **Does training transfer to unseen workflows?** Task-identity exclusion cannot answer this. Next experiment: hold out complete workflows or software families before collecting training trajectories (Appendix A; §5).
2. **How much gain comes from unique trajectories versus repetition?** Sampled-entry scaling conflates them. Next experiment: independently vary unique-trajectory count and repeated exposure at matched loss-token budgets (Appendix F).
3. **Does reconstruction training improve scientific work itself?** External evaluations measure terminal skills. Next experiment: evaluate independently verified, end-to-end scientific workflows rather than reconstruction tasks (§4.1; §5).
4. **How robust is verification to broader candidate errors?** Existing replay controls and finite mutation audits cover selected failures. Next experiment: audit fresh agent-generated incorrect programs and alternative valid geometries with blinded domain review (§4.2; Appendix I).
