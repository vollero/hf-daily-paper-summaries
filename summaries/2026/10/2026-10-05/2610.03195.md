# Source Preference in the Wild: How LLM Agents Favor Items by Source, and How to Reduce It

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.03195) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03195)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across evaluated search agents, source identity changes selection even with content and position controlled, while balanced preference training and targeted missing-information interventions reduce this preference in narrower shopping experiments (§§4–7).

## Problem and motivation
Agents selecting products, accommodations, or papers can favor particular websites even when their results satisfy the same requirements. This may reduce user satisfaction and disadvantage sources offering suitable items. The authors identify a gap between earlier controlled source comparisons and end-to-end search, where agents retrieve their own candidates with different content, satisfaction, and positions (§1; Appendix A).

## Approach and contributions
- **Measurement in end-to-end search:** Evaluate 12 models on 4,822 requests across shopping, accommodation, and scholarly search. Match items on their satisfied requirement vectors, counterbalance positions through cyclic rotations, and estimate source preference using Bradley–Terry ratings with Davidson ties and request-level bootstrap inference (§§2–3).
- **Evidence separating content from source identity:** Measure selections of less satisfying items, then hide source cues or swap displayed sources while holding content fixed (§§4–5).
- **Mechanism and mitigation experiments:** Manipulate source–satisfaction associations during DPO; separately supply missing prices or change source-related assumptions through prompts (§§6–7). These establish possible mechanisms, rather than reconstructing the evaluated agents’ actual training histories.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Source preferences appear throughout the evaluation | All 12 models have preferred and dispreferred sources in each domain. Among the four most frequent sources per domain, 110/144 model–source combinations are significant, with median τ of +15/−18 percentage points for preferred/dispreferred groups. | §4.1; Table 1; Table E.1 |
| Source preference accompanies worse requirement satisfaction | For pairs differing by exactly one satisfied requirement, conditional on exactly one item being selected, median inversion rates are 68% when the worse item has a preferred source, 2% with labels reversed, and 19% for neutral-source pairs. | §4.2; Figure 3; Table F.5 |
| Explicit source cues strengthen preference | Across 11 models, restoring URLs and source names increases preferred-source mean τ by 5.8 pp and decreases dispreferred-source mean τ by 5.9 pp, averaging domains equally within models and then models equally. | §5.1; Figure 4; Appendix G |
| Changing source identity changes selection | With fixed content and balanced positions, displayed preferred sources have positive selection advantages in every model–domain cell. For Llama-4-Maverick shopping pairs, T+−T− is +64.7 pp; T+ is 82.35% over 289 complete four-arm pairs. | §5.2; Table 3; Table H.1 |
| DPO can create preference for unfamiliar sources | Across three models and three fake-source pairs, equal-satisfaction target-source selection reaches 70.1–75.1% under 80% alignment and 24.5–28.5% under 20% alignment, versus approximately 50% before DPO or with balanced assignment. | §6.1; Table 4 |
| Balanced DPO reduces existing Amazon preference | Across three models comparing Amazon against eBay, Etsy, and AliExpress, equal-satisfaction Amazon selection changes from 53.7–62.7% before DPO to 49.7–51.9% after balanced DPO. | §6.2; Table 5 |
| Missing-price interventions reduce preference selectively | In five shopping models, adding identical qualifying prices reduces preferred-source selection by 2.8–28.3 pp. Designating the dispreferred retailer as inexpensive reduces it by 7.4–22.5 pp; the general source–price independence instruction changes it only −1.1 to +1.3 pp. | §7; Table 6 |

The source-swap experiment supports a causal effect of displayed source identity **within its controlled pairwise setting**. Matched natural-search comparisons remain observational, and “better” means one additional judged requirement, rather than demonstrated overall user utility. DPO shows that training associations can produce preference; it does not establish why the original agents acquired theirs (Appendix I).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated boundaries:** Agents cannot inspect webpages; selection relies entirely on titles, snippets, and URLs. API decoding is not guaranteed deterministic. Gemini is excluded from source-disclosure experiments because of cost (Appendices B, G).
- **Authors’ mitigation caveat:** The targeted prompt counteracts preference rather than removing its cause and requires identifying the dispreferred source (§7.2).
- **Analyst inference—measurement validity:** Requirement matching inherits judge errors and rubric omissions. Human validation samples only agent-selected results; accommodation extraction removes subjective preferences. Equality under this rubric therefore need not imply equal suitability (§D).
- **Analyst inference—selection and estimation:** Empty-selection runs are excluded; source labels are estimated on full data rather than out of fold; swap tests report unadjusted significance. These constrain interpretation of conditional rates and selected source groups (Appendices E, H).
- **Analyst inference—generalization:** Training mitigation uses shopping pairs and three models; missing-price interventions use five models. Transfer to full search workflows or other domains is not established (§§6–7).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Appendix J details one fake-source pair and a checkpoint protocol naming two models, whereas Table 4 reports three source pairs and three models. Implementation coverage for the additional configurations is unclear.

## Open research questions
- **Does balanced DPO improve full-workflow selection quality?** The reported mitigation uses equal-satisfaction shopping pairs. Next experiment: evaluate trained agents on held-out end-to-end requests, measuring preference, inversions, and requirement satisfaction.
- **Can missing-information mitigation transfer beyond prices?** §7 tests shopping price gaps. Next experiment: withhold and restore accommodation amenities or scholarly-method details, then compare neutral and targeted prompts.
- **How much preference survives richer evidence?** The environment prevents webpage inspection. Next experiment: compare snippet-only agents with agents allowed to inspect complete pages under matched source assignments.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported patterns; **medium** in mechanism generalization and mitigation effectiveness.

Material gaps include intervention sample sizes and uncertainty for Table 6, complete training details for every Table 4–5 configuration, search collection dates, and demonstrated mitigation effects on end-to-end user satisfaction. Code and data are promised but their availability is not established in the supplied text.
