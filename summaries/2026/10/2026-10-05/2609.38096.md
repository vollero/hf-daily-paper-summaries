# Tail-Influence Sampling for CVaR Policy Evaluation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.38096) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38096)


## One-sentence takeaway
Tail-Influence Sampling learns which independently queryable transition laws most affect a fixed policy’s lower-tail CVaR, attaining oracle asymptotic accuracy under stated assumptions but showing practical gains that depend on pilot reliability and whether tail-specific allocation is needed.

## Problem and motivation
Estimating the average return in a policy’s worst fraction of runs can require many costly trajectories. When evaluators can directly query selected state–action transitions or reconstruct workflow prompts, they can allocate that budget more selectively.

The authors identify a gap: visitation and reward variability do not capture how uncertainty propagates into the final tail estimate, particularly when the same conditional law is reused across stages. The objective is accurate evaluation of a fixed policy, rather than policy optimization (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Tail influence for shared kernels:** A categorical Bellman model and backward sensitivity calculation map each observation to its first-order CVaR effect. Effects across every reuse of a kernel are summed before computing variance, preserving cross-stage covariance. These scales determine the fixed-design efficiency bound and classical Neyman allocation (§3, Theorems 1–2).
- **Learning the allocation:** TIS fits a uniform pilot, estimates influence scales, and allocates fresh samples proportionally with a uniform exploration floor. The authors prove oracle asymptotic variance and first-order MSE, including discarded pilot cost (§4, Theorem 3).
- **Defensive anchoring:** Anchored TIS averages tail-influence and learned-visitation allocations. Its asymptotic MSE constant is at most twice the oracle’s under the same assumptions; this is not a finite-budget guarantee (§4, Corollary 1).
- **When tail specificity matters:** On exact grids, if submaximal returns occur less frequently than the chosen tail level, tail- and mean-optimal allocations coincide. Tail–mean allocation divergence motivates a method-selection diagnostic (§4, Proposition 2; Appendices E.6, E.9).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

The evidence supports conditional-query efficiency and identifies concrete pilot-underallocation failures. It does not establish universal superiority, live API/GPU savings, or generalization beyond the evaluated task panels. Rollout comparisons match charged transition cost but use different access models; the efficiency theorem covers conditional sampling, not trajectories (§5; Appendix F).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Guarantees are pointwise asymptotic results with fixed dimensions and positive quantile margin; finite-budget MSE guarantees remain open (§§4, 7).
- **Author-stated:** Both anchor components can underallocate the same consequential kernel. Pilot cost can outweigh any allocation advantage (§4; Table 1).
- **Author-stated:** A nonexact grid introduces CVaR approximation error bounded by \(H\Delta\); reported experiments use exact closed grids (Appendices E.1–E.2).
- **Author-stated:** Language-model experiments use frozen laws and restricted outputs. Uncertainty measures simulation error conditional on fixed panels; live-call cost is outside scope (Appendix E.2.6).
- **Analyst inference:** FinQA’s screen selects candidate banks with material severity disagreement, limiting transfer to unscreened financial questions (Appendix E.3).
- **Analyst inference:** The diagnostic needs further validation under rare failures: near-deterministic FinQA pilots sometimes overestimate tail–mean divergence and select the worse blend (Appendix E.9).

## Open research questions
- **Can finite-budget guarantees guide pilot size and exploration?** Existing results do not provide an operational finite-sample guarantee. Test budget-adaptive pilots and floors across increasingly rare outcomes and shrinking quantile margins.
- **Does the method transfer to realistic live workflows?** Frozen, restricted-output evaluation leaves this open. Compare allocations using live free-form reviews at matched call, token, and monetary budgets.
- **Can method selection remain reliable under near-determinism?** The pilot diagnostic fails in some rare-error settings. Test uncertainty-aware divergence estimates on fresh screened and unscreened panels.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the summary of the supplied theory and experiments; practical transfer remains uncertain.

Unavailable evidence includes live-call cost-to-accuracy measurements, finite-budget MSE guarantees, and performance on unrestricted outputs or history-dependent workflows. The paper links code, but its contents and artifact completeness cannot be verified from the supplied text alone.
