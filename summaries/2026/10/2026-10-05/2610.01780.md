# RealCompanion: Benchmarking Human Understanding from Reasoning over Longitudinal Real-World Conversations

> [← Daily summary](../2026-10-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.01780) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01780)


## One-sentence takeaway
RealCompanion benchmarks memory selection and persona reconstruction on ten real companion relationships, showing that pooled scores can obscure poor performance on messages requiring distant history, while reconstruction scores are materially affected by ground-truth construction (§6; Appendix G.6).

## Problem and motivation
A companion must infer who a person is and decide which earlier disclosures matter to the current message. The authors argue that generated histories, predefined personas, and authored questions often determine these answers in advance, emphasizing factual recall over deciding whether memory is warranted (§§1–2).

The proposed gap is evaluation against real longitudinal conversations with traceable evidence for both personal claims and memory requirements.

## Approach and contributions

- **Real longitudinal benchmark:** Ten consenting users contribute 27,218 messages across relationships lasting 36–120 days. Each relationship has a conversation, profile, persona, chat ground truth, and question set; derived claims cite conversation messages (§§1, 3, 5).
- **Auditable annotation:** A five-stage pipeline resolves referents, verifies candidate evidence, assigns labels, writes reference replies, and checks grounding. Decisions and their reasoning are recorded together; mechanical checks enforce citation resolution and temporal consistency (§4).
- **Separate evaluation tracks:** Reconstruction tests profile/persona extraction; chat tests replies to observed user messages; questions test authored queries over the same histories. Random proportional sampling, enriched distant-history probes, and cold-open controls serve different purposes (§§4.1, 5).
- **Measurement framework:** The authors formalize restraint, evidence location, and response evaluation, and show why an aggregate context benefit cannot identify the contribution from memory-dependent probes without a demand split (Appendix A).

These are dataset and evaluation contributions, rather than a new memory architecture.

## Evidence and main results

All figures below are author-reported.

| Required history can be distant | For memory-bearing probes, the furthest required message is a median 2,157 messages back; the nearest is a median 450 back. | §6 |

| Context benefit largely concerns probes without distant-memory demand | Recorded evidence versus three recent messages improves proportional-stratum content match by +0.108 [0.077, 0.159] on a 0–2 scale; 96% of that gain comes from probes needing no memory. On 167 reference-dependent items, improvement is +0.455 [0.319, 0.588]. | §6; Table 5; Appendix A.3 |
| Tested local detectors struggle | With the probe and three preceding messages, proportional-stratum AUROC is 0.530 [0.488, 0.572] against absent-referent negatives. Against its own target, AUROC is 0.601 [0.527, 0.676]; its threshold catches 6/40 positives with 40 false alarms. | §6; Appendix A.3 |
| Context framing changes memory use | Labeling the same ten messages as retrieved memories rather than earlier chat turns raises memory use by 10–14 percentage points across tested populations, including probes without memory demand. | §6 |
| Similar reconstruction overlap accompanies different token consumption | Three runs per system over ten users yield persona F1 0.69–0.70; Codex processes about 31× fewer tokens than Claude. This is processed-token accounting, not an established monetary-cost comparison. | Appendices G.4, G.7 |

The evidence supports separating natural-rate detection, distant retrieval, and response application. It does not establish that memory demand is equally rare elsewhere, that every possible detector must fail, or that reconstruction F1 measures psychological accuracy. The authors explicitly restrict detector conclusions to those tested (Appendix A.3).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Users selected one application; they are not a representative population. Two relationships supply 73% of messages, and strict memory-conditioned results involve only five participants (§3; Appendices H.5, I.5).
- **Author-stated annotation uncertainty:** Verification can miss genuine referents. A low measured demand rate is therefore not an upper bound on true demand; the rarity interpretation depends on annotation recall (Appendix A.3).
- **Author-stated preprocessing effects:** Every message was rewritten, media are omitted, and rewriting’s effect on retrieval and response scores was not measured (Appendices H.2, H.4).
- **Author-stated judging limits:** On-screen judgments rely on one reader without inter-reader agreement. Graded judge agreement is κ=0.545; the question track retains a 3.8% [3.2, 4.6] content-defect rate after repair (Appendix I.5).
- **Reconstruction validity:** Presence-based scoring does not assess prose correctness; exact matching rejects some correct demographic paraphrases. Persona ground-truth truncation and differing system context handling confound capability comparisons (Appendices G.2–G.6).
- **Unseparated effects:** C2 versus C1 also changes headings; the paper does not isolate that contribution (§6).
- **Privacy and interpretation:** The authors report residual authorship matching after anonymization. Persona traits are neither validated psychological measures nor participant-reviewed assessments (Ethics statement; Appendix H.4).
- **Analyst inference:** High agreement among systems may reflect shared extraction tendencies or incomplete ground truth; it cannot establish that their common interpretation is correct.

## Open research questions

- **Can history-aware gating outperform local detectors?** Only the tested detectors are ruled out. A next experiment could compare local and history-aware gates on held-out participants, reporting false alarms and recall at natural demand rates (Appendix A.3).
- **How much does anonymization change benchmark difficulty?** Its score impact is unmeasured. A next experiment could compare original and rewritten histories within authorized private evaluation (Appendix H.4).
- **Does corrected persona ground truth change system rankings?** Truncation removes supported dimensions. A next experiment could rebuild complete annotations and evaluate claim correctness alongside field presence (Appendix G.6).
- **Do demand rates transfer across products?** The corpus covers one application. A next experiment could replicate proportional sampling across independently consented companion settings (Appendix I.5).

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the main design and reported findings, but the source is substantially truncated; this analysis does not cover the complete paper.

Material unavailable information includes full contents of many tables, detailed chat-audit results, generation/detection model settings, complete scoring prompts, per-participant response results, and absolute token totals. These omissions prevent independent assessment of several comparisons and reproducibility details.
