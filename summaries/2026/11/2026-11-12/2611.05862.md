# CARTBench: Rubric-Based Evaluation of Shopping Advice for Complex and Ambiguous Requests with Timelines

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.05862) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05862)


## One-sentence takeaway
CARTBench finds that strong compliance with basic shopping requirements can coexist with weak decision support when models must reconcile changing needs, uncertain preferences, and limited product evidence.

## Problem and motivation
Shopping advice must account for interacting constraints, expired requests, and facts that remain unknown. The authors argue that existing benchmarks insufficiently combine explicit timelines, preservation of unknown needs, and consistent case-specific evaluation criteria (Introduction; Table 1).

They identify two construction challenges: obtaining real commercial conversations containing difficult requests, and tailoring evaluation criteria without losing consistency across cases (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Benchmark:** 169 expert-selected cases across 17 retail domains, with timestamped dialogue and 1,603 cached product records. Evaluation concerns the current advice reply; upstream elicitation and purchase outcomes are excluded (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Principles and rubrics:** Four Ideal Response Principles, distilled from selected successful-order replies, guide 1,328 frozen criteria across Core, Excellence, and Safety. Models receive the principles but cannot access the case rubrics (§§3.2–3.3).
- **Judging protocol:** Evidence-linked criterion labels undergo blinded expert review. Pass rates exclude unresolved and not-applicable judgments; resolved coverage is defined separately (§3.4).
- **Comparative diagnosis:** Ten model configurations are evaluated, followed by analyses of unsupported claims, unjustified suitability, context tracking, and plan validity (§4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Required behavior substantially exceeds high-quality decision help | In the first 169-case evaluation, 3 of 10 models exceed 90% Core; none exceeds 45% Excellence, under shared principle-guided prompting. | Abstract; §§1, 4.1 |
| High Core scores do not imply high Excellence scores | Across three suites, GPT-6.1-Sol scores 95.9 ± 0.6% Core and 29.6 ± 1.1% Excellence; Claude-Opus-5 scores 89.0 ± 1.0% and 39.8 ± 1.2%, respectively. Values are means ± sample SD. | Table 2 |
| Judge–expert agreement is high in the reviewed evaluation | Initial judge labels agree with one blinded expert on 13,239/13,280 judgments, or 99.69%, in the first suite. Reported scores use expert-confirmed labels. | §4.1 |
| Unsupported product claims and unjustified suitability dominate diagnosed Core failures | They account for 30.8% and 22.1% of pooled failed Core judgments, respectively; both occur in every evaluated model. Each failed instance receives one primary diagnosis. | §4.2.1; Figure 5 |
| Failure profiles differ by scenario | In task-switch cases, context-state drift affects 24.5% of replies. In decisive-unknown cases, unsupported claims affect 19.4%, unjustified suitability 15.0%, and premature need resolution 5.3%. Rates equally weight models and cases; groups overlap. | §4.2.2; Figure 6 |
| Passing assessed need and evidence checks does not ensure plan validity | Among eligible replies passing all resolved need and evidence checks, 13.0% fail a plan check; equal-model-weighted 95% interval: 8.1–18.4%. | §4.2.3 |

These results support the authors’ claim of a rubric-measured gap between basic compliance and useful purchase guidance. They do not establish effects on actual purchases or user satisfaction. Core and Excellence measure different obligations, so their difference is descriptive rather than a causal measure of an internal “understanding-to-execution” process.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Specialized shopping needs and adaptation across longer purchase, return, and replacement cycles remain untested (Limitations, pp. 8–9).
- **Analyst inference—scope:** Stratified difficult-case selection supports targeted diagnosis, but its distribution does not represent shopping traffic (§3.2; Figure 4).
- **Analyst inference—evaluation independence:** Agreement with one expert does not establish agreement across multiple independent experts; no inter-expert reliability result is supplied (§4.1).
- **Analyst inference—method attribution:** No prompting ablation is reported, so the benefit of the proposed principles over alternative prompts is not established (§§3.3–4).
- **Analyst inference—reproducibility:** The successful-order replies used to derive principles are withheld for privacy. Detailed principle-distillation procedures and generation settings are not supplied (§3.2).
- **Analyst inference—metric interpretation:** Unresolved judgments are excluded from pass-rate denominators, but corresponding resolved-coverage results are not shown in Table 2 (§3.4).

## Open research questions
- **Do the principles improve advice?** All reported models use them, leaving their contribution unresolved. Compare identical models with and without the principles under unchanged hidden rubrics.
- **How robust are the rubrics across evaluators?** One expert reviews labels. Have multiple independent experts reannotate a stratified sample and measure agreement separately by tier.
- **Does rubric performance predict useful purchases?** Purchase outcomes are excluded. Prospectively compare rubric scores with user assessments and subsequent product-use outcomes.
- **Can stronger evidence and state tracking reduce plan failures?** Diagnostics identify recurring errors without testing remedies. Compare explicit timeline tracking and claim verification against the existing prompting setup.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the descriptive summary of the supplied text; **medium** in broader validity.

Material missing information includes full prompts and rubrics, model generation settings, tier-specific criterion counts, reported resolved coverage, detailed expert-selection and annotation procedures, the plan-analysis sample size and interval-construction method, and actual purchase outcomes. Code/data availability is asserted by the paper but was not independently verified.
