# Precision on Demand: Escalating Weight Bits at Uncertain Decoding Steps in a Small Local Model

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.14418) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14418)


## One-sentence takeaway
Uncertainty gates identify precision-sensitive decoding steps in Qwen3-0.6B replay, but the small GSM8K live study does not establish preserved accuracy or practical speedup, and verified speculation achieves better distributional fidelity at similar modeled cost (§§6–8).

## Problem and motivation
Fixed weight precision trades decoding cost against accuracy across an entire generation. The authors ask whether selectively redoing uncertain low-precision steps at higher precision allocates weight reads more effectively than random escalation. Their identified gap is a per-token policy combining nested weight precision, uncertainty gating without verification, and explicit byte accounting; the novelty search covered titles and abstracts only (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Selective precision escalation:** Store one 8-bit weight representation, derive a low version from its upper four bits, and redo a step at 8 bits when low-pass entropy is high or the top-two logit margin is small. Thresholds are calibrated on 32 GSM8K training problems. Embeddings and the output head remain bf16 (§3).
- **Replay evaluation and controls:** Measure disagreement on fixed all-high trajectories, comparing gates with analytic matched-random expectations, true-KL ordering, and verified speculation (§§5–6).
- **Live evaluation and cost accounting:** Compare eight gates with all-high and all-low decoding on paired problems, explicitly charging for the discarded low pass before escalation and the bf16 head (§§3, 7–8).

The contribution is this policy and its empirical evaluation; nested storage and entropy-based switching are acknowledged prior ideas (§2).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Uncertainty predicts precision disagreement | On 36,724 all-high replay steps from 150 problems, flip-prediction AUC is 0.928 for entropy (95% interval 0.924–0.932) and 0.959 for margin (0.956–0.962). | §5, p. 6 |
| Gates outperform random allocation in replay | At an exact 15% escalation budget, entropy captures 78% of flips and 59% of KL mass; margin captures 94% and 55%. Matched random captures 15% of each **in expectation**, without a sampled run. | §6, Figs. 10–11 |
| Live entropy gating has uncertain accuracy effects | On 150 paired GSM8K problems, ent2 scores 62.7% at 15.7% escalation and 0.874× all-high weight bytes/token. Its difference is −1.3 points versus all-high (95% interval −8.0 to +5.3) and +7.3 versus all-low (+0.0 to +14.7; McNemar p=0.080). All-high scores 64.0%; all-low 55.3%. | §7, Table 7 |
| Best observed gate needs cautious interpretation | mar2 scores 66.0% at 0.868× all-high bytes/token. Versus all-low, its paired gain is +10.7 points (+2.7 to +18.7; p=0.017), but tests are uncorrected and mar2 is the highest-scoring configuration. | §7, Table 7 |
| Verified speculation wins the modeled fidelity comparison | Replay with proposal length k=4 costs 0.845× all-high bytes/token with zero KL by construction; calibrated ent2 costs 0.867× with residual KL 0.0084 nats. Verification assumes one weight read serves the verification block. | §6.1, Tables 5–6 |
| The output head constrains modeled savings | A low pass costs 0.717× a high pass including the bf16 head, versus 0.529× for blocks alone. Every escalation additionally pays for a high pass. | §3.3, Table 2 |

These results establish useful **step-level ranking on fixed reference trajectories**. They do not establish live superiority over matched random escalation, equivalence to all-high accuracy, or hardware speedup. The “oracle” ranks by KL, so its flip recall is not an upper bound on flip recall (§§3.4, 6).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope:** One model, one benchmark, greedy non-thinking decoding, and a token cap that affects answer completion (§11).
- **Statistical uncertainty:** The live sample is small, comparisons are uncorrected, and selecting the best observed gate can inflate its estimated benefit (§§7, 10).
- **Missing controls:** No live matched-random control, live KL, thinking-mode phase run, second model, or second benchmark (§§1.1, 11).
- **Simulation and cost limits:** Computation uses dequantized bf16 tensors without packed kernels. The cost model excludes activation traffic, KV traffic, kernel launches, and dequantization overhead (§§3.1, 8).
- **Calibration and quantizer sensitivity:** Only one calibration sample, group size, and low-bit setting were tested; tuned quantizers were not evaluated (§§10–11).
- **Analyst inference:** Replay cannot establish recovery after context divergence because it keeps the all-high prefix fixed; early departures make this distinction consequential (§§5.4, 6).

## Open research questions
1. **Does gating beat random escalation in live decoding?** Only replay expectation was evaluated. Run paired live gates and matched-rate random controls on a larger problem sample (§§6, 10).
2. **Do modeled byte savings translate into latency savings?** Packed kernels and runtime measurements are absent. Implement nested precision kernels and benchmark gating against all-high, all-low, and verified speculation (§8).
3. **Does the policy transfer across quantizers and models?** Evidence covers one simulated quantizer and model. Repeat calibration and evaluation with tuned low-bit weights, additional models, and another benchmark (§11).
4. **Can phase or lagged gates avoid redundant work?** Both remain untested. Compare them with current-step gating and matched random controls, including thinking-mode generations (§§3.2, 9).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly supports the replay findings and live uncertainty, but practical efficiency and generality remain unestablished.

Material missing information includes public access to code and cached results, packed-kernel measurements, live random-control and KL results, calibration-repeat variability, and evaluations beyond the single model–benchmark setting.
