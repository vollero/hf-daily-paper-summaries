# CogBattery: Human-Referenced Evaluation of Cognitive Task Behavior and Structure in Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.24999) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24999)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across 54 open-weight models, CogBattery finds that matching human cognitive-effect directions can conceal different errors and confidence reports, while predefined cognitive task groups provide no reliable predictive advantage (§§5–6).

## Problem and motivation
Human cognitive tasks offer controlled comparisons, but reproducing an effect’s direction does not establish human-like behavior. Aggregate accuracy can also hide differences in error types and confidence. The authors identify limited coverage across models and paradigms in prior evaluations and address this with source-specific human comparisons (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Human-referenced battery:** Text adaptations of 13 paradigms covering working memory, cognitive control, episodic memory, social reasoning, and metacognition, evaluated on 54 models from 23 families. Each paradigm uses its own published human reference rather than a shared human cohort (§§2–3).
- **Behavioral analysis:** Condition-level errors, confidence discrimination, wagering, minimal-performance gates, and model-size comparisons test whether apparent similarities survive closer inspection (§§4–5).
- **Structure analysis:** PCA, factor analysis, jointly measured human subsets, and family-held-out prediction test shared score dimensions and whether predefined groups outperform equally sized random groupings (§§4.2–4.3, 6).
- **Reusable evaluation materials:** The authors describe common text materials, human references, and scorers for testing additional models; response release is promised upon publication, subject to material licenses (§§1, 8).

## Evidence and main results

Unless otherwise stated, behavioral differences below are model minus human; their 95% intervals resample model families while holding human means fixed (§4.1).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Shared n-back lure effects conceal excess ordinary false alarms | In 2-back, ordinary non-target false-alarm rates were .328 for models versus .071 for humans: difference .257, 95% CI [.159, .341]. Excess occurred in 39/54 models. | Table 4; §5.1 |
| Stroop interference is substantially larger | Conflict-related error increase was .371 versus .051: difference .320 [.247, .448]. Models answered with the word on .376 of incongruent trials versus .042 for humans. | Table 4; §5.2 |
| Verbal confidence discriminates correctness less well | Calibration type-2 AUROC was .573 versus .714: difference −.141 [−.196, −.105], with lower values in 47/54 models. | Table 4; §5.5 |
| Token confidence gives a different picture | Among 41 direct-answer models meeting a post hoc option-coverage threshold, token AUROC was .763 versus matched-item verbal AUROC .569; token AUROC exceeded the human reference by .049 [.021, .067]. Across 48 computable models, token AUROC was .705. | §5.5 |
| Wagers often reflect response-label preference | 37/54 models always chose one wager. In the counterbalanced-label rerun, 30/51 chose the same letter on at least 90% of wagers under both mappings. No human constant-wager proportion was available. | Table 4; §5.5 |
| Larger models do not uniformly close gaps | Among nine models with at least 27B parameters, the 2-back false-alarm excess remained .351 [.095, .644]; Stroop and verbal-AUROC gap intervals included zero. | §5.6 |
| A broad score dimension tracks scale | PC1 explained 39.23% of standardized task-score variance and correlated with log parameter count at r=.797. Parallel analyses retained two dimensions, but the second was unstable under family resampling. | §6 |
| Predefined groups lack reliable incremental prediction | Adding same-group information to PC1 reduced family-held-out RMSE from 1.563 to 1.515: gain 3.02% [−5.85%, 6.01%]. Of 600,600 same-size random groupings, 14.62% performed at least as well. | §6 |

These results support behavioral differences under the supplied protocols. They do not identify a common causal mechanism, establish human-like cognitive constructs, or demonstrate equivalence between predefined and random groups. Token AUROC measures discrimination, not whether models use confidence to guide behavior (§§6–7).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors:** Humans were not tested on the text adaptations. Visible histories, omitted timing and motor demands, and nominal delays prevent direct interpretations as memory capacity, retention, or human-equivalent control ( §§5.1–5.3; Limitations).
- **Authors:** Each model supplied one temperature-zero response per step; response distributions and closed-model behavior were not evaluated. Training-data contamination was not tested.
- **Authors:** Human references differ across paradigms. Operation-span correspondence is weaker, wagering uses published summaries, and joint cohorts omit human structural comparisons for calibration, recursive mindreading, and wagering (§3.1; Limitations).
- **Authors:** Key endpoints were selected after initial results, and the token-coverage threshold was post hoc. Human sampling uncertainty removes some initially nonzero intervals, including the source-memory endpoint (Table 4; §5.5; Limitations).
- **Analyst inference:** Gates reduce some explanations based on poor task performance but cannot isolate cognitive mechanisms: the authors explicitly note that gates need not establish comprehension (§5.6).

## Open research questions
1. **How much of each gap comes from adaptation?** No matched human text-version experiment was run. Test humans and models on identical text materials, alongside the original human protocol.
2. **Can verbal confidence reliably expose the token-level correctness signal?** The two measures disagree and correlate weakly (§5.5). Prospectively compare confidence prompts and mappings on held-out items.
3. **Do cognitive groupings generalize to other paradigms?** The negative predictive result concerns this battery, and the second dimension is unstable (§6). Preregister additional tasks and evaluate prediction in new model families.
4. **Does native thinking explain stronger n-back performance?** Only three models used native thinking, and they were the only models reaching the stated high gate-passer accuracy level (§§3.2, 5.6). Compare the same models with thinking enabled and disabled under matched budgets.

## Confidence and missing information
