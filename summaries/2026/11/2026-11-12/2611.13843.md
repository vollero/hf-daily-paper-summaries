# PolyRoute: A Twelve-Language and Code-Mixed Benchmark for Typed-Decision Routing, with Baselines and a Leaderboard

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.13843) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13843)


## One-sentence takeaway
PolyRoute provides a synthetic multilingual routing benchmark where task fine-tuning substantially improves encoder accuracy, but small test sets, prior language exposure, and strong lexical baselines constrain conclusions about transfer and model ranking.

## Problem and motivation
Typed-decision encoders classify messages in one forward pass, making them attractive for support routing, but their performance across languages needs systematic evaluation. The authors identify a gap in fixed, documented tests for this interface and propose a deliberately simple billing/technical/other decision to emphasize language coverage rather than task complexity (§§1–2).

The benchmark targets model comparison and language-gap auditing; it does not measure performance on real customer traffic (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Benchmark and training assets:** 322 native-language test messages across 12 languages and nine writing systems, 156 Hinglish/Spanglish/Arabizi test messages, and 967 optional native training rows. Questions and option descriptions remain in English (§§2–4).
- **Documented synthetic construction:** Qwen3.8-27B generates label-conditioned messages, then blindly relabels them for filtering. A separate gemma4-31b audit checks native-test labels; character-level similarity checks examine split overlap (§§3, 5).
- **Evaluation protocol and baselines:** Separate training-exposure tracks, 13 systems, uncertainty estimates, paired comparisons, calibration metrics, and rank-stability analysis (§§6–8).
- **Diagnostic studies:** Reduced-exposure transfer, native-row learning curves, code-mixed performance, script groups, tokenizer fertility, and sensitivity to option names/order (§§9–11).

The primary contribution is the evaluation resource and protocol, rather than a new encoder architecture.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Multilingual task fine-tuning gives large native-split gains | On 322 items: English root **61.2%** [55.8–66.3], shipped multilingual **77.3%** [72.4–81.6], multilingual fine-tune without dedicated native rows **93.8%** [90.6–95.9], all-native-row fine-tune **96.6%** [94.0–98.1]. Intervals are 95% Wilson. | Table 10 |
| Lexical and prompted references are strong | Native accuracy: TF-IDF+LR trained on all department rows **91.9%**; gemma4-31b **97.2%**. The encoder’s advantage over TF-IDF is **4.7 percentage points**, p=0.014; gemma’s advantage over the encoder is **0.6 points**, p=0.804. | §8.2; Table 10 |
| Label agreement is high, without human validation | Independent model agrees on **313/322 items: 97.2%** [94.8–98.5]; Spanish agreement is **86%**. | §5.1; Table 2 |
| Surface overlap is low | Native-test versus training maximum character 5-gram Jaccard similarity **0.328**, with **zero exact duplicates**; this audit does not detect paraphrases reliably. | §5.3; Table 4 |
| Transfer is demonstrated under reduced exposure | On 103 items from four withheld languages: shipped multilingual **76.7%**, withheld-language fine-tune **90.3%** (+13.6 points, p=0.004), all-language fine-tune **93.2%** (+2.9 points, p=0.250). Generic examples of these languages remain in training. | §9.4; Table 14 |
| Code-mixed and native results differ materially | On 156 code-mixed items: root **84.6%**, shipped multilingual **79.5%** (p=0.134). English-root fine-tuning reaches **96.2%**, versus **73.6%** on native text. | §9.6; Table 16 |
| Top-system ordering is unresolved | Top five fine-tuned systems average **3.6%** pairwise discordance; estimated detectable difference is **3.6 points** at α=0.01 and 80% power, exceeding their largest **2.8-point** accuracy gap. | §7.2 |
| Schema wording strongly affects results | On the same native items, rotating option names while retaining descriptions reduces the all-native-row fine-tune from **96.6% to 6.5%**; its predictions change across option orders on **1.6%** of items. | §10.1; Table 18 |

These results support substantial improvements on this fixed synthetic routing task. They do not establish real-world multilingual competence, exposure-free transfer, or a reliable ordering of closely scoring systems. Independent-model agreement is evidence of label consistency, not human correctness. The supplied prose occasionally reverses the sign of the TF-IDF comparison; Table 10 clearly places TF-IDF below the best fine-tuned encoder.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** All messages and labels are LLM-generated, with no native-speaker review. Dialect, typos, domain vocabulary, and realistic language mixing are underrepresented; cultural fit and stereotypes were not audited (§13).
- **Author-stated:** One topical task and fixed English schema limit generalization to syntax-sensitive tasks and other decision interfaces. Strong lexical results and a high ceiling reduce discrimination (§§2.3, 8, 13).
- **Author-stated:** Small language cells and code-mixed samples yield wide uncertainty. Most recipes have one training run; only the best recipe has three seeds (§§7, 8.4, 13).
- **Author-stated:** Every training track includes generic multilingual rows. Withheld-language results therefore measure reduced exposure (§§4.3, 9.4).
- **Author-stated:** Language-generation artifacts remain, and no classical or prompted-LLM baseline was evaluated on code-mixed text (§§4.4, 13).
- **Analyst inference:** The audit model’s leaderboard score and label-agreement score come from the same predictions, so they are not independent pieces of validation (§8.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Removing ambiguous candidates through teacher self-consistency may favor easily separable messages; lower retention for “other” and its remaining boundary errors support this concern (§§3.2, 11).

## Open research questions
- **Do gains survive human-written traffic?** Synthetic-only evaluation leaves deployment validity open. Next experiment: collect native-speaker customer messages and independently adjudicate labels.
- **How much transfer remains without generic language exposure?** The base training block prevents an exposure-free comparison. Next experiment: rebuild it without withheld-language examples and repeat matched fine-tuning across seeds.
- **Can models follow descriptions when names conflict?** Rotated-name collapse shows strong name dependence. Next experiment: train with varied names and descriptions, then test unseen schemas separately.
- **Do encoders outperform lexical baselines on realistic code mixing?** Those references were not run on the code-mixed split. Next experiment: evaluate matched baselines on a larger, human-written split spanning mixing intensity and romanization styles.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported benchmark findings; **low** in extrapolation to deployment.

Material missing information includes human judgments of fluency and labels, exposure-free transfer results, multi-seed results for most recipes, code-mixed reference baselines, and complete fine-tuning hyperparameters. Appendix D paraphrases generation prompts, and Appendix C supplies only abbreviated checksums; artifact contents and availability cannot be verified from the supplied text.
