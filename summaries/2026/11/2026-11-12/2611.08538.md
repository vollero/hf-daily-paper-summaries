# Subliminal Learning in Vision-Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.08538) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08538)


## One-sentence takeaway
In controlled VLM experiments, finetuning students on teachers’ unrelated visual-number outputs transferred induced image preferences and explanation wording, with the strongest reported animal preference shift reaching +9.92 percentage points (§5.1).

## Problem and motivation
Teacher-generated training data may transmit behaviors beyond their explicit task content. The authors ask whether this “subliminal learning,” previously studied in language models, also occurs when supervision depends on images. This matters for synthetic-data training and distillation: inspecting examples for explicit preference-related content may miss behavioral effects (§§1–2).

The identified gap is whether hidden visual preferences can propagate through an unrelated, visually grounded task involving only geometric shapes and numerical outputs.

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled multimodal transfer experiment:** Finetune biased and balanced-control teachers from the same base VLM, then train fresh students on their respective numerical outputs for identical shape-counting images. Joint validity filtering ensures that only the numerical sequences differ between student datasets (§§3.1, 3.3–3.4).
- **Preference-transfer evidence:** Test animal and facial-attribute preferences across selected settings involving four VLMs, using biased-minus-control target-selection rates as the transfer metric (§§4.1, 5.1, 5.3).
- **Sensitivity analysis:** Vary carrier-data quantity and decoding strategy; test a restriction to the first language layer. These experiments characterize conditions affecting transfer rather than establish a universal mechanism (§5.2; Appendices E, H).
- **Wording-transfer evidence:** Compare students’ explanations with the biased teacher’s explanations after removing animal names and choice labels, including subsets where all models choose the same image (§§4.3, 5.4).

The parameter-alignment argument offers a possible first-order explanation; the supplied results do not directly establish that mechanism (§§3.2, 3.5–3.6).

## Evidence and main results

Here, Δ denotes the biased student’s target-selection percentage minus the control student’s.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strongest reported animal transfer | Qwen2.5-VL-3B, panda, greedy carrier decoding: control 30.50%, biased 40.42%; Δ = +9.92 percentage points, 95% CI [7.83, 12.08], on the 1,200-panel animal evaluation. | §5.1; Fig. 3; §3.3 |
| Transfer depends on decoding and model | For panda with Qwen2.5-VL-3B, temperature sampling gives Δ = +1.58 points. For dolphin with Qwen2.5-VL-7B, temperature gives +6.00 [4.33, 7.67], versus greedy +3.25 [1.17, 5.33]. | §5.1; Fig. 3 |
| More data can strengthen transfer | Qwen2.5-VL-3B, panda, greedy: Δ rises from +3.17 points with 512 carrier examples to +9.92 with 8,000, increasing at every tested size. Training remains ten epochs, so optimization steps also increase. | §§4.2, 5.2; Fig. 4; Appendix E |
| Facial effects are weaker | Qwen2-VL-2B, temperature sampling, 600 six-way panels per attribute: all five estimates are positive, ranging from +1.33 to +3.50 points; some confidence intervals include zero. | §5.3; Fig. 5; Appendix B.3 |
| Wording similarity persists with image choice controlled | Qwen2.5-VL-3B panda: on 334 panels where all three models choose the same image, token F1 is 8.82 for control versus 14.45 for biased; difference +5.63, 95% paired-bootstrap CI [4.19, 7.09]. All four similarity metrics favor the biased student. | §5.4; Table 1 |
| First language layer is insufficient in one diagnostic | Restricting student adapters to language layer 0 in the Qwen2.5-VL-3B panda condition produces Δ = −2.58 points; both students retain 100% animal recognition accuracy. | Appendix H |

The matched controls support behavioral transfer through differences in numerical supervision under this protocol. Recognition diagnostics also support interpreting animal results as choice changes rather than recognition failures (Appendix D.3). The evidence does not establish universal transfer, a measured parameter-alignment mechanism, or transfer of internal reasoning. Additional scaling conditions are explicitly nonmonotonic (Appendix E).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Preferences are deliberately induced; naturally occurring biases may transfer differently. Results do not establish generality across VLM families, finetuning methods, or multimodal carriers (Appendix I).
- **Author-stated:** Data scaling combines more examples with more optimizer steps, preventing attribution to data quantity alone (Appendix E).
- **Author-stated:** Similar wording does not establish shared reasoning processes (Appendix I).
- **Analyst inference:** Animal evaluation reuses only 60 unique held-out images across 1,200 panels, limiting visual diversity despite varied arrangements and prompts (Appendix B.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Shared initialization and language-layer LoRA leave cross-model transfer and other adaptation regimes unresolved. Face experiments cover selected combinations rather than the full model–decoding grid (§4.1; Appendix B.1).
- **Evidence availability:** Several supplementary tables and plots appear only as captions in the supplied text, preventing inspection of their detailed values.

## Open research questions
1. **Does transfer occur for naturally acquired traits?** The authors induce preferences explicitly. A next experiment could use teachers with independently measured pre-existing preferences and matched controls (Appendix I).
2. **Does additional data help independently of additional optimization?** Current scaling changes both. Compare nested datasets under a fixed optimizer-step budget (Appendix E).
3. **How much does transfer depend on shared initialization and LoRA?** The protocol fixes these choices. Test different student bases and alternative finetuning methods with matched carrier datasets (§3.1; Appendix B.1).
4. **Which numerical features carry the effect?** Similar marginal distributions coexist with different sequences, but causative features remain unidentified. Shuffle sequence positions or match numerical distributions before retraining students (Appendix B.5).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported controlled behavioral effects; **medium** in broader generalization.

Material missing information includes detailed values from caption-only supplementary tables and figures, training-seed replication and variability, complete confidence-interval procedures for animal results, a code-release link, and direct measurements of the proposed parameter alignment.
