# FAME-QP: A GPU-Accelerated Quadratic-Programming Solver for Real-Time Model Predictive Control

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.13766) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13766)


## One-sentence takeaway
FAME-QP combines structure-aware ADMM operators, fused CUDA execution, penalty probing, and lazy matrix updates to reduce dense MPC solve latency in the reported benchmarks while preserving matched random-QP accuracy and comparable simulated control performance.

## Problem and motivation
Linear model predictive control must solve a convex QP within each control period. GPU-native optimization can avoid CPU–GPU transfers, but dense operators, repeated kernel dispatch, and rebuilding precomputed operators can limit its efficiency. The authors identify these costs in existing GPU solvers, particularly ReLU-QP, and target individual dense MPC solves, including problems whose matrices change online (§I–I-A).

## Approach and contributions
FAME-QP retains an existing ADMM formulation and the pre-inversion/penalty-bank approach; its contributions concern operator representation and execution rather than a new optimization algorithm (§II-A, §III).

- **Structure-aware operators:** Separate diagonal constraint rows from dense rows, then use either a stacked operator or a smaller factored representation (§III-A–B).
- **Automatic GPU execution selection:** Combine fused single- or two-kernel iterations with CUDA graph replay; device calibration and problem-specific timing select the operator form and kernel configuration (§III-D–E).
- **Penalty and update management:** Probe cached penalties during setup, tune relative penalties for split constraints, and lazily refresh stale bank entries when matrices change (§III-C, §III-F).
- **Implementation and evaluation:** Provide an author-linked open-source implementation and evaluate numerical QPs, random-system MPC, and simulated humanoid and quadrotor control (§I, §IV).

## Evidence and main results

All results below are author-reported. Experiments use an RTX 3080 Laptop GPU and Intel Core i7-11850H system (§IV).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Faster accuracy-matched random-QP solves | For \(n=220–1400\), float64 FAME-QP is **1.2–25.9×** faster than DAQP and **1.5–4.3×** faster than ReLU-QP. Tolerances are selected to achieve solution \(\ell_\infty\) error ≤\(10^{-3}\) against Clarabel references on five instances per size; setup excluded. | §IV-A; Fig. 1 |
| Lower random-system MPC latency with differing feasibility accuracy | Across 50 closed-loop steps per size, mean step time is **0.658–4.23 ms**, **3.2–7.3×** faster than ReLU-QP and **3.2–13.8×** faster than πMPC. Costs agree across methods within **0.06%**; FAME-QP’s largest returned-input bound violation is ≤\(2.0\times10^{-3}\). Stopping tests differ; setup and initialization excluded. | §IV-A; Table I referenced |
| Larger humanoid horizon within the mean-time budget | In simulated G1 push recovery, mean QP time improves **2.3–3.1×** over ReLU-QP. The largest tested horizon meeting the **2 ms mean-time budget** increases from **10 to 20**; reported CoM RMS deviations across both methods are **18.7–20.5 mm**. | §IV-B; Table II referenced |
| Changing-matrix quadrotor MPC meets the reported tail-latency target | At 50 Hz with \(n=280\), mean time including updates, shifts, and host transfer is **12.5 ± 3.2 ms**, versus **59.2 ± 4.8 ms** for CuPIQP (**4.7×** faster). The 99th percentile is **17.6 vs 72.1 ms**. Position RMSE is **2.7 vs 3.4 mm** in the simulated trajectory. | §IV-B; Fig. 3 |
| Fusion and graph replay reduce execution overhead | At \(n=128\), stacked operators with \(m_c=n/4\), fixed-penalty iterations without termination checks take **140 μs** unfused/eager, **24.6 μs** unfused/graph, and **4.37 μs** fused/graph. | §IV-C; Table III |
| Extracting diagonal constraints reduces storage and iteration time | For \(G=[I;C]\), tested dense-row counts and both operator forms, extraction reduces storage **1.67–4.00×** and iteration time **1.07–3.03×**, with unchanged iteration counts and solutions within \(10^{-12}\). | §IV-C |
| Automatic selection tracks the faster operator form | Selects the faster form at **30/36** tested points; selected execution is at most **9.5%** slower than the faster fixed form. | §IV-C |
| Probing trades setup for faster repeated solves | At tolerance \(10^{-5}\), starting-penalty probing reduces random-QP iterations **1.6–3.2×** and solve time **1.4–3.3×**. A separate full-probe/fusion experiment recovers setup cost after approximately **36–122 solves**. | §IV-C; Table III |
| Lazy refresh reduces changing-matrix overhead | Recorded quadrotor QPs show roughly halved update and median step times relative to refreshing every bank entry, with identical iteration counts and numerically equal solutions. | §IV-C |

The evidence supports lower latency in the tested settings, with ablations separating several contributing mechanisms. Random-QP comparisons explicitly match solution accuracy; MPC comparisons instead assess achieved control quality under unequal stopping rules. Mean-time compliance in the humanoid experiment does not establish a worst-case deadline guarantee.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** The solver targets dense formulations; sparse and horizon-structured scalability is future work (§V). Its penalty bank also imposes a GPU-memory feasibility constraint (§III-E).
- **Author-stated numerical limits:** Float32 residuals may stagnate near \(10^{-6}\), and stalled solves can return `solved_inaccurate` under relaxed tolerances (§III-F).
- **Analyst inference—generalization:** One hardware system and simulated robotic tasks do not establish performance across GPU architectures or physical robots (§IV).
- **Analyst inference—comparison fairness:** MPC stopping tests and warm-start capabilities differ. CUDA MPC is an author reimplementation, while ReLU-QP is excluded from changing-matrix tracking because its native interface lacks updates (§IV–IV-B).
- **Analyst inference—amortization:** Most headline timings exclude setup and initialization. Reported probing break-even results indicate that short solve sequences may benefit less (§IV-A–C).
- **Evidence availability:** Tables I and II appear only as captions in the supplied text, limiting inspection of individual timing entries despite narrative summaries.

## Open research questions

1. **How well does automatic configuration selection transfer across GPUs?** Only one device is evaluated (§IV). Repeat the operator-selection sweep and end-to-end benchmarks across GPUs with different memory and execution characteristics.
2. **Can the solver provide reliable deadline compliance under disturbances?** Humanoid results emphasize means, and quadrotor results report a percentile rather than a worst-case guarantee (§IV-B). Test longer, varied disturbance sequences and record maximum latency, deadline misses, and solver statuses.
3. **When do sparse or horizon-structured formulations outperform dense pre-inversion?** The authors leave these formulations to future work (§V). Sweep horizon length and state dimension while measuring total setup/update cost, solve latency, and memory against structured alternatives.
4. **How robust is setup-time penalty probing to changing operating conditions?** Probing occurs at setup, while online adaptation uses a fixed penalty grid during each solve (§III-C). Test abrupt changes in conditioning and active constraints, comparing fixed setup probing with periodic re-probing.
