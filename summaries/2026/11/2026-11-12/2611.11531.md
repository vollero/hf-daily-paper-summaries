# Probing Transformer Representations Layer by Layer, Module by Module

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.11531) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11531)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across the evaluated vision and language models, choosing both the transformer layer and its internal module improves frozen-feature classification more than selecting depth alone, with simple fixed locations recovering substantial portions of the test-selected oracle gains (§§3.1–3.2).

## Problem and motivation
Linear probing commonly uses the final transformer block’s residual stream, although intermediate layers can yield better downstream features. The authors identify a further gap: depth-based studies usually treat blocks as atomic units, overlooking normalization, attention, feedforward, and residual representations within them. They investigate whether these internal locations improve classification and whether fixed probing rules can avoid exhaustive search (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Joint layer–module evaluation:** Freeze pretrained models, extract eight module outputs per block, average content tokens while masking special tokens, and train logistic-regression classifiers. Compare final-layer residual probing (RC2@100) against test-selected layer-only, module-only, and joint oracles (§2).
- **Practical probing rules:** Derive and evaluate final-layer feedforward activation for vision (**Act@100**) and second pre-normalization output at 60% depth for language (**LN2@60**). These rules emerge from the evaluated models rather than an independently specified selection procedure (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Transfer analysis:** Examine how vision probing gains relate to baseline error, controlled image corruptions, pretraining scale, and downstream finetuning (§3.1; Appendix C.1).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Joint selection improves over depth-only probing | Vision oracle gains reach approximately 10 percentage points (pp) over RC2@100 across 270 model–dataset pairs; language gains reach approximately 4 pp in benchmark-average accuracy across nine models. Joint selection exceeds layer-only selection in the reported comparisons. | §§3.1–3.2; Figs. 3, 7 |
| Fixed vision probing captures useful gains | Across 15 models and 18 datasets, Act@100 improves top-1 accuracy over RC2@100 in 63.7% of 270 pairs. Mean oracle-gain recovery is 41.9%, versus 31.9% for best-layer RC2 and 78.1% for best-layer Act. | §3.1; Fig. 5 |
| Fixed language probing performs strongly | Across nine models and 24 MTEB classification tasks, LN2@60 gains an average 2.44 pp over RC2@100; improves 77.8% of 216 model–task evaluations; and improves task averages in 21 of 24 tasks. | §3.2; Table 2 discussion |
| Language recovery exceeds residual-stream depth search | Mean joint-oracle-gain recovery is 86.0% for LN2@60, 39.3% for best-layer RC2, and 99.8% for best-layer LN2. | §3.2; Fig. 9 |
| Vision gains correlate with empirical transfer difficulty | Dataset-average joint oracle gains correlate with mean baseline probing error: Spearman ρ = 0.87, p < 0.001, with dataset results averaged across 15 models. | §3.1; Fig. 6(a) |
| Controlled corruptions favor earlier representations | Training probes on clean CIFAR10 and testing on five corruption types at severities 1, 3, and 5 yields increasing oracle gains and decreasing optimal depth as severity rises. | §3.1; Figs. 6(b–c) |
| Activation width alone is insufficient to explain gains | On the common 307M-parameter variants, Act@100 generally beats equal-width FC1@100, while FC1@100 underperforms RC2@100 overall. Numerical table cells are absent from the supplied text. | Appendix C.1.7; Table 18 discussion |

These results support improved **linear accessibility of classification information** at particular internal locations. They do not establish improvements in generation, retrieval, or causal understanding of model computations. Oracle gains are explicitly test-selected upper bounds, not deployable selection results (§2).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Evaluation focuses on linear classification probes; other tasks, modalities, and representation-aggregation methods remain future work (§5).
- **Author-stated confounding:** Relationships involving pretraining scale are observational; architecture, training objectives, and pretraining-data quality and diversity vary together (§3.1).
- **Author-stated statistical limit:** Few vision model sizes restrict power when testing size effects (Appendix C.1.6).
- **Analyst inference—selection validity:** Fixed rules were chosen after inspecting these evaluations. Their performance on independently held-out model families and benchmarks remains unestablished (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Analyst inference—baseline dependence:** Conclusions apply to content-token mean pooling and the specified logistic-regression protocols. Alternatives such as special-token pooling or attentive probing were not directly evaluated (§2; §4).
- **Observed exceptions:** Act@100 can substantially hurt performance: ViT-B’s mean accuracy falls from 61.8% to 55.2% across the 18 vision datasets (Table 17). Larger activation features also prevent interpreting “no search” as demonstrated equal memory or classifier cost (§2; Appendix C.1.7).

## Open research questions
1. **Do the fixed rules generalize independently?** Their selection and assessment use the reported evaluation collection. Next experiment: lock both rules and test unseen model families and datasets without retuning.
2. **Do preferred locations change beyond classification?** The authors explicitly restrict their study to classification (§5). Next experiment: compare the same module locations on retrieval or other downstream tasks with task-appropriate metrics.
3. **Can validation-based selection recover oracle gains economically?** The oracle requires test performance (§2), while fixed rules leave gains unrecovered. Next experiment: select depth within Act or LN2 using validation data and report held-out accuracy alongside search cost.
4. **What causes the transfer-difficulty relationship?** Baseline error is an empirical proxy, and cross-family pretraining comparisons are confounded (§3.1). Next experiment: vary pretraining exposure while holding architecture and training objective fixed, then evaluate controlled shifts.
