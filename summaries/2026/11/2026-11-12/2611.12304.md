# Evolving Infra: Agent-Adaptive LLM Serving at Scale

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.12304) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12304)


## One-sentence takeaway
Evolving Infra combines runtime prefill–decode reassignment with branch-aware KV caching, reporting up to 2.73× Dynamo’s throughput on stationary agent workloads while sometimes sacrificing tail time to first token (§6.2).

## Problem and motivation
Agent workloads vary in prefill versus decode demand, and their context histories change through forks, sub-agents, compaction, and tool waits. The authors argue that existing serving systems couple node roles to cached KV and use coarse scheduling or eviction signals, causing expensive role switches, compute imbalance, and unnecessary prefix recomputation (§§1–3).

The identified gap is coordinated adaptation: compute allocation should follow sustained workload composition, while cache management should follow which contexts active branches still reference.

## Approach and contributions
- **Decoupled infrastructure:** Symmetric SGLang-based engines support either phase without restarting. A shared host-memory KV pool permits direct peer access over RDMA, preserving access to cached KV across phase switches (§§4.1–4.2).
- **Workload-based allocation:** Prefill and decode timing models are fitted during online warmup, then their coefficients are frozen. The planner separates cached from uncached tokens and changes the split only when predicted throughput improvements persist across window slices (§§5.1–5.2).
- **Branch-aware caching and routing:** A distributed placement directory tracks active-branch references through context rewrites, completion, and transfers. Eviction prioritizes unreferenced copies; routing minimizes predicted TTFT and overlaps KV fetching with queueing (§§4.3, 5.3; Appendix E).

The authors claim novelty for the integrated agent-adaptive framework (§1); independent priority verification is outside the supplied evidence.

## Evidence and main results
All results below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Higher stationary throughput and lower mean latency | Across nine workload/concurrency settings on 36 GB300 GPUs: 1.10–2.73× Dynamo throughput, 1.15–4.12× lower mean E2E request latency, and 81–95% less lost KV reuse. | §6.2; Figure 6 |
| Tail-latency gains depend on workload | CC and OSWorld achieve 3.25–13.92× lower P95 TTFT than Dynamo; Putnam’s P95 TTFT is higher at 224 and 288 concurrent agents. | §6.2; Figure 6 |
| Faster phase reassignment | Across the nine stationary settings, switches average 6.2 s including draining requests, versus 754 s for Dynamo relaunches; Evolving Infra continues serving running requests during draining. | §6.2 |
| Adaptation helps changing demand | Three-hour Kimi K2.6 run on 36 GB300 GPUs, shifting Putnam → mixed → OSWorld: throughput is 2.14× Dynamo and 1.28× best static SGLang; mean E2E latency is lower by factors of 2.15 and 1.33, respectively. | §§6.1, 6.3; Figure 7 |
| Timing model predicts held-out measurements | GLM-5.2 on four GB300 GPUs: prefill mean error is 4.2% for single requests and 6.3% for batches of 2–8 outside fitted settings. | Figure 5 |
| Data-plane improvements contribute independently of split adaptation | CC, 126 agents, 36 GB300 GPUs, fixed 4P5D and identical routing: asynchronous load-back yields 1.12× upstream SGLang throughput and reduces prefill idle GPU time from 23.6% to 14.4%. | Appendix D.6; Table D.5 |
| Physical scaling preserves low tail latency | CC/GLM-5.2, 10 agents per GPU, scaling from 36 to 256 GB300 GPUs: static SGLang P95 TTFT rises from 142 to 418 s; Evolving Infra stays within 10–14 s. | Appendix D.2; Figure D.1 |
| Open-loop capacity improves, but control can fail | CC/GLM-5.2 on 36 GB300 GPUs: highest tested arrival rate meeting all specified targets is 1.0 agent/min versus 0.5 for static 4P5D. Evolving Infra nevertheless fails at 0.75 because of a harmful split change. | Appendix D.3; Table D.2 |

“Lost KV reuse” measures missed cache hits relative to an unlimited shared cache without phase switches; its reduction is not an equivalent reduction in total computation (Figure 6).

The evidence supports serving-efficiency gains in the tested configurations. It does not establish universal tail-latency improvements, improved agent task success, or that allocation alone explains the gains: engines, decode binding, routing, and cache management also differ.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-reported tradeoff:** Throughput-oriented allocation worsens Putnam P95 TTFT in some settings (§6.2).
- **Author-reported control failure:** An open-loop planner decision causes prolonged queueing and misses latency targets at a lower arrival rate that another run exceeds successfully (Appendix D.3).
- **Author-stated simulation assumption:** The large-cluster simulation charges no KV-fetch time, assuming transfers finish before prefill. **Analyst inference:** This limits conclusions under network contention or short queues (Appendix D.2).
- **Comparison scope:** Arrow is reimplemented; SGLang runtime role switching is excluded because it rejects the speculative decoding used here. Llumnix cannot run on the primary testbed’s Arm CPUs (§§3, 6.1).
- **Attribution and repeatability:** The authors acknowledge that Appendix D.5 does not separate routing from data-plane effects. Several appendix comparisons explicitly run each configuration once, limiting uncertainty assessment (Appendices D.1, D.3, D.5–D.6).
- **Analyst inference:** Replay fixes coding outputs and tool durations, so it cannot establish how serving changes affect live agent trajectories or task success (§6.1).

## Open research questions
1. **Can allocation protect tail latency while preserving throughput?** Putnam regressions and the open-loop failure leave this unresolved. Next experiment: compare the current planner with explicit TTFT constraints under identical arrivals (§6.2; Appendix D.3).
2. **How robust is asynchronous fetching under contention?** Large-scale simulation assumes fully hidden fetches. Next experiment: vary RDMA bandwidth, contention, and queue lengths while measuring exposed transfer time (Appendix D.2).
3. **How much benefit comes from each cache-management mechanism?** Some comparisons combine routing, eviction, and data-plane changes. Next experiment: independently toggle those components using identical engines and splits (Appendix D.5).
4. **When do frozen timing coefficients become stale?** Coefficients freeze after warmup. Next experiment: introduce changing hardware availability or serving configurations and compare frozen versus periodically refitted models (§5.1).
