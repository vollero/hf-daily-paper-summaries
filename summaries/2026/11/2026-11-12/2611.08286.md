# Interactive Visual Gym: Evaluating Visual Interaction Models with Simulated Users in Interactive Worlds

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-12.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.08286) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08286)


## One-sentence takeaway
Interactive Visual Gym evaluates visual assistants through simulated users whose actions respond to guidance, revealing substantial failures in task completion and latency sensitivity, while demonstrating limited, setting-dependent reinforcement-learning gains (§§4.1–4.3; Appendix C).

## Problem and motivation
Offline video replay cannot measure how an assistant’s advice changes a user’s subsequent actions or task outcome. The authors address this gap with executable environments and simulated users who perform tasks, make mistakes, and accept or reject guidance. Unlike agents that directly control an environment, these assistants must influence execution through communication (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Executable environments:** CookSim, a VirtualHome-based household simulator (VHSim), and ScreenSim provide visual observations, semantic actions, and state-based task verification (§3.1).
- **Controlled user simulation:** A persona-conditioned VLM user plans and acts, while a harness inserts progress-triggered mistakes, questions, requests, and plan changes. Assistant latency can advance the environment before guidance arrives (§3.2).
- **Benchmark and evaluation:** IVG-Bench combines 105 task cases with three personas each, yielding 315 scenarios. Metrics cover timely completion, error detection/prevention, and state-grounded interaction quality; a human study assesses metric alignment and simulation fidelity (§3.3; §§4.1–4.2; Table 17).
- **Training demonstration:** GRPO experiments test whether simulated interactions provide useful learning signals, with encouraging CookSim results but no reliable frozen-test improvement in ScreenSim (§4.3; Appendix C).

The evidence establishes useful measurement capabilities and substantial assistant weaknesses **within these simulated settings**. Human preference agreement supports the quality metric, but does not validate every simulated behavior. RL results demonstrate a learning signal rather than robust transfer: CookSim validation shares recipe/layout cells with training, and ScreenSim’s task-disjoint test results are mixed.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-reported simulation mismatch:** Simulated users speak and respond much more often than humans. Action parallelism also matches poorly; scheduled mistakes do not reproduce natural error frequencies, and some observed human errors fall outside the taxonomy (Appendix F).
- **Author-reported RL limitations:** CookSim has no separate frozen test set. Its two evaluations differ in user model, judging coverage, and recall definition, so absolute scores are not directly comparable. Original validation quality uses a small matched subset (Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference—harness dependence:** During injected mistakes, users are explicitly prevented from self-correcting until persuaded. This makes assistant intervention measurable but constrains natural recovery behavior (§3.2; Appendix J.5).
- **Analyst inference—comparison boundaries:** Streaming APIs are polled; VHSim supplies structured spatial aids; measured latency is capped and excludes user-model inference and rendering. Rankings therefore depend on this interface and timing setup (Appendix H).
- **External validity:** Human validation covers browser-based cooking, not physical cooking or the other domains. The authors explicitly caution that simulator performance does not establish real-world safety (Ethics statement).

## Open research questions
1. **Do assistant rankings persist with more realistic users?** Conversation frequency and recovery behavior differ from humans. Compare rankings under a calibrated quieter harness and a matched human study (Appendices F, J).
2. **Does RL generalize beyond familiar task structure?** CookSim validation shares recipe/layout cells, while ScreenSim lacks reliable frozen-test gains. Train and evaluate on disjoint recipes, layouts, user models, and domains (Appendix C).
3. **Can training improve prevention without sacrificing detection?** CookSim recall falls after RL, and the authors suggest selective flagging as a possible cause. Ablate false-flag penalties and prevention rewards while measuring recall, false alarms, success, and quality (§4.3).
4. **How much do latency assumptions determine rankings?** The current evaluation uses capped delays and polled streaming interfaces. Sweep action speeds and delay caps, then compare native turn-taking against polling (Appendix H).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported findings and methodological qualifications; real-world applicability remains unestablished.
