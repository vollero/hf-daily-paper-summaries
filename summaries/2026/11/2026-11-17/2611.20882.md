# Accelerate Bench: Evaluating Agents for Application-Level Program Optimization

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-17.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.20882) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20882)


## One-sentence takeaway

On Accelerate Bench v1.1, all 16 coding agents produced correct submissions on most of the 32 CPU workloads, while their mean correctness-gated runtime savings ranged from 25.5% to 67.4% against the supplied starting programs (§§3–4).

## Problem and motivation

Application speed depends on how algorithms, code paths, build options, and runtime settings work together. A local improvement may save little time across the full workload or violate an output requirement. The authors identify a gap in benchmarks anchored to known human optimization patches. They ask whether agents can discover where to intervene when given a working application, a workload, and a specification of the behavior to preserve (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Workload-defined benchmark.** The authors adapt 32 scientific and supercomputing workloads to bounded single-node CPU tasks. Each specifies permitted edits, required outputs, and timing boundaries. Agents iterate with public tests, and a separate verifier rebuilds and checks the final submission on private workloads (§2, Appendix D).
- **Agent comparison.** The v1.1 evaluation compares 16 model–harness pairings. Score@1 averages the fraction of baseline runtime saved across equally weighted tasks, with zero credit for incorrect, unscorable, or unaccelerated submissions (§§3–4).
- **Analysis of optimization decisions.** The authors annotate final programs and tool-use trajectories from a five-agent v1.0 panel, review a stratified trajectory sample, and replay selected programs to isolate interactions among changes (§5, Appendices I–L).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

Four invalid paged-generation variants had passed the legacy component checks. | §2.3, Appendix E |
| Computation changes were more common than build tuning. | In 476 labeled v1.0 final submissions, 225 used computation or reuse changes and 40 used compiler or build tuning. Categories could overlap. | §5.1, Fig. 4, Appendix I.1 |

| The payoff from a code change depended on another change. | In a three-round QiboTN factorial replay with fresh-process circuit workloads, deferred library loading gave about 1.01× with broader simulator dispatch off and about 6.4× with it on, with state reuse disabled in both comparisons. | Appendix L.2, Table 32 |

The v1.1 results establish verified gains for the tested model–harness pairings under the benchmark’s workloads and scoring rules. Fine rankings remain uncertain because task-bootstrap intervals omit run-to-run and re-judging variation. The controlled replays isolate effects for the selected artifacts and settings. The v1.0 trajectory comparisons show associations and leave the causes of the v1.1 ranking unresolved (Appendices A.3–A.4, C, L).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** The benchmark uses scaled CPU workloads and starting programs with varying degrees of tuning. Some gains exploit structure specific to the prescribed workload. Transfer to other scales, hardware, and production settings requires separate evaluation (§7).
- **Author-stated.** Scores combine model, harness, inference compute, and execution conditions. Only two agents received repeated v1.1 runs. The v1.0 trajectory panel covers five agents and predates changes to verifiers, timing, and the execution environment (§7, Appendices A–B).
- **Analyst inference.** The planned verifier controls support the selected checks on two tasks. Benchmark-wide false acceptance remains unmeasured. Near a correctness tolerance, input variation can also change whether a fixed submission passes, as observed in v1.1 re-judging (Appendices E, A.3).
- **Analyst inference.** Candidate histories favor runs with source-confirmed, comparable correct candidates. Most candidates in those histories were timed once, so some apparent advantages of the fastest public candidate may reflect measurement noise (Appendices J.5, L.3).

## Open research questions

1. **How much of the v1.1 score gap comes from the harness?** The reported comparisons pair models with particular harnesses (§7). Run the same compatible models in both harnesses under matched task budgets, with repeated trials for each pairing.
2. **Which gains persist at larger scales or on different CPUs?** The benchmark scales workloads down, and one HPCG-derived replay changed both the allocation and input seed, leaving their individual effects unresolved (§2.2, Appendix L.3). Replay frozen submissions while varying workload size, seed, and hardware independently.
3. **Does repeated public timing improve final candidate selection?** Most candidates in the analyzed histories were timed once, and the fastest public candidate did not consistently beat the final submission in private HPCG-derived replays (Appendices J.5, L.3). Compare single-timing selection with repeated interleaved timing under the same agent budget, then score both policies on private inputs.
