# ViRA: Toward Ultra-High-Resolution Visual Agents via Cross-Input Relative Policy Optimization

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-17.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.21605) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21605)


## One-sentence takeaway

CrossRPO trains zoom-capable visual agents to answer correctly across images whose local evidence changes, reaching 63.3% set accuracy on ViRA-Bench at maximum resolution versus 59.3% for per-version GRPO in a matched Qwen3.5-9B experiment (§§1, 5.3, Table 3).

## Problem and motivation

Downsampling ultra-high-resolution photos can make the small detail needed to answer a question unreadable. A zoom tool can recover that detail, but agents may still default to a plausible answer, even after cropping the right region. The authors identify a credit-assignment gap: a correct guess and a correct reading earn the same final-answer reward on one image, while rewarding a crop can credit evidence that the agent never uses (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Cross-input policy optimization.** Each version is presented independently. CrossRPO compares a rollout with other rollouts of the same version using a leave-one-out baseline, then increases its weight as correctness on the other versions rises. The objective combines per-version accuracy with joint correctness. For larger sets, it uses correctness on triples to avoid a vanishing full-set signal (§§3.2–3.3, Fig. 2).
- **Credit-assignment guarantees.** Under independent sampling, the authors show that the unscaled gradient estimate divided by the number of rollouts per version is unbiased. A deterministic same-answer shortcut yields zero advantage when correctness is constant within each version (§§3.3–3.4, Appendix A.1).
- **Datasets and agents.** The authors build ViRA-Train and ViRA-Bench using local image edits, construct ViRA-Bench-Real through physical object swaps, and train ViRA-9B and ViRA-27B. They also test the objective on counterfactual text passages (§§4, 5.1, 5.4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| CrossRPO leads the matched maximum-resolution comparison | On ViRA-Bench at maximum resolution, Qwen3.5-9B variants reach **63.3% set accuracy** with CrossRPO, **59.3%** with per-version GRPO, **52.6%** with pooled GRPO, and **58.7%** with per-version RLOO without the joint term. Results are means over three evaluations with matched training data, reward, and step count. | §§1, 5.1, 5.3, Tables 3 and 17 |
| Correct grouping matters | Removing the joint term lowers ViRA-Bench set accuracy by **4.6–7.4 percentage points** across tested budgets. Computing the weight from unrelated questions brings performance close to the no-joint-term run. | §5.3, Table 3 |
| The gain depends on pixel budget and crop behavior | Per-version GRPO leads CrossRPO in set accuracy at **0.5 and 1 MP** on ViRA-Bench and through **4 MP** on ViRA-Bench-Real. The crop analysis associates the photographed-set gap with tighter crops and more zooms. | §5.3, Appendix G.2 |
| The trained agents are competitive across the suite | The authors rank ViRA-9B first among tested open models in its size group on **10 of 10** benchmarks and ViRA-27B on **9 of 10**, using set accuracy for the contrast benchmarks and single-image accuracy for the general benchmarks. | §§5.1–5.2, Tables 1–2 |
| Text transfer shows a modest gain over matched training | On three-version ConFiQA with Qwen3.5-9B, mean test set accuracy across three training runs is **81.9%** for CrossRPO, **81.0%** without the joint term, **79.6%** for pooled GRPO, **80.2%** for pooled RLOO, and **58.6%** for the base model. On substituted NQ-Swap passages and FaithEval, the four trained methods are within **2.0 percentage points** of one another in accuracy. | §5.4, Table 4 |

The matched ablations support a benefit from the joint term on ViRA-Bench. At 1 MP, the authors report similar last-crop object coverage after removing the term, alongside a larger accuracy drop on edited variants than on originals (§5.3, Appendix H). This is consistent with better use of visible evidence, although crop coverage alone cannot identify which pixels drove an answer. The low-budget reversals and close text-transfer results limit claims of a general advantage.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ limitations.** Editing traces within an object may influence answers, and the first-zoom coverage check addresses search behavior only. The photographed benchmark is small, indoor, and restricted in object variety. Questions cue the object’s location, and larger sets are tested only on text (Limitations, §4).
- **Authors’ limitations.** Contrast sets cost more to construct than single images. Accuracy-only rewards can yield broad crops that leave tiny objects hard to read. Only the Qwen family is trained, and text training is small and uses one dataset (Limitations, §5.4).
- **Analyst inference.** Model rankings reflect complete evaluation configurations. Differences in crop magnification, prompts, turn limits, and model-specific maximum resolutions make those rankings insufficient to isolate the training objective. The matched Qwen3.5-9B ablations provide a cleaner comparison (§5.1, Appendix E, Appendix G.1).

## Open research questions

1. **Does the gain hold when agents must find the object without a location cue?** The ViRA questions describe where to look. Evaluate the same photographed scenes with and without that cue, measuring crop coverage and set accuracy separately (§4, Limitations).
2. **Can tight, iterative crops close the low-budget gap?** The crop-size intervention shows that object scale matters, while the training reward scores final-answer correctness. Compare accuracy-only CrossRPO with a crop-size-aware reward at matched token budgets on photographed sets (Appendix G.2, Limitations).
3. **Does the joint term help across architecture families?** The visual training and matched ablations use Qwen models. Repeat CrossRPO, per-version RLOO, and per-version GRPO on another vision-language backbone with the same data, tool, and rollout budget (§§5.1, 5.3, Limitations).
