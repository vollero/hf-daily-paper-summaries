# SPILLWAY: Separating Target Admission from Sanitization to Defend Tool-Using Agents against Prompt Injection

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-17.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.07222) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07222)


## One-sentence takeaway

SPILLWAY combines a deterministic tool-target check with rule-based sanitization and limited model review, recording zero executed redirections under the standard AgentDojo attack across all 12 backbone–suite combinations and the highest mean task success under attack among the evaluated defenses (Tables 1–2).

## Problem and motivation

Tool outputs can contain instructions that hijack an agent into sending email, transferring money, or granting access with the user’s authority. The authors identify a gap between defenses that rely on model judgments at runtime and provenance defenses that require a model to plan or route each task. They ask whether typed tool returns and the user’s request can determine where an action may go without per-task routing (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Deterministic target admission.** SPILLWAY builds an identity index from qualifying read-only returns before serialization. For a target that appears in tool output but is absent from the user’s request, the lookup requires a typed identity match and refuses values repeated in untrusted free text. Fixed delegation rules can authorize additional targets, and grants and destructive actions receive separate checks (§3.1, Algorithm 1).
- **Evidence-linked sanitization and recovery.** The sanitizer removes rule-matched instruction-like regions before the agent reads them and retains the removed spans. Eligible refusals go to a local judge that must approve both the route and the requested action and content. A target in a removed span cannot reach the judge. The authors formalize this bound and identify deterministic delegation as a separate admission path (§§3.2–3.4, Figure 2).
- **Comparison with routed-origin enforcement.** The authors compare SPILLWAY against ROPE on tasks that often require destinations inferred from tool returns, reporting safety and task utility under both ROPE router configurations (Figure 4, Table 8).

## Evidence and main results

BU is benign task success, UAU is task success under attack, and ASR is attack success rate. The paper’s raw ASR counts executed effects. In-scope ASR also excludes task-named target collisions and content-only manipulation (§4, Appendix A).

The standard-grid results show that SPILLWAY can preserve task utility while blocking the tested redirections. The ablation shows a benefit from sanitization in that setting. The isolated span veto prevented no observed attack because the sanitized agent proposed no removed target. The zero-hit results leave broader effectiveness against adaptive attacks and content manipulation unresolved, and the latency figures describe one serving setup (§§5, 7, Appendix B).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope.** The formal guarantee covers identity targets under the stated matching and trust assumptions. Targets absent verbatim from tool output, attacker-controlled typed identity fields, injected content sent to an admitted destination, and manipulation of final answers fall outside that guarantee. New tool sets require role declarations (§§2, 3.4, 7).
- **Author-stated residual risk.** Delegation and judge recovery can still admit attacker-written targets. AutoDojo exposed failures through recovery, and the attacker-as-sender evaluation omitted instructions that identify the sender only by role (§§5, 7, Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference.** Open-loop replay measures robustness to fixed attack texts and leaves defense-aware adaptation unmeasured. AutoDojo’s development-set zero has limited value as a generalization estimate because failures from that set informed the rules (§5, Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference.** The delegated-routing comparison has one run per suite, and AgentDyn has no benign runs. Its composites substitute under-attack utility for benign utility, limiting direct comparison with the breadth-grid composites (Tables 2 and 8).

## Open research questions

1. **How well does SPILLWAY hold up when attack inputs adapt to the deployed defense?** The strongest reported PISmith comparison used fixed replay. A prospective evaluation could freeze the defense, run an authorized adaptive benchmark assessment, and independently score executed effects (§5, Table 7).
2. **When does the span veto independently prevent harmful actions?** Removing it changed no attack outcomes in the reported ablation. A controlled call-level test could present the same refused proposals to both configurations and measure harmful admissions and legitimate recoveries (§5, Table 3).
3. **How reliably do role declarations transfer to unseen tool schemas?** The authors added AgentDyn roles, and broader integration effort remains unmeasured. A held-out integration study could have independent implementers label new schemas, then measure annotation agreement, setup effort, false refusals, and executed effects (§7, Appendix B).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported findings within the specified benchmark settings. The evaluation leaves several material quantities unavailable.

- Executed-effect ASR against attacks adapted to the deployed SPILLWAY configuration.
- Benign AgentDyn utility and repeated-run uncertainty for the full delegated-routing comparison.
- Performance when attacker-controlled typed identities are referred to only by role.
- Integration effort and role-labeling reliability across unseen tool schemas.
