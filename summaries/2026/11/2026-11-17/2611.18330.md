# Marimba: A System One Model for Speech

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-17.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.18330) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18330)


## One-sentence takeaway

Marimba reuses a frozen speech encoder to answer typed questions about short audio windows, with strong results on several evaluated tasks but weaker turn-end discrimination than Smart Turn on its own test and chance-level transfer to unseen scene classification.

## Problem and motivation

Speech applications often need several decisions about the same audio window, such as whether a term was spoken, whether a turn ended, or which language was used. The author identifies a gap between audio language models that generate an answer for each prompt, transcript pipelines that discard alternative recognition hypotheses, and single-task tools that lack a shared interface. Typed probabilities can support review or deferral, though their usefulness for those policies requires a separate evaluation. (§§1–2, 6.3)

## Approach and contributions

- **Shared interface.** Marimba encodes each eight-second window once with frozen Qwen3-ASR 0.6B. It returns typed choices, yes/no answers, or scores. A question-conditioned cross-attention head handles voice activity, dedicated layer-18 and layer-12 readouts handle turn-end and speaker decisions, and native ASR language scores provide language identification. (§§3.1–3.3, 5)
- **Lexical scoring and task suite.** Term scoring counts exact normalized matches across the greedy transcript and four beam hypotheses, then applies Platt calibration to binary scores. The task suite includes held-out terms and real medication-confusion negatives. Personal-information detection uses an external text detector on the greedy transcript and returns a binary answer. (§§3.3–3.4, 5.2)
- **Evaluation across tasks and sources.** The paper combines discrimination, calibration, and accuracy-targeted coverage in one evaluation, with source-specific breakdowns and matched comparisons where available. The author identifies the breadth and integration of the suite as contributions and credits prior work for typed, calibrated speech decisions. (§§1–2, 6)

The results establish task-specific performance under the reported settings. The ICSI interval leaves both a turn-end advantage and a disadvantage plausible, and next-speaker labels measure a different outcome from semantic completion. The paper’s coverage metric uses test-set confidence curves, so it cannot validate an abstention threshold fixed before deployment. (§§6–6.3, Appendix A.4)

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Exact term scoring inherits ASR misses and confusions, including rare medication names. Private lexical results are corpus-specific, and the personal-information experiment uses templated synthetic Portuguese speech and a private detector. The private recordings are unreleased. The author reports that the study had no research ethics committee review or study-specific patient consent. (§§4.2, 6–7)
- **Author-stated:** Speaker decisions are evaluated per window on AMI and VoxConverse and undercount windows with three or more speakers. The ICSI subset includes only meetings the author could download. Calibration under deployment shift and incremental streaming remain unvalidated. (§§3.2, 6–7)
- **Analyst inference:** The supplied text gives no greedy-only versus multi-hypothesis term-scoring result, leaving the ranking, calibration, and latency contribution of beam hypotheses unclear. The audio LLM turn-end comparison also mixes a trained in-distribution readout with zero-shot baselines, so it cannot isolate an architectural advantage. (§§5.2, 6.1, Appendix A.5)

## Open research questions

- **How much do beam hypotheses help on rare terms?** The paper reports the combined scorer. Compare greedy-only and multi-hypothesis scoring on the same held-out terms and confusion pairs, measuring AUROC, ECE, and latency. (§5.2, Table 1)
- **Does the turn-end readout detect semantic completion across domains?** ICSI labels describe speaker succession. Annotate held-out meetings for both semantic completion and next-speaker identity, then compare frozen models on each label. (§§6–7)
- **Can lexical confidence support a fixed review threshold under corpus shift?** Reported coverage is derived from test-set curves. Fix the threshold on a development corpus, then measure accuracy and coverage prospectively on a separate conversational corpus. (§§6.2–6.3, Appendix A.4)

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported metrics and comparisons in their stated settings. The supplied text does not provide access to the private recordings or PII detector, a quantitative greedy-only lexical ablation, complete quantitative LoRA results, or enough artifact details to verify availability of Marimba’s code and weights. It also provides no prospective evaluation of fixed review thresholds or personal-information detection on natural conversational audio.
