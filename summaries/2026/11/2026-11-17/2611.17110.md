# SALA: Semantically Aligned Latent Actions Enable Dual-Axis Scaling of Embodied Foundation Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-17.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.17110) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17110)


## One-sentence takeaway

SALA uses atomic-action descriptions to align unpaired human and robot videos in a control-grounded latent space, and the authors report that more general human video lowered held-out robot action error while more task-specific human video improved real-robot success (§§2.2–2.4, 3.3).

## Problem and motivation

Robot teleoperation is costly to scale, while egocentric human video offers diverse manipulation experience without robot action labels. The authors identify a gap in reconstruction-only latent action models: a code can explain a visual transition by retaining scene information even when it fails to relate human and robot executions of the same action (§§1, 2.1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Robot-anchored semantic alignment.** A frozen text encoder embeds atomic-action descriptions from independently collected clips. For each human clip, the similarities define a soft distribution over robot clips, which supervises its latent neighborhood. Both streams train visual feature reconstruction. Robot state and action chunks additionally train an action decoder, and the alignment loss stops gradients through the robot latents (§§2.2–2.3, Fig. 2a).
- **Soft latent targets for a shared policy.** A frozen SoftVQ tokenizer labels clips from their endpoint frames. The policy learns to predict those codebook distributions on both streams, and its flow-matching action generator attends to the latent queries. Robot examples also supply direct action supervision (§2.4, Fig. 2b).
- **Two uses of human video.** The same targets support general human-video pre-training before robot mid-training and task-specific human-video post-training alongside teleoperated demonstrations. The authors vary the video budget at each stage separately (§§2.4, 3.3).

The simulation ablations support a contribution from semantic alignment and one-way anchoring in that benchmark. The physical experiments show two distinct trends: general-video scaling was evaluated by offline prediction error, and task-video scaling was evaluated by robot rollouts. Physical success across general-video budgets remains unmeasured. Retrieval on tasks absent from the robot data was scored by description similarity, leaving execution of those tasks untested (§6).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ stated limitations.** Tokenizer training requires atomic-action descriptions. General-video scaling was tested through 2,678 equivalent hours, and the authors leave larger budgets open (§5, Table 1).
- **Analyst inference — generalization.** The simulation uses robot-to-robot transfer, and the physical control results cover four tasks on one platform. The claimed transfer benefits have limited direct evidence across other physical embodiments and settings (§§3.2–3.3, 11–12).
- **Analyst inference — attribution.** The real-world vanilla VLA comparison combines the effect of latent-action supervision with access to human videos, while OpenWAM-α and SALA have different pre-training histories. The reported alignment and stop-gradient control ablations are in simulation, and the hard-VQ comparison reports tokenizer losses (§§3.3–3.4, Table 5, Fig. 7).
- **Analyst inference — precision.** Table 4 provides trial counts but no uncertainty intervals or variation across training runs. The supplied results also lack a quantified cost comparison for the authors’ claim of lower training cost versus OpenWAM-α (§3.3).

## Open research questions

1. **Does more general human video improve physical task success?** Figure 4a gives only offline L1 across budgets. Evaluate physical rollouts for each pre-training budget, extend the range, and hold robot data and task post-training fixed.
2. **How much annotation quality and coverage does alignment need?** The tokenizer uses scripted labels in simulation and VLM annotations in the real world, with no reported sensitivity study (§§9–10). Vary annotation coverage and noise on a fixed video pool, then measure retrieval and robot rollout success.
3. **Can task-specific human video substitute for some teleoperation?** Figure 4b varies human demonstrations at a fixed robot budget and starts at 10% human data. Cross several teleoperation budgets with human-video budgets that include a zero-human condition, then compare physical success.

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the method and tabulated comparisons. The supplied text lacks the exact L1 values and relative reductions in Fig. 4a, the exact success rate at the 50% condition in Fig. 4b, repeated-run uncertainty, and quantified training cost for the OpenWAM-α comparison. It also does not establish availability of SALA code, checkpoints, or the real-world datasets.
