# LoopFill: Pipelined Multi-Token Speculative Decoding for Looped Transformers

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-17.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.19649) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19649)


## One-sentence takeaway

LoopFill combines suffix retrieval and shallow-loop predictions in a training-free pipeline that preserves full-depth decoding and delivers 5.13–5.85× mean greedy decoding speedups over autoregressive decoding across three model checkpoints and seven benchmarks (§5.1, Tables 2 and 8).

## Problem and motivation

Looped language models reuse Transformer layers to save parameters, but each output token requires multiple sequential passes through the shared block. Shallow self-speculation overlaps work across tokens, yet each prediction proposes only the next token and must wait for a shallow loop to finish. Wrong drafts can also force restarts. LoopAhead fills more of the pipeline by drafting several tokens at once with a trained drafter. The authors ask whether suffix retrieval can provide that lookahead without additional training (§§1–2, 5.1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Two drafters extend the same tree.** Suffix trees retrieve multi-token continuations from the current prompt and generated text, or from completed requests. Shallow-loop predictions add candidates from the target model. Either drafter can extend the other’s branch, and overlapping candidates share a node (§3, Fig. 3).
- **One node budget governs scheduling and reuse.** LoopFill ranks paths using suffix frequencies and shallow model probabilities, then batches the highest-ranked nodes across requests and loop depths. Full-depth verification selects the emitted token and preserves computation on its matching branch, if present (§3, Eqs. 2–5).
- **The analysis quantifies when the drafters complement each other.** Under immediate scheduling for a single request, the relevant quantity is how often shallow predictions hit when suffix retrieval misses. The authors also explain how pruning and concurrent requests can erode that gain (§4, Eqs. 7–9).

## Evidence and main results

The reported experiments used a single NVIDIA L20X GPU in BF16 (§5, §A.1).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Greedy decoding speedup | Single request, seven-benchmark arithmetic mean versus autoregressive decoding (AR). LoopFill reaches **5.85×** on Ouro-2.6B and **5.13×** on Huginn-3.5B, versus **4.19×** and **4.12×** for LoopAhead. Ouro-1.4B reaches **5.47×** versus AR. | §5.1, Tables 2 and 8 |
| Speedup with sampling | At temperature 1.0, single-request means over the same benchmarks are **3.50×** on Ouro-2.6B and **2.60×** on Huginn-3.5B versus AR, compared with **3.25×** and **2.37×** for LoopAhead. Ouro-1.4B reaches **3.21×** versus AR. | §5.1, Tables 2 and 8 |
| End-to-end throughput | On SpecBench at temperature 1.0 and concurrency 16, throughput including prefill is **189.56 tokens/s (2.81× AR)** on Ouro-2.6B, **129.18 (2.67×)** on Huginn-3.5B, and **339.28 (2.50×)** on Ouro-1.4B. Each ratio uses AR at the same concurrency. | §5.1, Tables 3 and 7, §A.4 |
| Shallow drafts help suffix retrieval | On Ouro-2.6B, mean correct draft length rises from **1.34 tokens** with suffix-only drafting to **1.73** after adding the first shallow draft and **2.28** when suffix retrieval can also extend that draft. | §1, Fig. 1b |
| Larger draft budgets trade latency for throughput | Raising the per-request node budget from 16 to 64 on Huginn-3.5B increases single-request decode rate by **19.5%** and reduces end-to-end throughput at concurrency 16 by **43%**, both relative to the 16-node budget. | §§1, 5.2, Fig. 5 |

The results support faster decoding and higher serving throughput for the tested workloads and engine. Equation 5 supports the authors’ output-preservation claim because the full-depth model determines each emitted token in both greedy decoding and sampling. Empirical output-parity checks and performance on other hardware are unreported in the supplied text.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ limitations.** Suffix drafts match less often when outputs are harder to predict, and extra draft nodes can consume capacity needed by concurrent requests (§§5.2, 7, Fig. 11). The theoretical advantage assumes immediate execution of drafts for a single request. With a finite budget, a suffix candidate can displace a correct shallow candidate (§4).
- **Analyst inference.** The best node budget and the size of the throughput gains may depend on the GPU and serving engine. The two evaluated model families share one hardware and precision setup, and the throughput comparison uses SpecBench (§5, §A.1).
- **Analyst inference.** Because the global suffix tree indexes completed requests, request order and index warm-up may affect results. The supplied text describes local and global retrieval but gives no separate cold-start or local-only serving comparison (§3, §5.2).

## Open research questions

1. **Can the server adjust the draft budget as load changes?** The fixed-budget ablation shows opposite effects at low and high concurrency (§5.2). Replay a workload with changing arrival rates and compare fixed and adaptive budgets on tail decode latency and end-to-end throughput.
2. **How much does retrieval benefit from the global suffix tree?** The method searches both trees, while the reported results leave their individual contributions open (§3, §5.2). Compare local-only retrieval, an initially empty global index, and a warmed global index under multiple request orders.
3. **How portable are the throughput gains?** All reported runs use one GPU type and precision (§A.1). Repeat the concurrency and budget sweeps on another accelerator, measuring tokens/s and loop passes per emitted token to see whether the best budget shifts.

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the described algorithm and headline results explicitly reported in the prose. The supplied text omits full result cells for Tables 2–3 and 7–8, evaluation-set sizes from Table 4, and training settings referenced in Table 6. Run-to-run variability, empirical output-parity checks, global-index initialization and request ordering, and implementation release status are also unavailable.
