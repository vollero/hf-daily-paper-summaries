# Centered Ablation: Why Last-Layer Directional Ablation Under-Fires or Collapses, and a Closed-Form Fix

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-17.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.20148) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20148)


## One-sentence takeaway

Centered Ablation removes a shared benign component from the last-layer steering direction and, in the authors’ greedy-decoding tests, increased fluent-only attack success on two under-firing Llama models, sharply reduced repetition on Gemma-2-9B, and preserved more benign answers on high-overlap models (§§3.4, 4.3).

## Problem and motivation

Standard directional ablation removes the component of a model’s hidden state along a direction estimated by contrasting two prompt classes. The same intervention can leave refusals largely intact on one model, rewrite benign answers on another, or drive generation into repetition. The authors identify a gap in prior work on refusal steering and affine correction: behavioral outcomes alone do not predict which failure will occur before generation. They trace the failures to overlap between the steering direction and a shared component of benign last-layer states (§§1–3.3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Exact last-layer analysis.** With a final RMSNorm and linear unembedding, rank-1 ablation adds a fixed vocabulary-level bias scaled by the cosine between the state and the ablation direction, followed by a positive rescaling that preserves the greedy token ranking. This decomposition explains why a direction aligned with the benign mean can perturb ordinary text (§§3.2–3.3, Appendix B).
- **Closed-form correction and affine comparison.** Centered Ablation (CA) projects the benign common mode out of the difference-in-means (DIM) direction. The last-layer affine baseline, DIM+offset, centers the raw projection coefficient while retaining the original direction and its sensitivity to variation along the common mode. The authors derive the resulting trade-off and test it at a shared intervention site (§§3.4–3.5, 4.4).
- **Diagnostics and evaluation.** The authors use common-mode overlap, simulated first-token changes, and bias size relative to logit spread to characterize faithfulness gains, attack-success gains, and collapse. They also show that two safety judges assign nearly opposite labels to the same repetitive outputs (§§3.6, 4.5, 4.7).

The experiments support a selective benefit within the tested setting. CA improved different outcomes on different models, while the affine offset sometimes achieved higher ASR (§4.4). The closed form describes a single decoding step under its assumptions. Sequence-level refusal can persist after the opening changes, as the Gemma-2-9B deflections illustrate (§4.6). Likewise, small changes on constrained capability benchmarks leave open the effect on broader free-form capability (§4.7, Table 13).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ stated scope.** The greedy-token result applies to rank-1 ablation after the final decoder block. Sampling makes the logit rescaling relevant, and earlier-layer interventions propagate through attention. The affine comparison uses ACE’s offset at the final layer. Appendix B also sets the RMSNorm constant to zero in the displayed identity (§5, Appendix B.1).
- **Authors’ stated sample limit.** The diagnostic correlations use eight models, with only two below the efficacy split and only one model-condition combination above the proposed mean collapse threshold. Appendix F also reports partial collapse below that threshold in a strength sweep, limiting its use as a general cutoff (§5, Appendix F, Table 21).
- **Analyst inference.** String similarity captures textual change and can count a good paraphrase as a rewrite. The separate quality check helps, but its high tie rates limit what it establishes about free-form quality. Similarly, the compression rule measures a specific form of degeneracy (§4.1, Tables 12 and 14).
- **Analyst inference.** Later semantic deflection cannot by itself explain a mismatch on the first emitted token. The source reports both observations for Gemma-2-9B, leaving the cause of the first-token discrepancy unresolved (§§4.2, 4.6).

## Open research questions

1. **Do the diagnostic relationships generalize to new model families?** The correlations come from eight models and a small post-pilot check. A prospective test could lock the prediction rules and scoring criteria, then measure faithfulness and collapse on additional families (§§4.5, 5).
2. **Can a prompt-level statistic predict partial collapse?** The mean index missed the onset seen in the strength sweep. A follow-up could compare the mean, upper quantiles, and the fraction of prompts above a fixed index against per-prompt collapse on held-out data (Appendix F, Table 21).
3. **How much of the emotion result depends on surface cues and judge errors?** The study used synthetic stories, and the emotion judge made systematic confusions. A follow-up could vary whether emotion is conveyed through dialogue, narration, or events and use blinded human ratings alongside the automatic judge (Appendix H).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported main comparisons and **medium** for the diagnostic and mechanistic generalizations. The supplied text refers to released code without giving a repository location or revision. It also leaves the first-token mismatch on Gemma-2-9B unresolved and does not explain how the “collapse-fix” label was credited in the reported 4-of-5 score. Table 13 supplies GSM8K results only for the three pilot models, so all-eight-model GSM8K outcomes are unavailable.
