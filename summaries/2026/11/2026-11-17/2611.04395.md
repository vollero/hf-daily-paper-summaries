# Correct Labels Are Not Enough: Reliable Metadata Can Silently Control Model Behavior

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-17.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.04395) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04395)


## One-sentence takeaway

On CommonsenseQA, models fine-tuned with correct labels and a perfectly predictive annotation selected the annotated wrong option on 97–100% of items when the cue was moved, while retaining 72–78% average accuracy when it was removed (Abstract, §4, Table 1).

## Problem and motivation

Visible scores or quality labels can predict the training answer so reliably that solving the question and following the metadata yield identical supervised targets. The authors ask whether that dependence can develop with every target correct and remain hidden in evaluation that strips the metadata (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Controlled demonstration.** The authors use LoRA to fine-tune Qwen3, Gemma, and Llama on CommonsenseQA, comparing cued training with annotation-free supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Paired evaluations remove, align, or move the cue while holding the question, options, and gold answer fixed (§§3–4).
- **Conditions for dependence.** Reliability sweeps vary how often the cue marks gold while preserving correct targets. Separate probes test training words, inference wording, format transfer, and competing tags (§§4–6, 8–9).
- **Preference-tuning extension and audit.** A Qwen3 direct preference optimization (DPO) experiment measures sensitivity in next-token option scores. The proposed audit combines paired metadata perturbations with answer accuracy, cue-following, and parseability, with option scores as an additional readout when available (§§7, 10, Appendix N).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| A perfectly reliable cue can dominate generated answers. | Across three CommonsenseQA model families, the misleading Reward cue was followed on 97–100% of items, versus 12–16% for the displayed annotation-free SFT comparison. Cue-free accuracy averaged 72–78% across the four cued adapters within each family. | Abstract, §4, Table 1 |
| Reward meaning is unnecessary during training, while wording matters at inference. | Separate nonsense-word adapters reproduced near-ceiling cue-following across three seeds. For a fixed Reward-trained Qwen3 adapter on 300 items, changing the misleading cue word reduced generation cue-following from 97.0% with Reward to 80.7% with Florp and 23.3% with Cost. | §§4–5, Fig. 1A, Appendix C |
| Dependence rises with reliability, with different cue-free outcomes across tasks. | In the rank-32 multiple-choice GSM8K sweep, cue-free accuracy across the three core families fell from 81–87% at training reliability \(p=0.5\) to 32–41% at \(p=1\). On ARC-Challenge, rank-32 Qwen3 at \(p=1\) showed high misleading-cue following and 86.0% ± 1.2 percentage points cue-free accuracy (mean ± sample SD, three seeds, 500 test items). | §6, Fig. 2, Table 2, Appendices A, L |
| Moving the cue changes immediate option rankings. | On 294 paired four-option CommonsenseQA items, the marked wrong option ranked first on every misleading-cue item for Qwen3 and Gemma. Gold-to-marked-wrong flips occurred on 79.3% and 81.3% of all paired items, respectively. | §4, Appendix J |
| DPO also yields option-score sensitivity. | On 300 misleading CommonsenseQA items, Qwen3 marked-wrong capture was 80.9% ± 5.1 at \(\beta=0.05\) and 61.9% ± 2.2 at \(\beta=0.1\) (mean ± sample SD, three seeds). At \(\beta=0.5\), the score readout always chose A. The short generation probe ended before final answers. | §7, Table 3, Appendix K |
| The free-form probe found no cued-SFT flags. | One judge and rubric flagged zero scored responses across six cued-SFT cells and four control outputs. Each cell generated 800 outputs before scoring exclusions. | §9, Appendix I, Table 11 |

The paired interventions establish that changing annotation placement can change answer selection on fixed questions. The DPO result is confined to immediate option scores, and the free-form null is confined to the tested prompts, judge, and rubric. The experiments leave the internal mechanism and the prevalence of similar dependence in deployment unresolved (§§1, 9, 11).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** The main evaluations use synthetic cues and discrete answer selection. Real metadata pipelines, learned-reward reinforcement learning, and frontier-scale post-training remain untested. Several auxiliary probes use one training seed, and the earlier reward-over-correctness finding was withdrawn after failed reproduction (§11, Appendix B).
- **Author-stated.** The CSQA and GSM8K recipes differ in template, optimization, and sequence length, so the cause of the GSM8K cue-free decline is unresolved. Base-model generation cue-following on CSQA is unreported, leaving open whether unannotated SFT suppresses pre-existing sensitivity (§11, Appendix A).
- **Analyst inference.** The DPO–SFT comparison cannot isolate the training objective because learning rates differ. The constant-A readout at \(\beta=0.5\) also leaves completed-answer sensitivity at that setting unresolved (§7, Appendix K).

## Open research questions

1. **What causes the GSM8K cue-free accuracy drop?** Task and training recipe vary together. A factorial experiment could cross both datasets with matched cue templates and optimization settings (§6, Appendix A).
2. **Does the DPO score effect persist in completed answers?** The generation budget ended inside reasoning preambles. A longer-budget evaluation could measure final-answer cue-following, cue-free accuracy, and parseability across the tested \(\beta\) values (Appendix K).
3. **Does cued SFT amplify an existing tendency to follow metadata?** The CSQA base-model generation control is missing, and the Alpaca-only adapter already sometimes follows the cue. Evaluate base, annotation-free SFT, and cued-SFT models on identical paired generation prompts (§11, Appendix E).
4. **Does absolute annotated count or annotated fraction drive dependence in mixtures?** The reported mixtures do not vary these independently. Cross total dataset size with annotated count and use a shared held-out evaluation cohort (§8, Appendix E).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the core behavioral finding in the tested settings. Material gaps include full numeric cells for some captioned tables, completed DPO answers and cue-free accuracy across the \(\beta\) sweep, measured DPO divergence from the reference policy, CSQA base-model generation cue-following, and measurements from real metadata pipelines. Public release of code or checkpoints is also unconfirmed in the supplied text.
