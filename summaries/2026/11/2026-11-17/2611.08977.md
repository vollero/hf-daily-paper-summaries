# Energy-Based Reasoners: Your Recurrent Reasoner Should Be an Energy-Based Model

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-17.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.08977) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08977)


## One-sentence takeaway

Energy-Based Reasoners use one learned latent energy to guide refinement, stopping, and candidate selection, reaching 99.9% exact accuracy with five samples on Sudoku-Extreme and improving 20-sample solution coverage on the tested N-Queens and Graph Coloring settings (Tables 1–2).

## Problem and motivation

Looped reasoners repeatedly refine a latent state, which lets them spend more computation on a problem without adding parameters. The authors identify a gap in how that computation is guided. Existing stopping and selection mechanisms can judge when to halt or which candidate to return, while the recurrent update itself lacks an explicit objective for assessing progress. Training every trajectory against the same target can also suppress alternative valid solutions (§§1–2, 3.2).

## Approach and contributions

1. **One energy for refinement, stopping, and selection.** An input-conditioned transformer produces a scalar energy over a single latent state. Inference starts from Gaussian noise and iterates noisy gradient steps with projection onto a sphere. The tangent gradient norm determines when to stop, and the endpoint energy ranks independent candidates (§§3, 3.3, A.1).
2. **Convergence supervision.** Training supervises the final states of each segment and uses changes in the latent state to encourage trajectories with correct predictions to become stationary. Truncated backpropagation bounds the length of the training graph, while differentiating through descent requires second-order derivatives (§3.1, Fig. 2).
3. **State-based Explorative Modeling (SXM).** Multiple trajectories persist across segments, with one selected for gradient-based supervision per segment. Forward SXM picks a trajectory for a given target. Reverse SXM can reassign its supervised states to better-matching valid targets, using even a partial set of solutions (§3.2, Fig. 3, Appendices A.4 and E).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong Sudoku-Extreme exact accuracy | EBR achieved 99.4% with one sample and 99.9% with lowest-energy selection from five. Looped Flows reported 97.9% and 99.3% at the same sample counts. | Table 1 |
| Adaptive stopping reduced Sudoku inference FLOPs | At a tangent-gradient threshold of 0.2, eight-sample exact accuracy stayed at 99.92%, while inference fell from 6.60 to 0.61 TFLOPs per puzzle, a 10.8× reduction versus the fixed 64-iteration EBR. The authors report up to 345× lower FLOPs than EqR at comparable accuracy, using EqR’s published full-test accuracy. | §4.2, Fig. 5b, Table C.2, Appendix B |
| Maze-Hard accuracy depends on the path criterion | With the same trajectories, labeled-path exact match was 87.37% with one sample and 88.27% with lowest-energy selection from five. Accepting any shortest path raised those scores to 94.07% and 94.77%. | Table D.1 |
| EBR recovered more distinct valid solutions | On 10×10 N-Queens, 20-sample coverage was 92.0% for EBR and 57.5% for GRAM as tabulated. Under GRAM’s convention, EBR coverage was 89.3%. On 8- and 10-vertex Graph Coloring, EBR had zero conflicting test edges with one sample and achieved uncapped 20-sample coverage of 88.6% and 54.5%, versus GRAM’s 85.8% and 51.3%. | Table 2, Appendix C |
| Ablations support energy descent and convergence supervision within EBR | In a single-seed Sudoku-Extreme ablation with one inference sample, the reference EBR scored 98.96% exact accuracy. A directly learned transition scored 60.05%, and removing convergence loss scored 45.32%. | §4.4, Table 3, Appendix C |
| Partial target sets helped reverse SXM | In a single-seed 8×8 N-Queens ablation, moving from one valid training target per example to at most two raised single-sample accuracy from 72.18% to 90.73% and Cov@20 from 87.5% to 93.5%. | Table E.1, Appendix C |

The Sudoku experiments support energy as an empirical ranking signal and gradient norm as a stopping signal in that setting. Lowest-energy selection approached the label-based oracle in the reported comparison. Spurious minima remain possible, so stationarity alone cannot certify correctness. The benchmark results support the method on structured tasks, while the cross-model comparisons depend on the reported sample budgets, selection rules, and metric definitions (§4.2, Figs. 6–7, Tables 1–2).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors.** Second-order derivatives and SXM rollouts increase training cost, and descent introduces extra hyperparameters. The authors report higher training FLOPs than GRAM on N-Queens and Graph Coloring, despite the favorable Sudoku comparison (§5, Appendix B).
- **Authors.** SXM yielded no gain on Maze-Hard. The authors suggest limited training data as an explanation, but did not vary training-set size. Evaluation is confined to structured algorithmic tasks, leaving larger models and broader reasoning tasks open (§5, Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference.** The headline FLOP ratio should be read as a comparison between reported configurations. EqR’s full-budget accuracy was taken from its paper, and its checkpoint-based scaling curve used Sudoku-Lite. The supplied results do not establish a corresponding wall-clock speedup (§4.2, Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference.** The component ablations have one seed, and the direct-transition variant retains EBR’s training setup. They support conclusions about that setup, with uncertain transfer to other recurrent architectures (§4.4, Appendix C).

## Open research questions

1. **Does more maze training data make SXM useful?** The paper did not vary training-set size. Train with and without SXM across increasing maze dataset sizes under matched compute budgets, and measure both labeled-path and any-shortest-path accuracy (§5, Appendix D).
2. **How well do energy ranking and adaptive stopping transfer to broader reasoning tasks?** The experiments use structured algorithmic benchmarks. Compare energy-based and directly learned recurrence on a language reasoning task at matched parameter counts and inference FLOPs, measuring accuracy, premature stops, and the gap to oracle selection (§5).
3. **How many valid targets does reverse SXM need under a fixed training budget?** Appendix E varies target availability using one seed and shared hyperparameters. Repeat the sweep across seeds, tune each target budget under the same compute allowance, and track coverage of valid solutions withheld during training (Appendices C and E).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the method description and the reported within-benchmark findings. The supplied text does not provide matched wall-clock inference timings, a full-test EqR rerun under a common protocol, or multi-seed uncertainty for the ablations. The linked codebase’s contents beyond the authors’ description, including checkpoint availability, are unverified.
