# Zuse: A Decision Model for European Languages

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-17.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.06238) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06238)


## One-sentence takeaway

Zuse-27B reached 87.5% accuracy on a 27-language European development suite, where confidence errors varied substantially across sources but showed little detectable variation across languages on matched reading and topic items.

## Problem and motivation

Operational tasks such as ticket routing and policy checks require choosing among predefined options, and confidence scores can help decide which cases to automate or send for human review. The author found no published per-language evaluation of comparable decision models and asks how well the design transfers across European languages, how calibration varies, and how much work can be automated at a fixed error budget (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Model family.** Zuse trains a LoRA adapter and pointer head on frozen open-weight bases at 4B, 9B and 27B. The head scores caller-supplied options, and a fitted temperature converts scores to probabilities. Training combines cross-entropy with an option-permutation consistency loss. The request layout and serving stack follow Kev (§§2–3, Fig. 1).
- **Multilingual evaluation.** The paper evaluates 27 European languages across 11 sources and nine task groups, and measures reading and topic classification across a combined 210 languages. The author presents this as the first per-language evaluation of a decision model (§1, §§6.3–6.4).
- **Calibration and selective prediction.** The analysis compares confidence errors across sources and across matched items in different languages, tests threshold transfer, and relates in-sample automation coverage to chance-corrected skill and confidence ranking (§5.2, §6.3).
- **Training diagnostics.** Run comparisons examine base-model choice, conflicting labels across training sources, option-order sensitivity, and shortcut learning in planted-instruction robustness evaluation (§7).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| European development performance | Zuse-27B achieved 87.5% accuracy across 11 sources, nine task groups, and 27 languages. The author reports excluding the sources from training and using the suite for model selection. Pooled 15-bin expected calibration error (ECE) was 0.016, versus 0.035 when source-level ECEs were weighted by row count. | Abstract, §§5.1–5.3, 6.3, 9, Table 5 |
| Source-level confidence gaps exceeded variation across matched languages | On parallel Belebele and SIB-200 items across 26 languages, the standard deviation of Zuse-27B’s mean-confidence-minus-accuracy gap was 0.015, near estimated sampling noise of 0.014. Across European sources the gap ranged from −0.071 to +0.091. Multilingual MMLU and the Norwegian quiz were overconfident, and Nordic grammar was underconfident, at all three sizes. | §6.3 |
| Confidence ranking and chance-corrected skill tracked automation coverage | At a 5% in-sample error budget, Spearman correlation with coverage was 0.97 for chance-corrected skill and 0.91 for raw accuracy over 33 source–model pairs. Confidence-ranking AUROC reached 0.98 over 31 defined pairs. A threshold fitted on the Transfer v4 test split incurred 8.9% error on development. | §5.2, §6.3, Fig. 2 |
| World-language scores were stable within a model size across the runs studied | Across nine checkpoints evaluated on Belebele and SIB-200, world-language scores stayed within 1.2 percentage points of the same-size final model for languages on the approximate Qwen list and within 2.1 points for those outside it. The author interprets this as evidence that the base largely determines reach. | §§6.4, 7.1, Table 6 |
| Zuse trailed reported English comparators on Transfer v4 | Zuse-27B was 2.5 accuracy points behind Jev on development (indicative unpaired p = 0.19) and 2.9 points behind Kev-27B on test. Kev scores came from the Kev project. | §6.1, Table 3 |
| Training diagnostics exposed label interference and a robustness shortcut | In the closest paired comparison, adding one training family reduced SIB-200 topic accuracy from 87.6% to 77.3%, with shared labels proposed as the cause. In an early 4B run, planted-instruction following fell from 37.6% to 0.2% after augmentation, yet accuracy rose from 75.6% clean to 81.7% under perturbation. The author attributes this to a shortcut in the augmentation. | §§7.2–7.3 |

The matched-item evidence supports the author’s interpretation that task or source mattered more than language for calibration on the reading and topic tasks studied. Independent European performance, a general language-invariance claim, and deployable coverage at the stated error budget remain unverified. The world-language comparisons are observational and leave the causal contribution of the base unresolved.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported.** The European suite and Transfer v4 development split guided model and data choices, and English test scores were consulted repeatedly. Most configurations were run once, European rows cluster by source, and two Transfer v4 sources may share origins with training or earlier runs. The European source exclusion check used source identifiers, with row-level overlap unverified (§§5.1, 5.3, 8).
- **Author-reported.** The European suite lacks matched Jev, Kev, Clef, or structured-output frontier-model comparisons. English comparisons use externally reported scores, Jev’s revision is unknown, and breadth results use deployed 4-bit Zuse builds (§§6.1–6.2, 8).
- **Author-reported.** Knowledge-heavy tasks remain weak, order sensitivity and planted-instruction following persist, and the robustness test uses few wordings. Accuracy above 32k tokens is unmeasured, and evaluation uses public benchmarks or synthetic documents. Undisclosed training settings and data prevent training reproduction (§8, Availability).
- **Analyst inference.** The matched-language analysis covers two tasks, so it leaves open whether language effects emerge on knowledge or acceptability tasks. Base-model and order-stability findings also need replicated, controlled comparisons because the recipe favored Qwen and some run groups differed in data (§§6.3, 7.1, 7.4).

## Open research questions

1. **How does Zuse compare with other decision models on unseen European sources?** The European suite guided development and lacks matched competitors. Score frozen Zuse, Kev, and Clef once on a new European suite with identical inputs and metrics (§8, §9).
2. **Can task-specific calibration meet a 5% error budget on unseen sources?** The reported thresholds were fitted in-sample, and one split transfer exceeded the budget. Fit global and task-specific thresholds on separate calibration data, then compare realized error and coverage on held-out sources (§5.2, §6.3).
3. **How much multilingual reach comes from the base versus adaptation?** The checkpoints used similar training data, and the base comparison used a Qwen-developed recipe. Cross base choice with training-language coverage under matched settings and repeated seeds, then evaluate on the same reading and topic items (§§6.4, 7.1).
4. **Does the calibration pattern extend to other tasks?** The matched-language result covers reading and topic classification. Build parallel knowledge and acceptability sets and estimate confidence gaps within each task across the same languages (§6.3).

## Confidence and missing information

**Medium.** The retrieved prose supports the headline results and their evaluation settings, while the source does not include the cells of Tables 1–7. Material gaps include full score breakdowns, training data composition and size, hyperparameters and confirmed seeds, training code and open weights, sampled European evaluation rows, paired English comparator predictions, row-level overlap checks, and an independent European test result.
