# InclusionMed: Benchmarking AI for Inclusive Healthcare

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-17.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.21218) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21218)


## One-sentence takeaway

InclusionMed’s first release evaluates 22 model configurations across five healthcare benchmarks, finding that rankings vary by role and capability even though the portfolio covers only three of the nine categories in its evaluation framework.

## Problem and motivation

Medical AI can support clinical care, healthcare operations, and personal health management, but users need evidence that reflects the tasks and settings in which they will use it. The authors argue that existing benchmarks cover these needs unevenly and that an aggregate score can obscure whose needs the evaluation represents. (§1)

## Approach and contributions

- **InclusionMed Atlas and audit workflow.** The Atlas maps each benchmark to its predominant combination of healthcare role and AI capability. An LLM-assisted audit with human review screened 388 candidates against six criteria, then independently reran eight shortlisted benchmarks with the same eight models. Four public benchmarks or subsets were retained. (§§2–3, Appendix A.1)
- **InclusionMed-DxFiles.** The authors developed a private benchmark of 312 English-language diagnostic cases adapted from published case reports. A response earns a perfect grade when it identifies the reference primary diagnosis and every additional diagnostic claim is supported by the case. (§§3.5, 4.2, Appendix C)
- **InclusionMed Index and task tags.** The Index averages five benchmark scores with equal weights and reports separate scores by role and capability. Task tags support comparisons within benchmarks, and token usage and published prices support an inference-cost comparison. (§§4–5, Appendix A.2)

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Screening narrowed coverage | Eight shortlisted benchmarks were rerun with the same eight models. Three tested knowledge benchmarks met the saturation criterion and HealthBench met the limited-discrimination criterion. The four retained public benchmarks plus DxFiles cover 3 of 9 Atlas cells. | §§1, 3.3–3.5, Figures 1 and S3, Appendix A.1 |
| Overall scores ranged from 17.5 to 49.1 | Across 22 configurations, the equally weighted five-benchmark Index ranged from 17.5 to 49.1 out of 100. Gemini 4 Argon led at 49.1, followed by Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1, both at a displayed 48.9. | §§4.3, 5.1, Figure 4 |
| Rankings depended on capability | GPT-6 Luna ranked eighth in reasoning and twentieth in agency, based on the mean benchmark scores within each category. | §§4.3, 5.2, Table 2 |
| Communication scored higher than clinical decision-making | All ten highest-ranked Index models scored higher on patient communication than on clinical decision-making within HealthBench Professional. The median length-adjusted patient communication score was 69.1 out of 100. | §1, §5.3, Table 3, Appendix A.2 |
| Treatment tasks scored lower than assessment and diagnosis | All ten highest-ranked models scored lower on treatment planning and implementation than on clinical assessment and diagnosis in HealthBench Professional, with median length-adjusted scores of 53.0 versus 59.1 out of 100. The same direction held for eight of ten models on PhysicianBench case pass rates. | §5.3, Tables 4 and S5 |
| Similar Index scores came with different estimated costs | Kimi K3 scored 37.9 and GPT-5.6 Luna scored 36.9 out of 100. Their estimated mean token costs per task run, weighted equally across the five benchmarks, were $1.51 and $0.064, approximately a 24-fold difference. | §§4.4, 5.4, Figure 5 |

The evidence supports descriptive comparisons for the tested model configurations, tasks, scoring rules, and prices. Clinical benefit, deployment readiness, and performance across underrepresented populations and settings remain unestablished.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ stated limitations.** The portfolio lacks a primarily patient-facing benchmark and is predominantly English and text-only. Geographic setting was identifiable for 338 of 1,034 tasks, of which 318 were associated with the US. Differences in task content and scoring limit cross-category comparisons, and the authors propose weight and benchmark-omission sensitivity analyses as future work. Token-cost estimates exclude failed runs and retries for the four public benchmarks, plus judge, search, tool, sandbox, and internal platform charges. (§§6.1–6.3, Appendix B.2.1)
- **Analyst inferences.** The CLI results reflect model–harness combinations because harnesses vary across model groups. The private DxFiles cases limit independent verification, and the supplied text gives no clinician-agreement check for its GPT-6.1 Sol judge, which also belongs to the evaluated model set. (Appendix A.2, Table S2, Appendix C.3)

## Open research questions

1. **How stable are the rankings under different portfolio choices?** The release reports one equal-weight index. Recompute rankings under role-based weights and leave-one-benchmark-out variants, and measure rank changes. (§6.2)
2. **Do model rankings transfer to sustained patient-facing interactions across languages and care settings?** The portfolio has no primarily patient-facing benchmark and concentrates on English and identifiable US settings. Evaluate the same models on matched, clinician-reviewed multi-turn cases across languages and settings. (§6.1, Appendix B.2.1)
3. **Which models are cheapest per successfully completed workflow?** The reported estimates omit several execution costs. Rerun the same workflows while recording retry, judge, tool, and infrastructure costs, then compare total cost per successful completion. (§6.3)

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported design and descriptive findings. Material gaps in the supplied text include the full model-by-benchmark score matrix, uncertainty estimates for close rankings, the private DxFiles case inputs and grading prompt, and clinician agreement with the DxFiles judge.
