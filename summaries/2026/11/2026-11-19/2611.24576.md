# Late Entry in Looped Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.24576) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24576)


## One-sentence takeaway

In adapted looped language models, running only the final recurrent passes on new tokens while completing earlier tokens’ caches in parallel improved agreement over matched early exit and let Wake verification reach up to 8.42× the throughput of full-depth sequential greedy decoding on the tested hardware (§§2, 4.6).

## Problem and motivation

Looped language models reuse a Transformer stack to increase reasoning depth, but each recurrent pass adds a serial dependency during autoregressive decoding. Early exit saves time by running the first \(K\) of \(T\) passes, leaving shallow key–value (KV) caches for future tokens. The authors ask whether spending the same budget on the final \(K\) passes over deeper context preserves predictions better, and whether a decoder can build that context without stalling generation (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Pass allocation and adaptation.** After full-depth instruction tuning, a second training stage exposes the model to entering directly at a late pass over a full-depth prefix cache. Matched early-exit controls, entry-state ablations, and KV interventions test how adaptation and pass-aligned context affect predictions (§2, Appendix B).
- **Pipelined decoding and Wake verification.** An existing block decoder verifies candidates through the final \(K\) passes. Tentatively accepted tokens complete all \(T\) passes as parallel rows in subsequent calls. Wake verification scores those completed states in the same output projection and uses a logit-margin threshold to select greedy verification, margin-gated rollback, or lossy decoding without rollback (§3).
- **Depth and serving analysis.** The authors vary recurrence depth on a fixed Huginn checkpoint and fit an H100 Roofline model on full-depth Ouro runs to predict throughput at reduced pass budgets (§§4.6–4.7).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Late-entry exposure improved prediction agreement. | On Ouro-2.6B, GSM8K teacher-forced agreement with full-depth greedy predictions at \(K=1\) over a four-pass cache was 96.2% with late-entry exposure, 69.9% after Stage 1 alone, and 73.8% after Stage 2 without that exposure. | §2.1, Appendix B, Table 9 |
| Deep context compensated for fewer token passes. | On adapted Ouro-2.6B, one final pass over a four-pass cache reached 96.2% teacher-forced GSM8K agreement with full depth, versus 95.4% for two initial passes over a two-pass cache. | §2.2, Fig. 2 |
| Lossy late entry beat trained early exit at similar throughput. | On full GSM8K with Ouro-2.6B, \(K=2\), block size \(B=8\), and one H100, late entry scored 83.9% at 427 tokens/s versus 66.9% at 431 tokens/s for matched trained early exit, a reported 16.9-point gain before rounding. | §4.2, Table 4 |
| Exact Wake improved on full-depth block throughput. | On full GSM8K with Ouro-2.6B, \(T=4\), \(K=2\), \(B=8\), and one H100, \(\tau=0\) reached 5.20× same-job full-depth sequential throughput, 26% above \(K=4\) block decoding, at the same reported 86.7% accuracy. Committed-token discrepancy against teacher-forced full-depth replay was 0.007%. | §4.5, Table 7, Appendix E |
| Deeper Huginn recurrence reduced rollbacks. | On full GSM8K with a fixed Huginn-3.5B checkpoint, \(B=8\), and \(K=T/4\), exact-mode rollbacks fell from 12.46 to 0.58 per 100 emitted tokens from \(T=8\) to \(T=64\). At \(T=64\), exact Wake reached 8.42× same-depth sequential throughput, 153% above full-depth block decoding. | §4.6, Fig. 11, Table 8 |
| The serving model predicted held-out reduced-pass throughput. | For Ouro-2.6B on GSM8K on one H100, fitting only \(K=4\) runs yielded 8.3% mean absolute percentage error on 24 unseen \(K\in\{2,3\}\) continuous-batching settings. At 32 concurrent requests, \(K=2\) with block size selected for load reached 2.31× decode and 1.67× end-to-end throughput versus full-depth autoregressive decoding at the same concurrency. | §4.7, Fig. 12, Appendix I |

The results support a pass-allocation advantage in the tested checkpoints and a throughput advantage when rollback and batching costs stay low. Teacher-forced agreement measures local prediction fidelity on fixed prefixes, while free-running benchmarks measure task outcomes. The authors claim exact greedy equivalence in exact arithmetic. The bf16 audit supplies empirical fidelity measurements with small discrepancies, and similar task accuracy under margin gating leaves sequence equivalence unestablished (§3.4, §4.5, Appendix E).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Wake’s guarantee covers greedy decoding. Speed depends on tentative agreement and spare GPU capacity. At shallow Huginn recurrence, frequent rollbacks made Exact Wake slower than full-depth block decoding. Fixed large blocks also lost throughput under concurrent load, and adapting existing checkpoints required an additional supervised training stage (§6).
- **Analyst inference.** The recurrence-depth sweep uses one Huginn checkpoint on GSM8K, and the serving sweep uses Ouro-2.6B on GSM8K on one H100. The measured rollback trend and serving crossover have limited demonstrated portability (§§4.6–4.7, Appendix I).
- **Analyst inference.** Equation 2 uses a strict positive-margin test at \(\tau=0\). A tentative token tied with a different selected argmax has a zero margin, so the stated rule leaves exact tie handling ambiguous (§3.4).
- **Comparison caveat.** The LoopAhead results come from the authors’ reproduction. The reported cross-engine ranking therefore depends on that implementation and the configurations tested (Appendix G).

## Open research questions

1. **Can delayed Wake verification preserve the full-depth sampling distribution?** The guarantee and experiments cover greedy decoding. A next experiment could implement delayed rejection sampling and compare empirical next-token distributions and throughput against full-depth sampling at fixed temperatures (§6).
2. **Can adaptive pass budgets improve the speed–fidelity trade-off?** Fixed aggressive budgets incurred frequent rollbacks at shallow recurrence. A next experiment could tune a confidence-based budget selector on held-out prompts and compare committed-token fidelity, rollback rates, and throughput with fixed-budget Wake (§§4.6, 6).
3. **Does the Roofline model transfer across hardware and request mixes?** The serving validation uses one GPU type and one benchmark. A next experiment could fit full-depth runs separately on different accelerators, then test reduced-pass throughput predictions under mixed prompt and generation lengths (§4.7, Appendix I).
