# Thinking Harder Doesn&#x27;t Lead to Better Choices: Reasoning Effort Increases the Confidence but Not the Correctness of LLM Shopping Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.22033) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22033)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across three fixed-choice shopping benchmarks, raising requested reasoning effort often increased models’ stated confidence, while only one low-to-high accuracy gain survived correction for multiple comparisons (Table 1).

## Problem and motivation

Shopping assistants need to choose products that satisfy user requests and recognize when to defer to the user. The authors ask whether higher reasoning effort improves either product choice or confidence calibration. They identify a gap in end-to-end shopping evaluations, where search and navigation failures can obscure whether the model can choose correctly from the listings it sees (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Fixed-choice evaluation.** The authors build tasks from WebShop (496), Amazon SQID (500), and Wayfair WANDS (330). Each task has a fixed, shuffled list with one labeled target and nine near misses. WebShop uses simulator reward to score product and option choices. SQID and WANDS use human relevance labels (§3.1, Table S1).
- **Paired effort and calibration analysis.** Ten models answer the same tasks under provider-defined effort settings and report integer confidence from 0 to 10. The authors compare within-task changes and assess calibration and discrimination. Two models lack a medium setting (§§3.2–3.3, Tables 1–2).
- **Shared-error analysis.** The authors compare how often models fail together against a binomial baseline and how often they choose the same distractor against a uniform-choice simulation (§§3.3, 4.4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy seldom improved with higher effort | For paired low→high changes in success rate, only Qwen 3.8 Max on WebShop survived Holm correction (+5.5 percentage points). GPT-6 Luna on WANDS improved by +5.2 points at nominal significance, with a corrected +5.5-point gain from low→medium. | Table 1, Table S11 |
| Stated confidence rose more consistently | Paired low→high mean confidence rose at nominal significance for 9/10 models on WebShop, 5/10 on SQID, and 6/10 on WANDS. The corresponding counts after Holm correction were 8/10, 4/10, and 5/10. | §4.2, Table 1 |
| Calibration rankings were similar across datasets | Pooled over effort, cross-dataset Spearman correlations for model ECE rankings were 0.79–0.92. Both Claude models had the lowest ECEs on each benchmark, yet both were overconfident on SQID. | §4.3, Tables 2 and S2 |
| Models often failed on the same tasks | Across datasets and effort levels, the variance of the number of wrong models was 5.9–8.3 times the binomial baseline. Implied pairwise error correlations were 0.70–0.81. | §4.4, Table 3 |
| Models often selected the same wrong listing | On low-effort tasks with at least two wrong models, the most-selected distractor accounted for 87%, 81%, and 82% of wrong choices on WebShop, SQID, and WANDS, versus 36%, 32%, and 33% under uniform selection among distractors. | §4.4, Table S2 |

The paired results support a more consistent response of stated confidence than of benchmark accuracy to the tested effort settings. Nonsignificant accuracy contrasts leave small effects unresolved. The shared-error results quantify departures from the specified baselines. The cause of that agreement and the performance of a deployed second-opinion system remain untested.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ limitations.** The tasks omit search, pagination, and navigation. Label ambiguity can depress measured accuracy and may inflate shared-error estimates. In a manual audit, 15 of 100 sampled WebShop distractors were arguably acceptable (95% CI 9–23%). Confidence was elicited once in the answer, and each condition ran once with provider-default sampling. A requested effort level corresponds to different reasoning-token usage across providers (§6, Supplement §B, Fig. S8).
- **Analyst inference.** The same labels support paired comparisons, but effort could change how often a model selects a defensible distractor, so the bias need not cancel. Cross-dataset differences also cannot isolate the effect of images because the catalogs, queries, and label rules change together (§3.1).

## Open research questions

1. **Does higher effort help during search?** The experiments supply the candidate lists. Compare the same models and queries under fixed-list and interactive search conditions, scoring retrieval and final choice separately (§§6–7).
2. **Can confidence be made more useful for deferral?** The study uses one elicitation protocol. Compare alternative prompts and calibration learned on held-out tasks, then measure error rates at matched answer coverage (§6, Supplement §F).
3. **How much shared error comes from ambiguous labels or salient listing cues?** The baselines establish clustering, while the cause remains open. Independently adjudicate a sample of shared failures, then vary listing cues while preserving the product facts and repeat the error analysis (§§4.4, 6–7).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the main within-benchmark pattern. The paired contrasts and shared-error comparisons are tabulated. Run-to-run variance and provider-internal effort implementations are unavailable, and the supplied text describes a future release of the frozen tasks, logs, and code. Exact valid-decision counts for SQID and WANDS are ambiguous because Table S1 and Supplement §§C–D report different totals.
