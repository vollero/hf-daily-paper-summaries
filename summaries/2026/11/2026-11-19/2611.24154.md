# Game2Player: Scaling Gameplay Experience for Visual Agents through Environment-Player Co-Development

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.24154) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24154)


## One-sentence takeaway

Game2Player uses feedback from two kinds of players to build state-scored browser-game tasks and collect demonstrations that raise Qwen3.5-9B’s progress from 14.3% to 31.2% on held-out PlayerBench games and from 30.0% to 53.1% on the independent GameWorld benchmark with thinking enabled (Table 3).

## Problem and motivation

Training visual game agents requires trajectories with explicit goals, recorded actions, and reliable outcome signals. Human demonstrations are costly, online videos generally lack action labels, and interactive environments require game-specific engineering. The authors identify a further gap in automated construction: a game can pass the builder’s checks while actual play exposes broken controls, stalled progress, or premature success (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Environment–player co-development.** A Builder packages existing browser games for offline play and defines task starts, progress, and success using internal game state. A screenshot-based VLM Player and a real-time Python policy using structured, player-visible state test the frozen tasks. The Builder checks reports against recordings before making repairs (§§3.1–3.2, Fig. 2).
- **Gameplay corpus and held-out benchmark.** Both players record goal-conditioned trajectories in a shared action format. The collection contains 568 accepted games and 3,436 tasks, with 50 games and 287 tasks reserved for PlayerBench (§§3.2–3.3, §4.1).
- **Progress-weighted distillation.** The authors fine-tune screenshot-based agents on code-policy actions and VLM actions with notes or thinking where available. They resample training steps to favor actions near progress or success events, penalize unproductive repetition and some failed endings, and reduce the dominance of games with many examples (§3.4, Table 11).

| Weighted sampling helped at a matched example count | With thinking off, uniform → weighted sampling raised 9B progress from 23.9% to 28.6% on PlayerBench and from 31.1% to 33.9% on GameWorld. The matched base-model comparisons support transfer to unseen browser games under the authors’ protocol. Progress measures the best point reached in an episode, averaged over equally weighted games, while success measures task completion (§4.1). The rebuild supports an effect of returning reports in the studied games, with correctness gains depending on the Builder. The sampling comparison tests the weighting recipe as a bundle, so the gains cannot be assigned to individual rules. Some published baselines use different harnesses or trial budgets, which limits direct ranking comparisons (Table 3, Appendix D).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Acceptance can miss implementation errors. The rebuild audit found 72% of tasks correct with the Terra Builder and 94% with the Sol Builder in the full-loop settings. Human review of all 287 PlayerBench tasks found no environment or scoring errors (§5, Appendices E–F).
- **Author-stated.** The pipeline covers offline single-player browser games. Pausing at every VLM decision leaves real-time performance unmeasured, and rebuilding with different versions of proprietary models may change the environments. The authors report releasing source code, trained models, and task specifications. Games, assets, and instrumented environments are withheld from redistribution (Appendix H, §5).
- **Analyst inference.** The comparison between teacher sources mixes differences in data volume, observability, trajectory selection, and reasoning supervision. Code-policy episodes are excluded for tasks no policy completed. The ablation therefore leaves the independent effect of each source characteristic unresolved (§4.4, Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference.** The human scores cannot isolate benchmark difficulty because PlayerBench used first-time players and GameWorld used a practiced expert. The authors also caution that small ablation differences are comparable to the run-to-run variation measured with thinking off (Appendices D and F).

## Open research questions

1. **Which sampling rules drive the gains?** The uniform comparison changes progress credit, penalties, and game balancing together. Ablate each component at a fixed example count and repeat across training and evaluation seeds (Table 4, Table 11).
2. **When do the two teachers complement each other?** The best data source varies by benchmark, and the sources differ in task coverage, volume, and supervision. Match tasks and example counts, then vary the teacher mix and whether VLM notes and thinking are supervised (§4.4, Appendix C).
3. **How much of the gain survives real-time play?** VLM decisions are made with the game paused. Evaluate the same base and fine-tuned agents on held-out timing-sensitive games under controlled decision deadlines and compare their progress and success with paused play (§5).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported comparisons and protocol. The supplied text does not establish thinking-on variability across seeds, an error rate for the full accepted collection from an independent audit, or real-time performance for the fine-tuned VLMs. It also provides no access details for the full trajectory corpus, which falls under materials still under review (Appendices D–F and H, §5).
