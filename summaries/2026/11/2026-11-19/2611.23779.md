# TabBioMed: A Large-Scale Benchmark for Biomedical Tabular Learning

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.23779) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23779)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across 94 biomedical tabular datasets, foundation models led several aggregate rankings, while boosted trees and neural tabular models offered competitive performance at lower inference cost (§4, Table 2, Figure 6).

## Problem and motivation

Biomedical tabular prediction supports clinical research and biological discovery, but comparisons across studies are fragmented. The authors argue that general-purpose benchmarks underrepresent combinations of high dimensionality, variable sample sizes, missingness, and class imbalance, while biomedical benchmarks tend to focus on individual domains or task families (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Curated benchmark.** TabBioMed consolidates 94 public datasets across eight biomedical domains into feature matrices, targets, and metadata, with dataset-specific splits that can preserve patient groups or molecular scaffolds (§§1, 3.1).
- **Standardized evaluation.** The pipeline fits model-specific preprocessing on training data, selects hyperparameters using validation data under wall-clock budgets, and evaluates on fixed test sets or outer cross-validation folds (§§3.1–3.3).
- **Comparative evidence and release.** The authors evaluate 23 existing models across four families, analyze performance by data regime and compute cost, and report releasing the curated datasets, splits, preprocessing code, baseline implementations, and results (§§3.2, 4–5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Aggregate leaders depend on the summary metric | In the 21-model comparison across 94 datasets, TabICLv2 had the best mean rank (8.011), while TabDPT had the highest mean per-dataset min-max-normalized test score (0.865). | §4, Table 2 |
| Leading models were statistically hard to separate | Across 94 datasets, the Friedman test detected global rank differences at *p* < 0.05. Pairwise Wilcoxon tests on normalized scores with Holm correction detected no significant differences within the 10 highest-ranked models at α = 0.05. | §4, Figure 3 |
| Leading tuned Elo estimates persisted under two missing-score treatments | Tuned RealTabPFNv2.5 and TabPFNv2.5 ranked first and second by Elo point estimate on both the 94-dataset analysis imputing missing scores with Default Random Forest and the 79-dataset complete-case analysis. Leading bootstrap intervals overlapped in the imputed analysis. | §4, Figure 4, reported Figure 20 result |
| Boosted trees and TabM offered favorable latency trade-offs | In the 94-dataset aggregate, median inference times on successful evaluations were 0.020 s for LightGBM, 0.046 s for CatBoost, 0.417 s for TabM, and 31.769 s for TabDPT per 1,000 test rows. Tuned LightGBM, CatBoost, and TabM showed favorable Elo–latency trade-offs. | Table 2, Figure 6 |
| Binary-classification leaders depended on the metric | Across the 48-dataset analysis, ROC-AUC and PR-AUC had median within-dataset Spearman rank agreement of 0.926, yet chose different winners on 19 of 48 datasets. RealTabPFNv2.5 led mean rank under both metrics with 46 complete results, while TabICLv2 led under log loss and Brier score with 48. | §§H.2–H.3, Tables 45–46 |

The results support a group of competitive models whose ordering depends on the metric, dataset regime, and compute budget. The significance tests leave pairwise superiority among the leaders unresolved, and the benchmark leaves clinical readiness and performance with alternative feature representations unestablished (§§4–5).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Public datasets may underrepresent temporal and institution-specific clinical settings, rare diseases, and underserved populations. The supplied features and preprocessing may miss specialized workflows. Some runs ended in timeouts, memory exhaustion, or crashes (§§4–5).
- **Analyst inference.** Failures concentrated on larger, high-dimensional datasets can make successful-run timing medians less representative of the hardest workloads. Agreement between the imputed and complete-case Elo summaries supports the aggregate ranking trend, while performance on the failed runs remains uncertain (§§1, 4, Table 2).
- **Analyst inference.** Appendix I defines family performance using the best observed result from portfolios of different sizes. Those comparisons describe the tested portfolios and leave the performance of a typical family member unresolved (Appendix I).

## Open research questions

1. **Do clinical model rankings hold across hospitals and time periods?** The authors flag limited coverage of deployment settings (§5). Evaluate the leading models on held-out hospitals and later cohorts, measuring discrimination and probability quality.
2. **Can dataset properties predict when external PCA will help?** The reported gains vary by foundation model, and the authors present native dimensionality reduction as a possible explanation (§4). Compare external PCA and native preprocessing on identical high-dimensional splits under a matched tuning budget.
3. **How do compute limits affect which model is best in each regime?** The benchmark uses capped searches and records incomplete runs (§§3.2, 4). Run a budget sweep on fixed splits and report completion rate, predictive performance, and latency jointly.
