# Kezek: Splitting Mono Two-Person Conversations into Per-Speaker Tracks for Full-Duplex Dialogue Data

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-19.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.16933) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16933)


## One-sentence takeaway

Kezek splits mono two-person conversations into time-aligned speaker tracks and retains more short replies than pyannote→DialogueSidon over complete recordings, largely because it returns more audio, while quiet replies under louder speech remain difficult (Table 4, §6.2, Table 9).

## Problem and motivation

Full-duplex spoken dialogue models need separate speaker tracks to learn turn-taking, interruptions, and backchannels, but much of the available conversational audio is mono. The authors identify three consequential failures in converting it to training data: speaker-track swaps, dropped short replies, and leakage between tracks. They argue that evaluation should also account for missing audio and processing speed (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Recording-level speaker assignment and conditioned separation.** PairNet identifies two speaker profiles once per recording and estimates each speaker’s activity. A Conv-TasNet-style separator uses both profiles and the activity estimates, then projects the outputs so they sum exactly to the input (§4, Fig. 1).
- **Dialogue-specific evaluation.** The protocol measures short-reply retention, leakage, channel swaps, and coverage. It defines separate scoring rules for recordings with reference tracks and for those with utterance annotations alone (§3, Appendix B).
- **Training and diagnostic findings.** The authors show that close-talk crosstalk contaminates energy-based activity labels and that a prototype using word-gated labels and profile-conditioned heads swaps tracks much less often. They also show that all-pass augmentation reduces the raw-waveform speaker embedding’s sensitivity to phase changes (§5, Table 1, §7.1).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

| Similar gains on unseen English telephone calls | On 128 two-speaker CallHome calls, first 300 s each, annotation-based retention was 0.796 vs. 0.711 over speech and 0.907 vs. 0.798 in pauses. | Coverage explains much of the Open Yap retention advantage | On the as-recorded Open Yap audio returned by both systems, Kezek vs. the external cascade retained 0.859 vs. 0.865 of replies, with leakage −13.3 vs. −8.5 dB. | §6.2 |
| The word-gated prototype swapped tracks less often | On 113 Open Yap validation conversations, the word-gated prototype with profile-conditioned heads had no swaps on 97.3% clean and 96.5% as-recorded conversations, versus 63.7% and 54.0% for the energy-label prototype. | §5, Table 1, Appendix D exps. 01–03 |
| Diarization performance varied by domain | With overlap scored and zero collar, PairNet vs. pyannote community-1 had DER of 15.27% vs. 25.11% on clean Open Yap test, 19.31% vs. 25.79% as recorded, and 30.05% vs. 18.44% on CallHome. | §6.5, Table 7 |

The results establish whole-recording gains against the published, untuned cascade on the two tested sets. The authors attribute most of the retention gain to coverage, consistent with the near-tie on shared Open Yap audio. Energy-based retention must be read alongside leakage because copying the mixture to both tracks can score highly on retention (§3, Table 15). The generative baseline’s output precludes a comparable overlap SI-SDRi measurement (§6.6), and downstream dialogue-model training was unevaluated.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ limitations.** Kezek processes exactly two speakers offline and performs separation without restoration. Quiet overlapping replies remain the main failure. Training and the main quantitative evaluations are in English, and the small Kazakh spot check is insufficient for a language-transfer conclusion. The Open Yap test split was evaluated repeatedly during development with model choices fixed on validation. CallHome was evaluated once and lacks reference tracks (§8, Table 1).
- **Analyst inference.** Table 1 combines word gating with profile-conditioned heads, so it cannot isolate the effect of word gating alone. CallHome and Open Yap use different reply criteria, which limits direct comparison of retention scores across corpora (§3, Appendix B). Evaluation on the first 300 seconds of each recording leaves identity stability on longer conversations unverified (§6.1).

## Open research questions

1. **Can a different separator recover quiet replies with the same activity hints?** The labelled-activity separator still trails the ideal mask (Table 9). Compare alternative separators with identical speaker profiles and labelled hints, scoring audible retention by reply level alongside leakage.
2. **How much of the swap reduction comes from word gating alone?** The prototype changed both labels and heads (Table 1, Appendix D). Cross energy-based and word-gated labels with the original and profile-conditioned heads under a fixed training schedule.
3. **Do the extracted tracks improve full-duplex dialogue behavior?** The paper evaluates data preparation directly (§6). Train matched dialogue models with Kezek tracks and baseline tracks, using both full-recording and matched-coverage conditions, then measure backchannels and turn-taking on held-out conversations.

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported comparisons within their stated settings. The supplied text gives point estimates without uncertainty intervals, reports only one full external separation baseline, and provides no paired CallHome retention value for Kezek on the shared-coverage subset (§6.2). It reports neither downstream dialogue-model outcomes nor a systematic multilingual evaluation (§§6–8).
