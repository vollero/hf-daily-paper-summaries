# TuringSpace: Grounding Spatial Intelligence in Action with Interactive CAPTCHAs

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.25387) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25387)


## One-sentence takeaway

On TuringSpace’s synthetic browser puzzles, humans scored 94.1% pass@1 versus 74.1% for the best tested model, while fine-tuning Qwen3.5-9B raised its score from 1.6% to 33.1% and yielded reported gains on seven external benchmarks (Table 1, §4.3).

## Problem and motivation

The authors ask whether multimodal models can infer spatial structure from screenshots, predict the effects of actions, and adjust from visual feedback. They argue that conventional CAPTCHAs offer limited spatial challenge, while many spatial benchmarks use multiple-choice answers or symbolic commands that do not require models to act on the scene (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **TuringSpace-Bench** contains 125 synthetic browser tasks spanning five spatial abilities. Some require a single selection, while others require manipulation, planning, or frame-by-frame observation (§3.1, Fig. 2).
- **TuringSpace-Gym** uses the same task generators to produce fresh instances and verified demonstrations. Training and evaluation use disjoint seeds and asset pools, and matching spatial configurations are filtered out (§3.3).
- **TuringSpace-9B** is a supervised fine-tune of Qwen3.5-9B on 55,000 verified demonstrations, with the vision encoder frozen. The authors evaluate it on held-out instances and external spatial tasks (§§3.4, 4.3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Humans led every tested model | Over three held-out instances per task, humans scored 94.1% pass@1 and 84.8% pass³, versus 74.1% and 58.4% for GPT-6 Astra. Pass³ requires solving all three instances. | §4.1–4.2, Table 1 |
| Fine-tuning improved benchmark performance | TuringSpace-9B scored 33.1% pass@1 and 12.8% pass³, versus 1.6% and 0.0% for the non-thinking Qwen3.5-9B base and 32.0% and 7.2% for Kimi K3. | §4.2, Table 1 |
| Gains extended to external benchmarks | The authors report zero-shot improvement over the non-thinking base on all seven external benchmarks, including a 9.9-point gain on VSI-Bench’s aggregate of multiple-choice accuracy and numerical mean relative accuracy for real indoor videos. | §1, Fig. 1b, §4.3, Appendix B |
| Higher reasoning effort helped some models | On the same single test seed, Claude Sonnet 5.5 rose from 29.6% to 74.4% success from lowest to highest effort. Gemini 3.8 Flash stayed within 29.6%–33.6% across effort levels. | §5.1, Fig. 4a |
| Perception dominated labeled failures | Of 380 analyzed failures from the three strongest models, 217 (57.1%) were attributed to perception. Five refusals were excluded. | §5.3, Appendix D, Table 6 |
| Early wrong beliefs often persisted | Among 293 failed attempts from three open-weight models on one seed, 89.4% questioned the decisive wrong belief, 7.5% abandoned it, and 74% were labeled as containing a fabricated observation. | §5.2, Fig. 5, Appendix C |

The evidence supports a human–model gap under the reported protocol and transfer gains for one fine-tuned model on the selected benchmarks. The comparison leaves open how much of the gain comes from sequential visual feedback versus other aspects of fine-tuning. The robotics evaluations measure pointing and affordance predictions from images, so physical task execution remains untested (Appendix B).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope.** The authors flag potential dual use for deployed CAPTCHAs. Their benchmark evaluation uses synthetic browser tasks, and the transfer evaluation uses selected benchmarks (§§3, 4.3, Appendix H).
- **Analyst inference — generalization.** Held-out seeds, assets, and configurations test variation within the same generators. The results leave generalization to unseen task families open (§3.3).
- **Analyst inference — attribution.** The supplied text reports no matched ablation that isolates sequential feedback from spatial supervision or GUI practice. Appendix B.2 also describes differences in prompts or decoding for the base and fine-tuned models on the CAPTCHA comparisons, complicating attribution of those gains.
- **Analyst inference — sampling and labeling.** The human baseline used three authors, each assigned a different seed, so participant and seed effects cannot be disentangled. The belief analysis sampled failures only, which limits conclusions about whether the same behaviors also occur in successful attempts (§§4.1, 5.2).

## Open research questions

1. **Does sequential feedback drive the transfer gains?** The reported fine-tune bundles feedback with spatial and action supervision. A matched experiment could train on ordered trajectories versus static supervision from the same puzzles and compare performance on non-CAPTCHA spatial benchmarks.
2. **Do gains extend to unseen spatial task families?** The benchmark split reuses task generators. An experiment could hold out entire generator families and evaluate transfer on fresh synthetic spatial puzzles.
3. **Does early belief revision improve outcomes?** The trace analysis covers failed attempts, so it cannot show whether revision predicts success. An experiment could compare successful and failed traces, then randomly assign a visual recheck before the first consequential action in a synthetic manipulation task.

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the benchmark design and Table 1 comparisons. **Medium** for the magnitude of transfer across individual benchmarks and for mechanism claims. The supplied text includes the captions but omits the rows of Tables 2, 3, and 5, leaving most exact transfer scores, the full training configuration, and some decoding settings unavailable. It also does not establish whether the announced release occurred or whether model weights are available.
