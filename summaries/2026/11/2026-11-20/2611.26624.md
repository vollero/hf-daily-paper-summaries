# PlayCraft: Designing Engaging Games from Agent Play Experience

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.26624) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26624)


## One-sentence takeaway

PlayCraft uses reports from repeated visual agent play to revise games for mastery, yielding higher average normalized learning gains and slopes than code-only and policy-controller revisions at all three tested agent settings (§4.3, Tables 2–3).

## Problem and motivation

Generating a playable game leaves open whether practice will yield meaningful improvements in strategy and execution. Designing for that progression typically requires substantial human playtesting and tuning. The authors identify two gaps in prior work. Search-based or game-trained players may discover strategies unlike those humans develop, and agent playtesting systems generally target overall game quality with limited explicit attention to learning through practice (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Play experience as design evidence.** A foundation-model agent plays from rendered frames using the game’s controls. The game resets between episodes while the agent retains memory, consolidates lessons, and reports outcomes, decisions, and difficulties. The reports form the play record (§3.2.2).
- **Revision guided by proficiency targets.** A crafter estimates the player’s starting abilities and expected beginner, intermediate, and expert outcomes. It compares those estimates with expert-relative targets, then proposes, implements, and checks changes intended to reward better decisions and execution while preserving the game’s core interactions (§3.2.3, Figure 1).
- **Controlled comparison of revision evidence.** The study compares the full play–craft loop with code-only analysis and internal-state policy controllers, using shared design targets and revision procedures followed by fresh visual-agent evaluation (§§4.1.2–4.2).

## Evidence and main results

The final-build comparison used three fresh runs of ten episodes per game, method, and agent setting across ten games. Scores were normalized by the maximum observed outcome for each game and method, pooling all settings. Learning gain compares episodes 1–3 with 8–10 in percentage points (pp). Learning slope fits all ten episodes in normalized pp per episode. The first six rows report game-level macro-averages and PlayCraft’s differences from each comparator (§§4.1.3–4.2).

PlayCraft led both revision baselines on both aggregate metrics at every setting. Its Low-setting average slope was effectively tied with the initial games and slightly lower. These results support improved short-run agent learning under the study protocol. Human engagement, human mastery, and absolute performance comparisons across differently scored builds remain untested.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** The similarity of agent and human play and learning remains unverified. Longer active-play budgets can also increase gameplay and inference costs (§5).
- **Analyst inference.** The comparison varies several features together, including observation access, memory, and reflective reports. The reported experiments cannot isolate the contribution of each feature (§§3.2.2, 4.1.2).
- **Analyst inference.** Revisions can alter the scoring function, as in *Drone Delivery*. Separate normalization by each build’s observed maximum leaves open how much of a measured gain reflects better gameplay under a common outcome measure (§§4.2, 4.4).
- **Analyst inference.** Ten browser games evaluated with short, inference-paused episodes provide limited evidence about longer games or uninterrupted real-time play (§§4.1–4.1.1).

## Open research questions

1. **Do the revised builds improve human mastery and engagement?** The study evaluates agents only (§5). Randomly assign human players to initial or revised builds and measure repeated-play performance, perceived mastery, and willingness to replay.
2. **Which parts of the play record drive better revisions?** The comparisons change several evidence sources together (§4.1.2). Hold the visual interface and revision budget fixed, then vary cross-episode memory and the inclusion of reflective reports independently.
3. **Do the gains persist under shared gameplay measures?** Build-specific scoring and normalization limit direct comparison (§§4.2, 4.4). Evaluate the same builds with common task outcomes where feasible, such as objectives completed and time to completion, alongside the reported score metrics.

## Confidence and missing information

**Medium.** The supplied prose specifies the protocol and aggregate effects, but Table 1’s entries, the full per-game values from Tables 2–3, run-level outcomes, and uncertainty estimates are unavailable. The supplied text also leaves code, build, and play-record release status unspecified.
