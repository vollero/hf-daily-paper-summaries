# Inhabitant: An Agent Framework for Long-Horizon Human-Scene Interaction with Spatial Control and Quantitative Feedback

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.25005) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25005)


## One-sentence takeaway

Inhabitant uses spatial constraints and simulator measurements to revise whole-body motion before committing it, completing 74 of 75 simulated household tasks with Claude Opus 5.5 versus 26.7% success for the best adapted baseline using the same model (Table 2).

## Problem and motivation

Long-horizon human–scene interaction requires a character to carry out a sequence of actions from a single instruction in a changing 3D scene. A missed grasp can block every subsequent step. The authors identify two interface gaps in prior agents: text prompts and fixed skills offer limited control over where the body moves, while rendered frames alone leave the agent to estimate how far the motion missed its target (§1, Fig. 1).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Closed-loop spatial control.** A language-model agent writes root waypoints, hand or foot targets, and full-body keyframes directly for the pretrained Kimodo motion generator. After each simulated try, it can query body and scene measurements in the same coordinates as the controls, revise the candidate from the last committed state, commit it, or roll back earlier segments. The contribution is the coupling of these interfaces in a training-free framework (§3, Fig. 2).
2. **Inhabit benchmark.** The authors introduce 75 household tasks across 31 furniture layouts of one ReplicaCAD apartment. Tasks use generated whole-body motion for placement, throwing, moving objects with a foot, operating articulated furniture, and taking postures. Task-specific rules judge outcomes, alongside metrics for motion quality and cost (§4, Fig. 3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Inhabitant leads the adapted baselines | On all 75 Inhabit tasks, with Claude Opus 5.5 shared across methods, success was 98.7% (74/75) for Inhabitant, 26.7% for InteractAgent, 20.0% for HumanCLAW, and 6.7% for GEMS. | §5.2, Table 2 |
| Success varies across language models | On the same 75 tasks with Inhabitant, success was 93.3% with Claude Sonnet 5.5, 82.7% with Gemini 4 Argon, 44.0% with GLM-5.3-Flash, and 30.7% with gpt-6-luna. | Table 2 |
| Root waypoints account for most of the spatial-control gain | On the 20-task Opus ablation with feedback unchanged, text-only control solved 4/20 tasks, adding root waypoints raised this to 18/20, and adding end-effector targets raised it to 19/20. Adding full-body keyframes kept the aggregate at 19/20. | §5.3, Tables 3 and 18 |
| Measurements reduce retries and collisions | On the 20-task Opus ablation with full spatial control and the success check, removing both measurement types reduced success from 19/20 to 17/20, increased tries by about 50%, and roughly doubled body collisions. | §5.4, Tables 4 and 19 |
| Removing the success check reduces completion | With Opus and all measurements on the 75 tasks, success fell from 98.7% to 86.7%. Of the 72 episodes the agent declared complete without the check, 65 satisfied the task rule. | §5.4, Appendix D.4, Table 20 |
| Revising before committing improves the reported full-control results | On the 20-task Opus ablation, immediate commitment in Single-Try solved four fewer tasks and produced about four times as many body collisions as the full loop. Open-Loop solved 0/20. | §5.5, Table 5 |
| A physics controller tracks most eligible motion windows | PHC+ tracked 161/171 windows from the Opus runs without falling. The windows covered 73% of frames and were replayed on a flat floor with furniture and objects removed. | §5.5, Appendix D.6, Table 22 |

The evidence supports a task-completion advantage over the adapted baselines under the Inhabit protocol. The ablations attribute most of the completion gain to spatial control and show that measurements also reduce retries and collisions. Baseline episodes often end early and return shorter sequences, limiting direct comparisons of motion quality and cost. The tracking study leaves end-to-end physics-driven object interaction untested (Table 2, Appendix D.6).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** The framework relies on repeatable trials from a saved simulator state, favoring offline applications. The simulated body lacks balance, torque control, and fingers, and grasping is rule-based (§6).
- **Author-stated.** The agent receives structured simulator state and normally gets a binary success check after each try. Perception-based state estimation remains future work (§6).
- **Author-stated.** Each task was run once, most ablations used a selected 20-task subset, and the comparisons used adapted baseline implementations (§§5.1, 6, Appendix C.2).
- **Analyst inference.** Layout variation within one apartment leaves transfer to other homes and longer goal chains unmeasured. Single runs leave run-to-run uncertainty unresolved, particularly for ablations where a small number of outcomes changes the reported rate (§4, §5.1).
- **Analyst inference.** Three success-rule corrections were applied to recorded runs after execution. Because the original checks could influence retries or stopping, rescoring leaves open how the agents would behave with corrected checks during execution (Appendix B.4).

## Open research questions

1. **How much performance survives perception errors?** The agent receives structured state even when the success check is removed. Replace that state with estimates from rendered observations and compare task success, missed grasps, and premature stopping on the same tasks (§5.4, §6).
2. **Can the loop complete tasks with a physics-driven body?** PHC+ was tested on isolated windows without furniture or objects. Run complete contact-rich tasks with a torque-driven controller and compare task success, falls, and grasp failures against half-physics (§5.5, Appendix D.6).
3. **Does the advantage persist across new homes, longer instructions, and repeated runs?** The benchmark uses one apartment and one run per task. Repeat matched-budget comparisons across distinct apartments and longer goal chains, reporting variation across runs (§4, §6).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the main benchmark results as reported. The supplied text includes Table 2 and per-task outcomes for key ablations. Several tables appear only as captions, including Tables 3–5, so the exact per-setting means for retries, collisions, and missed grasps are unavailable beyond values stated in the prose. The evaluation supplies no repeated-run estimates, results from the original baseline implementations, or tests in distinct apartments.
