# AltRA-Test: An Alternative Annotator Test for LLM Judges Without Shared Annotators

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.26073) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26073)


## One-sentence takeaway

AltRA-Test uses paired bootstrap resampling to test whether replacing one human label per item with an LLM label preserves inter-annotator agreement within an adaptive margin, even when different people label different items (§3.1).

## Problem and motivation

Human labels often define the target for subjective tasks, so replacing a human annotator requires evidence that the LLM preserves the signal shared by the human pool. Agreement with a majority-vote label is hard to interpret because individual humans also disagree with that label. The authors identify a gap in prior replacement tests, Alt-Test and the Statistical Turing Test (STT), which require shared annotators across items. Eleven of the 20 evaluated datasets did not meet the prior tests’ requirements (§§1–2, Appendix E.2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Identity-free replacement test.** Resample items with replacement, draw \(k\) human labels per item without replacement, and swap one randomly selected label for the LLM label. Compare agreement in the paired human-only and mixed panels. The judge passes when the lower bootstrap quantile of the margin-adjusted agreement gap is positive (§3.1, Figure 1, Algorithm 1).
- **Adaptive margin and theoretical guarantee.** The allowable agreement loss scales with observed human disagreement and inversely with panel size. The simulation-calibrated default permits a 10% relative increase in disagreement at the calibration panel size of \(k=3\). The authors establish consistency and asymptotic false-pass control under regularity conditions (§§3.1, 4.1–4.2, Appendix C.3).
- **Variants for annotator perspectives.** Group-wise testing checks replacement separately within labeled groups. Eq-AltRA-Test bounds agreement changes in both directions when preserving variation matters (§3.2).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Better simulation trade-off than prior tests | In the shared-annotator simulation with 200 items and \(k=3\), margin sweeps gave AltRA-Test higher better-model pass rates than Alt-Test and STT-Alt at matched worse-model pass rates below 0.05. At \(c=0.1\), the rates were 0.98 and 0.01, respectively, averaged over the corresponding noise conditions. | §4.2, Figure 2, Appendix D.1, Figure 6 |
| Stable pass rates under sparse assignment | With 200 simulated items, \(k=3\), and \(c=0.1\), varying each human annotator’s workload from one item to all items yielded better-model pass rates of at least 0.98 and worse-model pass rates of at most 0.02. | §4.3, Appendix D.3, Figure 9(b) |
| Replacement was supported for a minority of evaluated pairs | Across 20 datasets and 18 judges, using 300 items per dataset and Krippendorff’s \(\alpha\), 71 of 201 judge–dataset pairs passed under chain-of-thought prompting (35.3%). The reported count rose to 95 of 201 with the best-prompt setting (47.3%). Mixed-panel agreement exceeded human-only agreement in 49 of the 71 chain-of-thought passes. | §§5.1–5.2, Tables 1–2, Appendix E.1 |
| Smaller samples often reproduced a passing verdict | Among real-world configurations that passed at 300 items, the median of the per-judge average minimum passing sample sizes was 111 items. | §5.2, Table 2, Appendix E.3 |
| Pass decisions largely tracked gold-label accuracy on CIFAR-10H | On 300 items, mean human accuracy was 0.94. Eight of nine model–prompt combinations that met or exceeded human accuracy passed, and zero of 36 less accurate combinations passed. | §5.2, Appendix E.1, Table 7 |
| Pooled passes could conceal subgroup failures | On DICES-350 with each judge’s best prompt, four judges passed with pooled annotations. Only Gemma-3-12B passed across all 13 tested demographic groups. The other three pooled passers failed in the Latinx group. | §5.3, Appendix B.3 |

The simulations measure replacement decisions against known differences in label noise. The real-world results mainly establish agreement relative to the observed human pool, with direct accuracy validation reported for CIFAR-10H. The sample-size result is conditional on passing at 300 items, and the best-prompt pass rate describes the evaluated prompt choices. Neither figure establishes performance on fresh items under a prospective deployment protocol.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Shared human–LLM errors escape detection, and low human agreement can let uninformative judges pass. In the low-agreement simulation, random judges began passing below human IAA of 0.1. The authors recommend applying the test at human IAA of at least 0.2 (§4.4, Appendix D.3, §6).
- **Author-stated.** Pooled agreement can mask minority perspectives. Group-wise testing requires enough labels per item from each group, and the main test uses a single LLM label per item (§§3.2, 5.3, 6).
- **Analyst inference.** The average simulated worse-model pass rate can hide harder conditions. At the default margin, one noise condition had a worse-model pass rate of 0.08 because its agreement loss fit within the margin. Ground-truth accuracy and the agreement-based null therefore answer different questions (Appendix D.1, Figure 6(b)).

## Open research questions

1. **How well does agreement-based replacement predict accuracy across domains?** Direct gold-label validation is reported for CIFAR-10H. Compare pass decisions against independently adjudicated labels on additional tasks with varying human agreement (§5.2, Appendix E.1).
2. **How well do smaller evaluation sets reproduce decisions on fresh items?** The sample-size analysis uses passing 300-item verdicts as its reference. Fix the prompt on a development split, then estimate pass and fail replication on independent evaluation samples of different sizes (§5.2, Appendix E.3).
3. **Can repeated LLM labels capture the range of human perspectives?** The main test uses one model label per item, and the authors leave distributional comparison open. Collect repeated model labels and compare their per-item distributions with pooled and group-specific human label distributions (§§3.2, 6).
