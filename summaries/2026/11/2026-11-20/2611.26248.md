# TwinLab-Bench: Evaluating AI Scientists in Agentic Twins of Real Labs

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.26248) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26248)


## One-sentence takeaway

TwinLab-Bench tests whether AI agents can design budgeted experiments and defend their conclusions in twins built from real laboratory records, with the best tested model averaging 45.0 out of 100 across 52 tasks (§§3, 4.2).

## Problem and motivation

The authors argue that existing science benchmarks mainly test whether agents can follow a workflow, analyze supplied data, or choose measurements from predefined candidates. Evaluating whether an agent can design its own experiments requires an environment that can answer those requests with credible readings while tracking costs and scarce materials. TwinLab-Bench addresses that gap using laboratory records and locally calibrated calculators (§§1–3, Figure 2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Benchmark and scoring.** The 52 tasks cover claim review, troubleshooting, and research campaigns. Agents receive a handoff drawn from the record at a defined cutoff. A judge grades their final reports against hidden answers and checks whether the run produced supporting evidence. A core rubric item that falls short of full credit sets the task score to zero. The authors report releasing the tasks and evaluation code (§§3.1, 3.3, 5, 14).
- **Laboratory twins.** A task-specific environment agent retrieves recorded readings where applicable and uses deterministic calculators for nearby conditions with a documented basis. It tracks state and resource use and flags unsupported measurements as simulation limits (§3.2, §13.1).
- **Empirical evaluation.** The authors compare 13 actor models, audit twin fidelity and memorization, and examine where Opus 5.5 loses credit between planning, experimentation, interpretation, and reporting (§§4, 8–10).

| The twin improved replay fidelity | On expert-route requests from 12 tasks, DeepSeek-flash with the task description alone, with raw materials, and as the TwinLab agent deviated from recorded readings on 41%, 14%, and 2% of answers, respectively. | Readings were often grounded in the same lab’s data | In the audit of 831 first-valid runs, 53% of readings came directly from records and 23% from calculators calibrated on the same lab’s data. On three tasks with recorded conditions withheld for calculator rebuilding, median reading error was 0.74% for interpolation and 2.47% for extrapolation. | §4.3, §§9.2, 9.5 |
| Agents sometimes found alternative experimental routes | In both audit rounds, 36% of requests in successful runs had no counterpart in the expert route. A different kind of experiment partly supported a core item in 10 of 74 successful runs in the first round and 6 of 30 in the second. | §9.6 |
| Access to experiments mattered in the tested subset | On 33 tasks whose decisive data were absent from the handoff, Opus 5.5 and DeepSeek-flash each passed zero tasks with zero experimental budget. DeepSeek-flash passed 6 of the same tasks with the normal budget. With identical handoffs and altered lab states, conclusions fully followed the new state in 8 of 10 cases that allowed a verdict. | §4.4, Table 3, §§8.1, 8.3 |
| Opus 5.5 lost credit even when it planned the right action | High-effort Opus earned 87%, 66%, and 62% of available decision credit when its plan stated decisions fully, in principle, or omitted them, respectively. Of 16 zero-score runs on experiment-dependent tasks, 12 failed on experimental decisions, including 7 that accepted a mechanism without a discriminating test. | Tables 5–6, §§10.1–10.2 |

The results support the authors’ claim that the benchmark can distinguish agents on interactive experimental tasks and that their conclusions often respond to the evidence they obtain. The zero-budget result applies to two models on the selected tasks, whose original rubrics can require evidence obtained during the run. The expert replay measures whether known routes are supported by the twins. Live-laboratory transfer remains untested in the reported evaluation.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-described scope.** The twins extend records locally and flag unsupported quantities. The held-out fidelity test covers three tasks. Across 12 version-matched comparisons in the extrapolation sensitivity study, two pass verdicts changed (§§3.2, 9.4–9.5).
- **Author-reported reliability limits.** Most actor configurations ran once per task. The headline results use the first valid run, and 44 of 875 attempts counted under that procedure were invalid. A separate audit found one disclosure of hidden truth that the judge missed in a run that scored zero (§6, §§7.4, 9.2).
- **Analyst inference.** Selecting records with closed answers, usable raw data, and specific diagnoses favors problems that can be scored retrospectively. The model rankings may differ on open-ended research where the target itself evolves (§14).
- **Analyst inference.** GPT models ran in Codex and other models in Claude Code, so cross-family score differences may reflect both the model and its harness. The reported comparisons do not isolate their effects (§4.2).

## Open research questions

- **How well do twin-based conclusions transfer to live labs?** Held-out tests reuse previously recorded conditions. A prospective study could execute agent-designed requests for matched physical-science or engineering tasks in both the twin and the lab, then compare readings and conclusions (§9.5).
- **Does prompting agents to test competing explanations improve outcomes?** Failure coding identifies premature acceptance of mechanisms, but the paper does not test an intervention targeting that error. A matched-budget experiment could randomize whether agents must state a competing explanation and a discriminating test before accepting a mechanism (§10.2).
- **How much do model rankings depend on the harness?** The main evaluation varies the harness with model family. A crossover study could run compatible models in both harnesses with matched tools and budgets and repeat each condition (§4.2, §6).
