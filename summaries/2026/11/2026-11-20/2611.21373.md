# WeVisForensics: Self-Evolving Agent for Omni AI-Generated Video Detection

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.21373) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21373)


## One-sentence takeaway

WeVisForensics jointly trains a video-forensics investigator and its visual specialists, reaching 95.2% mean detection accuracy across four benchmarks versus 86.2% for VideoVeritas, the strongest external baseline in the comparison (Table 4).

## Problem and motivation

As synthetic video becomes more realistic, authenticity judgments need evidence that can be inspected. The authors argue that single-pass reasoning can miss brief or localized artifacts, fixed workflows can bury decisive cues among irrelevant reports, and training a coordinator alone leaves it dependent on what frozen specialists can detect (§1, Fig. 6).

## Approach and contributions

- **Adaptive investigation.** An investigator chooses which specialist to call, where to inspect, and when to issue a real/fake verdict. Four trainable visual inspectors cover appearance, physical consistency, commonsense plausibility, and on-screen text. Two fixed audio tools analyze speech and speaker characteristics when a soundtrack is present (§2, Table 1).
- **Dual-timescale policy optimization (DTPO).** The investigator learns on every rollout batch from verdict correctness, role coverage, and call-level feedback from a frozen reference model. The visual inspectors periodically update their LoRA adapters through distillation on calls grounded in annotated artifact intervals and revise their reporting rules through held-out checks. Parameters stay fixed at inference (§4.2, Fig. 3).
- **Role-specific training and evaluation assets.** ForenCue-30K combines scoped annotations, trajectories built from executed calls, and an accompanying reinforcement-learning pool. AgenticCue evaluates binary accuracy by artifact category and measures whether the investigator calls a relevant specialist (§3, Fig. 4).

| More selective specialist use | On AgenticCue generated clips, OCR invocation precision rose from 72.8% with SFT to 95.4% with DTPO, and commonsense precision rose from 77.1% to 99.7%. Mean executed calls per video fell from 2.50 to 2.19. | Table 5, Appendix A |
| Both training levels contributed | Mean four-benchmark accuracy was 91.3% with both components at SFT, 92.0% with only evolved inspectors, 93.2% with only the trained investigator, and 95.2% with both. | Evolved inspectors gave better evidence on matched calls | On MintVid, physical-inspector F1 rose from 72.7% with the frozen library to 91.9% with the evolved library on calls overlapping annotated intervals. The matched-call evaluation supports improved inspector reporting with the role and window held constant. The component swaps support a contribution from both training levels under the tested setup. Invocation precision measures relevance among calls made on generated clips. How often the system skips relevant roles, or calls tools unnecessarily on real clips, remains open.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated constraints.** The authors cite scarce labeled audio forgeries as a reason to keep audio tools fixed. Their visual reporting protocol also recognizes that sampling, blur, occlusion, and resolution can make a scoped judgment inconclusive (§2.2).
- **Analyst inference.** AgenticCue uses generated clips selected for verified artifacts and reuses the same real clips across categories. Its scores characterize that curated setting, while performance at deployment prevalence and on clips without clear cues remains unknown (§3.2).
- **Analyst inference.** Matched-call F1 is conditional on the selected scope overlapping an annotation, and OCR F1 excludes text-presence routing. Table 8 gives evidence-quality results for appearance, physical consistency, and OCR. The commonsense sample in the paired human review is small (§5.4, Tables 8, 10).
- **Analyst inference.** The ablations test the evolving library as a package, leaving the separate contributions of adapter distillation and skill-card revision unresolved. SFT and RL also use additional videos beyond the described core sets, leaving the exact training corpus underspecified (§§4.1, 4.4, 5.3).

## Open research questions

1. **How many relevant cues does the investigator miss?** AgenticCue reports invocation precision, and matched-call F1 requires interval overlap. Measure role recall and temporal overlap against dense annotations while varying the call budget and frame-sampling rate.
2. **How much do skill cards add to adapter distillation?** The library ablations bundle the two updates. Compare adapter-only, card-only, and combined updates on the same rollouts, with held-out generators and matched-call human review.
3. **How robust is the audio evidence to benign mismatch?** The protocol cautions about dubbing and voice-over, while the Audio category reports aggregate detection. Compare false alarms and fake recall on matched sets of authentic dubbed or overlapping speech and synthetic voice mismatches, with and without the audio tools.

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the method summary and the tabulated benchmark comparisons. The supplied text does not establish public availability of code, checkpoints, or data, give the exact composition of the additional SFT and RL videos, or quantify run-to-run variation in the headline scores. Appendix D refers to complete skill cards, but the retrieved text contains only their summary and run headings.
