# S1CogBench: What System One Decision Models Can and Cannot Do on Cognitive Experiments

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.00911) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00911)


## One-sentence takeaway

S1CogBench shows that typed decision models vary widely across cognitive tasks, that good autonomous decisions can coexist with poor predictions of human choices, and that the largest measured speed gains came from dispensing with text generation.

## Problem and motivation

Software can immediately execute a typed decision over legal options, but a valid response gives little indication of whether the model used feedback, remembered relevant information, or accounted for future opportunities. Cognitive experiments isolate these demands by varying them directly. The authors identify a gap between cognitive benchmarks for language and multimodal models and existing typed-decision evaluations on static tasks and agent environments (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Benchmark suite.** S1CogBench adapts 24 established cognitive tasks into 27 modes. Adapters provide the visible state, available history, and legal responses. The authors preserve source-defined scoring and disclose changes such as text displays and zero-cost observation in autonomous Mouselab. They describe this as the first benchmark of typed decision models on established cognitive experiments (§§1, 3.1–3.3).
- **Behavioral evaluation protocol.** The benchmark records probabilities, native choices, executed actions, feedback, and timing. It scores human prediction and autonomous execution separately, and reports responses to experimental manipulations alongside aggregate scores (§§3.2–3.4).
- **Comparative study.** The authors evaluate five open checkpoints, Jev, and selected Claude runs. They test shared argmax and sampling readouts on Horizon and WCST, compare Intern-Decision with its Qwen backbone, and time single-pass decisions and generation (§§4.1, 4.5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Performance varies across typed decision models. | Under original readouts, Laya failed to beat source-defined uniform choice on 17 of 26 performance targets and Intern-Decision on 3 of 26. Intern-Decision outscored its Qwen3.5 backbone on 23 of those targets. | §4.2, Table 3 |
| Autonomous search and human prediction diverge in Mouselab. | On the same zero-observation-cost synthetic trees, Qwen achieved a terminal expected value of 17.40 versus 11.77 for MetaGreedy. On human choices from the original waiting-time protocol, Qwen’s NLL was 2.464 versus 1.634 for uniform choice. Laya had the lowest open-model NLL at 1.717 and achieved zero autonomous reward by stopping immediately. | §4.3, Figure 2, Appendix C |
| Pooled scores conceal sensitivity to future choices and memory load. | On the first unequal-information free choice in Horizon, humans selected the less-observed arm 10.00 percentage points more often at horizon 6 than at horizon 1. Model predictions under the same human histories shifted by −0.49 to +1.18 points. In RLWM under complete human-block histories, Qwen lost 20.34 accuracy points from set size 2 to 6, versus 11.37 for Intern-Decision and 12.74 for humans. | §4.4, Figure 3a, Table 7 |
| The largest measured latency gap is between single-pass scoring and generation. | In the Horizon follow-up with a 4,096-token generation cap, median generation versus single-pass times were 2.958 s versus 36.97 ms for Llama and 16.330 s versus 51.66 ms for Qwen. With the same single-pass readout on one A100 at batch size one, Intern-Decision and Qwen had similar median end-to-end latency ratios to Llama across matched requests, at 0.75–1.41× and 0.71–1.40× respectively. | §4.5, Table 8 |
| API probability precision can determine the reported prediction score. | Jev’s two-decimal probabilities assigned zero mass to some observed human choices, yielding infinite NLL on five of its nine prediction targets under the source scoring rules. | §§3.3, 4.3, Appendix A |

The evidence supports evaluating autonomous performance, human prediction, and condition sensitivity separately. The Mouselab modes differ in observation costs and use unpaired decisions, so the size of the observed gap cannot be attributed to the evaluation objective alone. The latency experiments show a large generation penalty for the two timed models and similar single-pass timing for one backbone pair. Broader effects of decision training on speed remain unresolved.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** The suite covers three typed decision models, and its ten task domains are organizational groupings. Text conversion, supplied histories, posterior summaries in Hybrid sampling, and free observation in autonomous Mouselab change what the models face. Checkpoint training data, compute, and readouts differ, and public descriptions cannot rule out training overlap with cognitive datasets. API comparisons use different serving paths and settings, and some human samples are small (§3.2, §4.5, Limitations).
- **Analyst inference.** Main-table rankings reflect both checkpoint behavior and execution rules. In Horizon and WCST, changing to a shared readout changes the top autonomous model in reported comparisons. The study reports these readout controls for those two tasks, leaving the sensitivity of full-suite rankings unresolved (§§4.1–4.3).
- **Analyst inference.** Some diagnostics reveal behavior without identifying its mechanism. In recent probes, the prompt exposes the current letter set, allowing the fitted reference to answer correctly without demonstrating working memory (Appendix C).

## Open research questions

1. **Does the Mouselab performance–prediction gap persist when costs and inputs are matched?** The reported modes differ in observation costs and use unpaired decisions. Replay a common set of trees and decision states under matched observation costs to measure both human-choice NLL and autonomous value.
2. **How much of Intern-Decision’s performance advantage comes from decision training across backbones?** The paper compares one backbone pair, and the other checkpoints differ in training data and compute. Compare base and decision-tuned checkpoints across several backbones with matched readouts and documented training exposure.
3. **What explains the weak Horizon exploration effect?** The human-history probe shows weak sensitivity to the number of remaining choices, but leaves open whether it comes from how the horizon is presented or how the model values information. Vary horizon and information asymmetry independently, then compare probability shifts and autonomous choices under a shared readout.

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported comparisons reproduced above. The supplied text gives captions without the full rows of most tables, including the complete task inventory and target-by-model score matrix. It also refers to repository materials for full curves, per-mode definitions, and per-target API records. Training-set overlap and Jev’s latency on matched hardware remain unresolved.
