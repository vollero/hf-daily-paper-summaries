# Phantom Success, Silent Failure: Auditing the Success Checkers of Simulated Manipulation Benchmarks

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.25179) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25179)


## One-sentence takeaway

SimAudit found 61 verified mismatches between task instructions and success checkers across three simulated manipulation benchmarks, and scoring the same policy rollouts with repaired checkers changed up to 9.1% of one policy’s episode verdicts (§4, Tables 1–2).

## Problem and motivation

Simulated manipulation benchmarks rank policies using success rates derived from hand-written checkers. An expert demonstration shows that a checker accepts one reference execution. Learned policies can also stop prematurely or complete the task in another valid way, exposing errors that expert validation misses. The authors identify checker agreement with task instructions as an underexamined source of benchmark error, alongside task design and simulator fidelity (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Error definitions and taxonomy.** A *phantom success* credits an execution that fails to satisfy the instruction. A *silent failure* denies credit to an execution that satisfies it. The authors group defects by specification, measurement, and timing (§3.1, Appendix A).
- **SimAudit.** An LLM agent separately interprets the instruction and checker code, proposes mismatches, and stages targeted probes in the unmodified simulator. A separate vision-language model reviews multi-view frames and checker verdicts without seeing the code. The audit counts visually clear disagreements on reachable states and deduplicates repeated instances of the same flaw within a task (§3.2, Fig. 2).
- **Repairs and policy re-scoring.** The authors document defects across three benchmarks, repair measurements and task conditions, require success to persist for one second, and compare original and repaired verdicts on the same released-policy rollouts (§§3.3, 4.4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Audit yield | Across 142 tasks, 183 of 568 proposed cases returned the predicted checker verdict. Visual review and deduplication yielded 61 distinct defects across 49 tasks, comprising 40 phantom successes and 21 silent failures. | §1, §4.2, Table 1 |
| Error directions differed by benchmark | Phantom successes / silent failures among verified defects were 20 / 11 in RoboTwin 2.0, 17 / 1 in RoboCasa365, and 3 / 9 in RoboDojo. | §4.2, Table 1 |
| Human checks supported visual review | Two independent annotators each confirmed all 61 reported defects. They found zero valid defects in a random sample of 50 rejected candidates. | §4.3 |
| Repaired checkers accepted expert executions | The repaired checkers accepted 1,233 expert episodes. The authors also describe using the 61 defect cases as a regression suite. | §3.3 |
| Largest episode-level change was on RoboDojo | For VPP on the 11 defect-bearing RoboDojo tasks, 9.1% of episodes flipped on paired re-scoring. Success rose from 30.0% to 39.1%, and 13.0% of original failures gained credit. | §4.4, Table 2, Appendix E |
| Re-scoring changed scores and policy gaps | Success rates fell for affected policies on RoboTwin 2.0 and RoboCasa365 and rose for the evaluated RoboDojo policies. The authors report unchanged policy ordering across all three evaluated settings. | §4.4, Table 2 |

Human review supports the reported defect cases, and paired re-scoring shows that checker changes can alter measured performance on the same policy trajectories. The targeted probes leave the overall prevalence of checker errors unknown. The re-scoring results apply to the evaluated tasks and policies, with RoboCasa365 and RoboDojo restricted to tasks where the audit found defects.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-described scope.** Defects require a visually clear disagreement on a state reached by robot or expert motion, or on a pose that settles after an object is moved and released. The policy re-evaluation allows execution to continue after the original checker first signals success, so the repaired checker can credit a later completion (§§3.1–3.2, 4.4).
- **Analyst inference.** Annotator agreement supports the reported cases. Sampling rejected candidates tests one part of the review stage, while defects the agent never proposed or successfully staged remain unmeasured (§§3.2, 4.3).
- **Analyst inference.** The one-second persistence rule may affect valid actions whose completion is brief. The supplied text reports expert acceptance and gives examples of transient errors, with no sensitivity comparison across persistence durations (§3.3, Appendix E).

## Open research questions

1. **How many defects does targeted generation miss?** The human review sampled candidates the pipeline had already generated and staged. Independently audit a stratified task sample, then measure overlap and unique defects from each approach.
2. **How sensitive are policy scores to the persistence window?** The repaired checkers use one second, and the paper supplies no comparison across durations. Re-score fixed rollouts at several durations against human judgments of completion.
3. **Do the score shifts persist across the full RoboCasa365 and RoboDojo suites?** Re-scoring covered only defect-bearing tasks in those benchmarks. Run paired, human-checked evaluation on representative tasks across each suite and compare episode flips, aggregate scores, and policy ordering.

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported defect counts and paired re-scoring results within the stated settings. The supplied text does not specify the audit LLM and reviewer VLM identities or configurations, provide exact rollout denominators for all policy rows, or give a separate aggregate pass count for the defect regression suite. It also supplies no artifact access link for the releases announced in §5.
