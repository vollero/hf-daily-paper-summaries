# Squoosh Bench: Evaluating UX-1 on 318 Historical Website A/B Tests

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.25200) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25200)


## One-sentence takeaway

In a blinded retrospective evaluation of 318 website A/B tests that ran longer than 21 days, UX-1 matched 83.96% of recorded outcomes versus 61.64% for the strongest general-purpose configuration, and its variant shortlist captured 118 of 137 recorded variant wins (Table 4, §5).

## Problem and motivation

Experimentation teams have limited live traffic and must decide which website changes to test first. Testing weak variants consumes traffic, while deprioritizing promising ones can leave gains undiscovered. The authors address a gap in earlier evaluations by asking whether a forecast can predict three recorded outcomes, including an inconclusive live test, and by measuring how many eventual winners a shortlist would capture or miss (§1, §5).

## Approach and contributions

- **Benchmark protocol.** The authors screened 412 private partner tests and scored the 318 that ran longer than 21 days. They assigned three outcome classes using the recorded 95% confidence interval on each test’s primary conversion metric. They masked page roles, withheld outcomes, reversed presentation order, and designated the primary run before scoring (§2.1, Table 3, §3.4).
- **Shopper simulation and system comparison.** UX-1 fine-tunes an open-source base model on human browsing data and simulates 1,000 shoppers per arm. The paper compares its outcome calls, relative-lift estimates, and order consistency with those of three general-purpose configurations. Three constant-choice rules provide classification baselines (§3.1–3.2, Tables 4 and 7).
- **Prioritization analysis.** The authors quantify the yield of a shortlist based on UX-1’s variant-win calls and identify the recorded winners outside it (§5, Table 6).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

| UX-1 led three-class outcome prediction | On 318 eligible tests, UX-1 matched 267 outcomes (83.96% accuracy, 83.40% balanced accuracy), versus Qwen3 Next 80B at 196/318 (61.64%, 60.25%) and the always-variant baseline at 137/318 (43.08%, 33.33%). | UX-1 recovered winners and inconclusive outcomes | On 250 directional tests, UX-1 matched 212 winners (84.80%), versus 137/250 (54.80%) for always-variant. | The variant shortlist concentrated recorded winners | UX-1 shortlisted 126/318 variants (39.6%). Of these, 118 won (93.7%), four lost, and four were inconclusive. The shortlist captured 118/137 recorded variant wins (86.1%), leaving 19 winners outside it. | Table 6, §5 |
| UX-1 was more consistent under order reversal | Mapped outcomes agreed across page orders in 301/318 tests (94.65%) for UX-1 versus 264/318 (83.02%) for Qwen3 Next 80B. Agreement included matching inconclusive calls. | Table 4, §4.5 |
| UX-1 had the lowest lift error on the shared numeric subset | On 289 tests with usable numeric estimates for all four systems, signed relative-lift MAE was 1.43 percentage points for UX-1, 3.27 for Gemini 3.5 Flash, 3.81 for Qwen3 Next 80B, and 4.12 for GPT-OSS 120B. | §4.4, Table 7 |

The authors interpret the shortlist results as support for prioritizing live tests. The evidence measures retrospective agreement with recorded decisions under the evaluated systems’ respective budgets. Prospective gains from using the shortlist and the separate effect of fine-tuning remain unestablished. An inconclusive label means the recorded interval included zero (§2.1, §3.2, §5).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors:** The duration cutoff excludes short tests, including some that reveal effects quickly. The extent of training-site overlap is unknown, and positive correlation among errors from the same site could make the reported intervals too narrow (§2.1, §4.3, §5).
- **Authors:** The comparison measures complete systems at their respective budgets, so it cannot isolate fine-tuning. The shared numeric subset may understate lift error across the corpus if forecasts were more often missing on difficult tests (§3.2, §4.4).
- **Analyst inference:** Because every system received both page evidence and pre-experiment design information, the reported results leave their separate predictive contributions unresolved. The private corpus and Squoosh’s role as both developer and evaluator also limit what readers can independently verify from the supplied materials (§2.1, §3.2–3.3, §5–6).

## Open research questions

- **Does the shortlist retain its yield prospectively?** The paper uses completed tests. Preregister forecasts for a new test queue, run candidates inside and outside the shortlist, and measure win rate and winner capture (§5).
- **How much does browsing-data fine-tuning contribute?** The comparison varies complete system configurations. Compare UX-1 with its untuned base while holding page evidence, simulation procedure, and budget fixed (§3.1–3.2).
- **How well does performance transfer to unseen sites and shorter tests?** Training-site overlap is unknown, and short tests were excluded. Evaluate on verified held-out sites, stratify by test duration, and report site-clustered uncertainty (§2.1, §4.3, §5).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported retrospective scores and denominators. The supplied text does not establish the identity of UX-1’s base model, the training corpus composition or site overlap, exact per-system resource use and adapter access, or public availability of the corpus and run logs. Site-clustered uncertainty estimates and prospective shortlist results are also unavailable (§2.1, §3.1–3.2, §4.3, §5–6).
