# WAM-Policy: World Action Models with On-Policy Distillation for Quadrupedal Loco-Manipulation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.24011) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24011)


## One-sentence takeaway

WAM-Policy adapts an arm-trained world action model to language-guided quadrupedal manipulation and distills two reinforcement-learning specialists into a single policy that reaches 85.0% success versus 77.0% for the transferred teacher on QLM-Bench Standard100, with roughly 14-fold faster video-and-action sampling (Table 1).

## Problem and motivation

An arm-free quadruped can walk on three legs and manipulate objects with a front foot, avoiding the weight and complexity of an added arm. The authors identify a gap in end-to-end language control for this setting. Existing visual leg-manipulation planners use point clouds without language conditioning, while pretrained world action models expect arm-specific camera views and commands. Their slow sampling can also leave commands stale as the robot shifts during inference, and imitation leaves some tasks unreliable (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Cross-morphology transfer.** Adapt LingBot-VA to two body-mounted RGB views and a nine-dimensional command for base motion, foot position, and contact force. Fine-tune on simulated demonstrations and use the fixed RAMBO controller to execute commands (§3.1.1, Fig. 2).
2. **Few-step self-distillation.** Train a LoRA student with one video step and two action steps to match the frozen teacher’s latent plans and action chunks from the same context and noise. The default training mixes cached teacher contexts with contexts from closed-loop student rollouts (§3.1.2, Fig. 3).
3. **Specialist training and merging.** Use a window-level variant of DiffusionNFT with task-specific rewards for box pushing and door opening, then route distillation targets by task to merge the specialists and the original teacher into one few-step policy (§3.2).
4. **QLM-Bench.** Introduce ten simulated tasks and 1,000 language-annotated demonstrations generated from model-drafted, human-reviewed trajectories, retaining successful simulated executions without teleoperation (§4, Fig. 4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Distillation recovers most of the success lost at the smallest sampling budget | On Standard100, across 300 episodes per policy, success was 77.0% for the transferred teacher at 25 video / 50 action steps, 69.3% after reducing it to 1 / 2 steps without distillation, and 76.7% for OPSD at 1 / 2 steps. | Table 1, §§5.1–5.2 |
| Sampling speed improves for both students | Each distilled student sampled a chunk in 0.159 s versus 2.226 s for the transferred teacher on an RTX 5070 Ti. These timings exclude observation encoding, and each chunk covers 0.32 s of robot time. | Table 1, §G |
| Specialist gains largely survive merging | On Standard100, each specialist succeeded in all 30 episodes of its own task. The merged student achieved 90% on box pushing and 87% on door opening versus 50% and 37% for the OPSD student. Ten-task success rose from 76.7% to 85.0%. | Table 1, §5.3 |
| Context source has little effect on the benchmark mean | At 1 video / 2 action steps, cache-only, streaming-only, and mixed OPSD scored 76.3–77.0% on Standard100. Without distillation, 2 video / 4 action steps already achieved 78.0%. | §5.4, Table S1 |
| OPSD also improves a few-step arm policy | On LIBERO-Long, success rose from 91.8% without distillation to 98.2% with OPSD over 500 paired episodes. On LIBERO-Pro2.0, the task-averaged gain was 3.9 percentage points over 2,520 paired episodes, with a 95% bootstrap interval of 1.7–6.0 points. | §§D.1–D.2, Table S6 |
| Stage 1 transfers to hardware with limited success | The simulation-trained OPSD student succeeded in 7 of 30 Go2 Air trials across three tasks. The off-board closed loop took 0.66 s per 0.32 s action chunk, so the robot held the last command between chunks. | §5.5, Table 7 |

The controlled sampler comparisons support a benefit from distillation at the smallest tested step budget. The context-source ablation gives little evidence that fresh student rollouts are necessary for the Standard100 mean. Simulation pauses for inference, so the success comparisons establish policy performance under that protocol. The hardware trials establish some sim-to-real transfer for Stage 1, while end-to-end real-time control and hardware performance of the merged Stage 2 policy remain unestablished (§§5.1, 5.4–5.5).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ limitations.** Stage 2 uses custom rewards for two tasks. The quadruped evaluation uses one platform, controller, and manipulating leg, with held-out scenes drawn from known tasks. Unseen tasks and instructions remain untested. Hardware success is lower than in simulation, and end-to-end latency exceeds the action chunk duration (§H).
- **Analyst inference — baseline comparability.** The Leg-Manip reproduction uses per-task point-cloud planners and unequal demonstration coverage, with door opening scored zero because it had no demonstrations. Its aggregate comparison cannot isolate the effect of the world action model architecture (§5.2.1, §F).
- **Analyst inference — evaluation configuration.** Some Stage 2 episodes used a second workstation and a numerically different inference configuration. The two tuned tasks were evaluated under the Stage 1 configuration for the merged student, making those gains easier to interpret than small differences on other tasks (§G).
- **Analyst inference — objective comparison.** The distribution-matching variant also changes the action-loss weight and context mix, and the reported comparison uses one policy seed. It supports the tested OPSD configuration without isolating the effect of the loss (§C, Table S1).

## Open research questions

1. **How much does latency drive the sim-to-real gap?** Hardware trials combine delayed updates with a change in physical setting. Evaluate the same policy under several measured control-loop delays on matched physical tasks, then inject those delays in simulation (§5.5, §H).
2. **Can teacher routing transfer useful skills beyond each specialist’s assigned task?** The door specialist sometimes swept the block successfully, while the merged student never did and received no sweep targets from that specialist. Compare fixed routing with routing based on held-out teacher performance, and measure gains and regressions across tasks (§5.3, §F).
3. **Does the policy follow unfamiliar wording when the scene supports several actions?** Standard100 varies scenes of known tasks, and unseen instructions were outside the evaluation. Test held-out paraphrases and alternate valid commands in scenes that support multiple tasks (§5.1, §H).
