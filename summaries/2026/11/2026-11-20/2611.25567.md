# Online Mistake Detection in Instruction-Conditioned Procedural Video

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.25567) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25567)


## One-sentence takeaway

On the repartitioned EgoPER test set, a joint video-language model improved online binary mistake detection over a modular pipeline (84.1% vs. 80.7% F1 at temporal IoU ≥ 0.5), with the largest class-specific gain on slips and similar step-segmentation scores (Tables 2–4).

## Problem and motivation

A procedural assistant needs to identify which step a person attempted, when it happened, and how it deviated from the instructions while there may still be time to intervene. The authors identify a gap between prior online detectors, which do not assess actions against explicit instruction text, and instruction-conditioned methods that process the full recording before making judgments (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Online task formulation.** From egocentric video observed so far, an ordered instruction list, and prior predictions, the system identifies completed attempts and predicts their time spans and execution classes: correct, modification, slip, correction, or addition. It samples video at 1 fps, extends the current window in five-second increments when no attempt is complete, and starts the next window at the predicted action end. It determines omissions after the video from instructions with no detected attempt (§§3.1–3.2, 3.5).
- **Modular and joint designs.** The modular pipeline identifies the step, localizes it, and verifies execution on the resulting crop. The joint model returns the step, span, and class in one structured response. Both use LoRA adapters on the same Qwen3.8-27B backbone (§§3.3–3.4, 4.4).
- **Supervised EgoPER evaluation.** The authors create a task- and error-stratified repartition that makes annotated mistakes available during training and validation. They compare the designs end to end and analyze how modular performance varies with upstream correctness (§4.1, Tables 1–5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Joint prediction improves binary mistake detection | Full 58-video test set, binary macro F1 at temporal IoU ≥ 0.5: joint 84.1%, modular 80.7%, unadapted backbone 8.9%. EDA was 97.8%, 97.7%, and 88.7%, respectively. | §4.3, Table 2 |
| Both designs score above the released baseline checkpoints | On the 25 erroneous test videos, binary macro F1 at temporal IoU ≥ 0.5 was 86.2% joint, 81.3% modular, 47.3% AEM, and 27.0% ESTANet. The authors’ models trained on annotated mistakes, while the baselines trained on correct videos. | §4.2, Table 2 |
| Slips account for the largest class-specific gain | On the full test set at temporal IoU ≥ 0.5, slip F1 was 56.4% joint vs. 37.5% modular across 19 annotated slips. Correction F1 was 40.0% vs. 53.3% across five annotated corrections. Four-class macro F1 was 63.7% vs. 62.8%. | Table 3 |
| Step segmentation is similar, while joint omission IoU is higher | On the full test set, step F1 at temporal IoU ≥ 0.5 was 93.6% joint vs. 92.1% modular. End-of-video omission IoU was 95.4% vs. 79.1%, while omission hit rate (O-Acc) was 97.8% vs. 99.6%. | §4.3, Tables 2 and 4 |
| Modular components score higher when upstream predictions are correct | On the full test set, localizer F1 at temporal IoU ≥ 0.5 was 85.0% across all calls and 96.9% on upstream-correct calls. Verifier macro F1 was 66.6% and 73.3%, respectively. | Table 5, §5 Module-Level Performance |
| Joint prediction takes less model time per accepted action | Across the 58 test videos on one NVIDIA B200 GPU per video, mean model-call time per accepted action was 5.58 s joint vs. 7.63 s modular, excluding data loading and startup. | Appendix C, Table 9 |

The results support higher joint-model binary F1 under the paper’s replay protocol. The authors interpret the conditional component scores as evidence of error propagation. Those scores compare different sets of calls, so they cannot isolate how much propagation explains the joint model’s gain. The timing measurements cover model calls, leaving end-to-end alert delay and effects on recovery unresolved.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Evaluation covers five EgoPER cooking tasks on a custom split. Baseline comparisons use only 25 erroneous videos because of potential overlap on the normal videos, and differences in supervision and evaluation protocols limit the comparison. Replay does not measure live interaction, alert usefulness, or recovery (§4.2, §5 Limitations).
- **Analyst inference.** The architecture comparison also changes vision-adapter placement across components, input and generation budgets, and checkpoint-selection criteria. The observed difference therefore cannot be attributed solely to joint prediction (Appendix B, Table 7).
- **Analyst inference.** Five annotated corrections provide limited evidence for ranking recovery recognition. The binary metric also groups corrections with mistakes, so its gains do not directly indicate better discrimination between failures and recovery actions (§4.3, Table 3).

## Open research questions

1. **How much of the joint model’s gain comes from avoiding upstream errors?** Table 5 conditions on naturally correct predictions. A follow-up could rerun the verifier on identical attempts with predicted versus annotated step identities and boundaries, then compare joint and modular designs under matched adaptation settings.
2. **Does instruction grounding transfer to unfamiliar procedures?** The evaluation stays within five cooking tasks. A follow-up could hold out entire recipes during training, then test on a separately annotated non-cooking procedure using the same output schema and scoring rules (§4.1, §5 Limitations).
3. **Do alerts arrive in time to improve recovery?** Replay scores and model-call timings leave intervention outcomes open. A live study could compare monitoring with and without alerts and measure action-end-to-alert delay, false alerts, and successful recovery (§5 Limitations, Appendix C).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the reported comparisons and their stated evaluation settings. The supplied text does not establish exact split membership or release availability for the authors’ code and adapters. It also gives no run-to-run uncertainty estimates, controlled isolation of the architecture effect, or end-to-end live alert and recovery measurements.
