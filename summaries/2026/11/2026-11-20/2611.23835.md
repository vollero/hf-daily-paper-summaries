# NarrowSpec: Draft Hidden Width Need Not Match the Target in Speculative Decoding

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.23835) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23835)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across four DFlash and EAGLE-3 pairings with two target models, training drafts at half or quarter target width improved decode-only speed by 1.06–1.12× on B200 and 1.13–1.44× on GB10 versus matched full-width drafts at batch 1 and temperature 0, with pooled acceptance-length losses on B200 of at most 3.0% and 5.4%, respectively (§4.1, Tables 3–4).

## Problem and motivation

Speculative decoding saves time when the target commits enough tokens per verification step to offset the cost of drafting. Feature-conditioned drafters typically inherit the target’s residual width because they consume its hidden states. The authors identify whole-draft residual width as an understudied way to reduce iteration cost and ask how far it can shrink before acceptance losses erase the latency benefit (§1, Eq. 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Whole-draft width as a design variable.** Train DFlash and EAGLE-3 drafts from scratch at full, half, and quarter target width. Project tapped target features and token embeddings into the narrower stream while keeping depth, attention head geometry, MLP intermediate size, and vocabulary fixed within each pairing. This shrinks projections throughout the draft and leaves the draft KV cache size unchanged (§§2–3).
- **Adapter folding for serving.** DFlash composes its trained linear output adapter with the frozen target vocabulary head at load time, yielding one narrow projection that is algebraically equivalent to the two linear maps. Finite-precision rounding can change draft proposals. EAGLE-3 trains its own narrow vocabulary head and folds its input adapter into the embedding table at export (§2.3, Eq. 2, Figs. 2–3, App. B).
- **Matched-budget evaluation with latency attribution.** Compare widths within each drafter–target pairing and profile how decoder projections, vocabulary scoring, and verification contribute to the observed gains across serving configurations (§§3–7).

The experiments establish a decode-only improvement over the authors’ matched full-width drafts in the tested configurations. The authors attribute the larger GB10 gains to faster draft iterations, supported by stage timings and a DFlash–Qwen3-8B kernel profile (§7.1). The measurements leave request latency including prefill and the effect of GPU bandwidth in isolation unresolved.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Each variant uses one training seed at a matched data budget. Training to convergence or changing the budget could alter the acceptance trade-off. The authors interpret the small observed half-width acceptance increases as parity (§§3, 4.1, 9).
- **Author-stated.** The main study covers two drafter families and two targets. The context sweep stops at 32k and measures iteration latency only. The MLP-only ablation times untrained slices, and trained MLP-only and head-only comparisons remain open (§§5.3, 6, 9).
- **Analyst inference.** The cross-GPU results support portability within the tested setups. A bandwidth-only explanation remains uncertain because GB10 uses different target attention backends, while the H100 check also changes target precision and KV-cache precision (§7, Apps. H–I).
- **Analyst inference.** Pooled acceptance can conceal workload-specific regressions, as the quarter-width RAG result shows. Fixed proposal length can also affect the estimated benefit. A separate EAGLE-3–Qwen3-8B sweep found smaller gains when each width could select its best proposal length on the evaluation prompts (App. D).

## Open research questions

1. **How stable is the width–acceptance trade-off across seeds and training budgets?** The paper uses one seed per variant at a fixed data budget. Train each width with multiple seeds at several token budgets and compare acceptance length and TPOT at matched budgets and after convergence.
2. **When can component-only compression match whole-draft narrowing?** The MLP-only comparison uses untrained slices, and the head-only comparison uses measured operation costs. Train both alternatives on the same data budget and compare acceptance, TPOT, and resident draft memory.
3. **How much speedup survives on very long inputs and responses?** The context sweep measures iteration latency through 32k, and the RAG analysis identifies a regression concentrated in examples exceeding the training sequence length. Evaluate longer workloads while varying training sequence length and measuring both acceptance and TPOT.

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported direction of the pooled decode-only results within the tested configurations. The supplied text omits row-level values for several key tables, including the main timing tables, even where it provides captions and summary ranges. It also leaves run-to-run training variance and public availability of the NarrowSpec checkpoints, processed training data, and serving changes unestablished.
