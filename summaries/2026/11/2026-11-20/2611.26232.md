# Time Series Structure Learning with Local Causal Parent Discovery

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.26232) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26232)


## One-sentence takeaway

Iterative Temporal Parent Discovery (ITPD) learns time-varying causal graphs by recovering each node’s parents, with exact recovery under the stated assumptions and an independence oracle, and a favorable runtime–accuracy trade-off in simulations.

## Problem and motivation

The paper addresses recovery of a directed causal graph from multivariate time series whose causal links may change over time. Pooling time steps into a recurring graph can conceal those changes, while constraint-based discovery can require many conditional independence (CI) tests with large conditioning sets. The authors identify a gap between generic local parent discovery, which can perform redundant tests when applied to every time-indexed node, and a procedure that uses temporal structure to make those tests cheaper. (§§1, 4.2)

## Approach and contributions

- **ITPD.** Starting from the assumed lag-1 self-parent, ITPD screens earlier variables in reverse time order using causal partitioning and previously learned ancestry and parent sets. It then prunes the candidate set in forward time order using a stricter significance threshold. The resulting parent lists form a full time graph for replicated nonstationary trajectories or a window graph for a single series under an assumed stationary maximum lag. (§§4.1, 4.3.1–4.3.2, Algorithm 1)
- **IPaDL baseline.** The authors adapt Parent Discovery on Leaves to temporal graphs by applying it to each target in time order. This provides a simpler local-to-global baseline with the same asymptotic CI-test count bound as ITPD. (§4.2, Appendix C)
- **Theory, code, and ablations.** The paper gives oracle correctness proofs and a polynomial test-count bound for ITPD, reports a Lean formalization of selected results, and provides a code link. The ablations found that additional partition checks incurred extra tests with little improvement in mean conditioning-set size. (§§1, 4.3, Appendices B, D, E.3)

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Oracle correctness and polynomial test count | Theorems give exact parent sets and full DAG recovery under the stated assumptions and an independence oracle. The CI-test bound is \(O(N^2T^2)\) for replicated nonstationary series and \(O(N^2\tau_{\max}^2)\) for a stationary window. Polynomial runtime requires a polynomial-time CI test. | Theorems 4.1–4.3, Appendix B |
| Oracle efficiency | ITPD and IPaDL recovered the true graph in all 32 exact-oracle settings, covering 4,240 graphs. ITPD used fewer unique CI tests per candidate than IAMB and IPaDL and smaller mean and maximum conditioning sets than all methods in those settings. | §5.2, “Oracle Validation,” Table 2 |
| Finite-sample accuracy and efficiency diverged | In the main nonstationary linear-Gaussian Fisher-z benchmark, ITPD had higher mean common-target F1 than Lasso, PCMCI, CD-NOD, and DYNOTEARS in every setting. Against iterative IAMB, it won 2 of 12 settings, tied 4, and lost 6. Across 76 finite-data settings, it used 0.10–0.86 times IAMB’s unique CI tests per candidate, while IAMB used smaller conditioning sets. | §5.2, Tables E.2, E.3, E.5 |
| Robustness to changing edges | With 5% of edges redrawn per time step, the authors report F1 of 0.984–0.994 for ITPD versus 0.562–0.808 for PCMCI, DYNOTEARS, and CD-NOD. Figure E.2 shows the redraw sweep at in-degree \(d=2\) and \(M=2{,}000\) trajectories. | §5.2, Figure E.2 |
| Scaling | ITPD was on the mean F1–wall-clock Pareto frontier in the main benchmark and was the fastest method tested from 1,000 to 2,000 time-indexed nodes. In the Fisher-z scaling experiment with \(M=2{,}000\) and \(d=2\), ITPD maintained F1 of 0.997–0.998. | §5.2, Figures 3–4 |
| Semi-synthetic results were mixed | On re-simulated DREAM4 networks 3 and 5, ITPD beat IAMB in mean F1 in all six settings. Against the baseline with the highest mean F1 in each setting, ITPD scored lower in four, tied in one, and scored higher in one. | §5.2, Table E.9 |

The oracle experiments support the reported correctness and test-efficiency claims within the specified model. The finite-sample results support a runtime–accuracy trade-off for the tested simulations. Some comparators pool time steps into one window graph, so their nonstationary results reflect that modeling restriction. Accuracy leadership varies by comparator and setting, and recovery on measured real-world series remains unverified. (§§5.1–5.3)

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ limitations.** A lack of suitable public benchmarks prevented real-data evaluation. ITPD excludes contemporaneous links and latent confounding after the first observed step, and the authors identify relaxing these restrictions as future work. The Lean formalization verifies graph-level results with Boolean oracle answers. Its scope excludes finite-sample test calibration and the Python implementation. (§§4.3.1, 5.3, Appendix D.4)
- **Analyst inference.** Because the lag-1 self-edge is inserted without a test and later screening uses earlier learned parent sets, absent self-edges or early estimation mistakes can affect downstream recovery. Oracle correctness gives no estimate of the size of that effect. (Algorithm 1, Appendix B.1)
- **Analyst inference.** F1 on common targets can obscure performance on later time steps when only early targets are shared. Some scaling comparisons also use runtimes from separate runs or fitted estimates at sizes where a method was not run. (Figure 4, Tables E.5 and E.9)

## Open research questions

1. **How much does recovery degrade when lag-1 self-edges are absent?** The method inserts those edges unconditionally, and the supplied text gives only captions for the assumption-violation tables. A next experiment could vary the fraction of missing self-edges and compare fixed versus tested initialization on full-graph F1 and runtime. (Algorithm 1, Tables E.7–E.8)
2. **Can local screening remain efficient with same-time effects or latent confounding?** The correctness results rely on restrictions that exclude these cases after the initial step. A next experiment could vary the two violations independently and compare lagged-edge precision, recall, and CI-test counts for an extended procedure. (§§4.3.1, 5.3)
3. **Does the runtime–accuracy trade-off hold on measured trajectories?** The evaluation uses simulations and re-simulated networks. A next experiment could benchmark aligned replicate trajectories with independently validated edges and score recovery separately at early and late time steps. (§5.3, Table E.9)

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the method description, assumptions, and reported comparisons. Several tables appear only as captions, so the supplied text does not establish their full per-setting values or intervals, including the magnitude of assumption-violation effects and which large-graph timing comparisons were directly measured. The paper reports a code release and Lean results, but the supplied text alone cannot verify a reproduction.
