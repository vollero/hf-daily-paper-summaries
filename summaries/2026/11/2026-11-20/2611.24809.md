# On-Policy Morphing into Hybrid Attention Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-20.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.24809) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24809)


## One-sentence takeaway

FlashMorph2 selects which layers retain full attention by testing discrete hybrid architectures while adapting their shared weights, improving average long-context and recall scores over FlashMorph after identical recovery training (Tables 1–2).

## Problem and motivation

Converting a pretrained Transformer to hybrid attention can reduce attention computation and cache use, but under a fixed budget, the placement of the remaining full-attention layers affects how much long-context capability survives. Exhaustively recovering and evaluating every placement is prohibitively expensive. The authors identify two mismatches in FlashMorph’s selection proxy: it mixes attention branches on teacher-provided hidden states, and it ranks layers using weights frozen before recovery (§§1, 3.2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Discrete architecture evaluation.** FlashMorph2 uses Gumbel-Top-K sampling to choose exactly the budgeted number of full-attention layers. Each sampled architecture runs end to end with its own hidden states, so earlier branch choices can affect later layers (§4.1).
- **Weight adaptation during selection.** It jointly updates layer scores and shared model weights using the same teacher–student logits distillation loss used in recovery. A budget-preserving straight-through estimator supplies gradients for the discrete choices. After selection, recovery starts from the original aligned checkpoint, so the comparison isolates the selected architecture from the weights learned during search (§§4.2–4.3).
- **Comparative evaluation.** The paper tests selection quality under matched recovery settings across attention variants, model sizes, and full-attention budgets, and reports selection costs and component ablations (§5, Appendix C).

The matched initialization, attention budget, and recovery support a comparison of **layer-selection methods** within the tested settings. Gains are clearest at the suite-average level. Individual tasks can regress, and the full-attention backbones retain higher RULER averages (Tables 1, 7). The GPU-hour measurements cover selection. Deployment latency, throughput, and peak memory are unmeasured in the supplied results.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ stated trade-offs.** The straight-through gradient estimator is biased and requires evaluating both attention branches to compute the mask gradient. Selection therefore costs more than FlashMorph’s selection stage (§§4.3, 5.3).
- **Analyst inference — scope and baseline coverage.** Quality comparisons are confined to Qwen3. The larger-model results use Lightning Attention only, and PostNAS is absent from the 0.6B and larger-model result tables. The reported wins apply to the baselines evaluated in each setting (Tables 1–2, 7–9).
- **Analyst inference — component attribution.** The ablation covers one backbone–attention pair, and removing both components performs better than either single removal in that setting. The table supports each component’s value in the complete method for that configuration, while leaving their interaction and generality unresolved (Table 3).
- **Analyst inference — reporting precision.** The supplied tables give point estimates without run-to-run uncertainty. The efficiency paragraph also points to Fig. 4, while the captions identify Fig. 3 as the cost plot and Fig. 4 as the ratio sweep (§5.3, Figs. 3–4).

## Open research questions

1. **Do the selection gains transfer beyond Qwen3?** Every tested backbone belongs to Qwen3. Repeat the matched-budget comparison on another pretrained model family using the same attention variants and recovery protocol (§5.1, Appendix C).
2. **How do discrete computation and weight adaptation interact?** Table 3 covers one configuration, and the joint removal outperforms either single removal. Run a controlled factorial ablation across attention variants and random seeds, and report both recovered scores and placement stability.
3. **How well does shared-weight search predict the ranking after recovery?** The paper evaluates the chosen architectures without reporting rank agreement across sampled candidates. Fully recover a tractable set of masks under identical settings and compare their search-time and post-recovery rankings (§§3.2, 4.2).
4. **Does layer placement affect serving cost at a fixed attention budget?** The timing analysis measures selection. Benchmark prefill latency, decoding throughput, and peak memory across recovered placements at matched batch sizes and context lengths (§5.3).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the method description and the direction of the reported suite-average gains under matched recovery. The supplied text does not establish run-to-run variability, exact RULER context lengths, hardware and full per-configuration timing details, measured serving performance, or code and checkpoint release status.
