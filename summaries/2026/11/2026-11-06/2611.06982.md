# Decode Before You Hear: Speculative Listening for Chunk-Interleaved Full-Duplex Speech Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.06982) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06982)


## One-sentence takeaway
Speculative Listening uses idle listening time to draft future outputs under assumed silence, accelerating verified decoding across four chunk-interleaved speech models while preserving autoregressive outputs in exact arithmetic (§§3, 5).

## Problem and motivation
Chunk-interleaved full-duplex speech models must process each incoming audio chunk before the next arrives. Listening leaves substantial compute idle, while speaking and tool calls require serial token generation that can delay responses and accumulate lag. The authors identify an unused opportunity: decoding the next chunk during this idle time, before its audio is available, rather than applying speculative decoding only after input arrives (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Empirical motivation:** An oracle probe compares outputs under real audio and silence. Outputs usually match, and mismatches predominantly start at the first output token (§3.2).
- **Speculative Listening:** The unchanged target model drafts under silence during the idle window. After audio arrives, a forward pass jointly prefills the real input and verifies the draft. A session-wide hypothesis pool reuses earlier drafts; a branch beginning with the runner-up first token covers alternative listen/speak decisions. Verification preserves greedy outputs in exact arithmetic; speculative sampling preserves the target distribution (§3.3).
- **TickTock-Bench:** A unified benchmark and replay harness evaluates decoding against chunk deadlines across speech reasoning, conversational dynamics, and tool use. The authors state that session manifests, the harness, and evaluation scripts are released (§4; Appendix A).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Silence often predicts the complete output | Oracle probe pooled across four models: **95.3%** of chunks match; **87.7%** of mismatched chunks diverge at the first token. Main probe uses Llama Questions; tool-call probe uses FDB v3. | §3.2; Figure 3; Table 7 |
| Faster speak and tool-call decoding | Mean speak-chunk speedups over AR across seven non-agentic tasks: **2.76×, 2.60×, 2.69×, 2.68×** for Streaming SLM, Gander, Realtime-Venus, and LWS. FDB v3 tool-call speedups: **23.41×** and **3.45×** for Streaming SLM and Gander. One A100, one-second chunks, greedy decoding, batch size one; Thinker only. | Table 2; §§4–5.2 |
| Lower accumulated lag | On FDB v3, session-mean maximum lag falls from **568→0 ms**, **444→42 ms**, **1→0 ms**, and **430→55 ms**, respectively. Authors report **94.4%** average reduction. | Table 3; §5.2 |
| Earlier output readiness | Across eight tasks, mean response-onset decoding latency falls **417→74 ms**, **763→228 ms**, **565→241 ms**, and **1089→204 ms**, respectively; authors report **73%** average reduction. FDB v3 tool-call latency falls **2623→112 ms** and **1827→513 ms** for Streaming SLM and Gander. These metrics include accumulated lag. | Table 5; §5.4; Appendix A |
| Earlier drafts matter for long calls | On Streaming SLM/FDB v3, tool-call speedup rises from **1.42×** with silence drafting alone to **22.04×** with the pool and **23.41×** with the runner-up branch. Corresponding Gander results: **1.21×→2.04×→3.45×**. | Table 4 |
| Gains persist in limited sampling and concurrency tests | Streaming SLM/FDB v3: at sampling temperature **1.0**, speak/tool-call speedups are **1.4×/5.4×**. With **four concurrent sessions** on one A100, speedups are **2.0×/10.5×**, and tool-call deadline misses fall **100%→2.9%**. | Figure 4; §5.4; Appendix B.4 |
| Practical output preservation is approximate | Float32 reproduces AR tokens on “nearly all” sessions, without an exact rate. On **100 AlpacaEval sessions**, bfloat16 judge-score differences are **+0.02 to +0.07** on a **1–5** scale, using DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash. | §5.4; Appendix B.1; Table 6 |

The evidence supports substantial reductions in post-audio decoding latency under the tested settings. It does not establish complete microphone-to-audible-response latency: speech synthesis is excluded, and response onset is measured from the arrival of the first speak chunk’s audio, rather than directly from the user’s turn end. Exact-arithmetic guarantees also do not imply bitwise equality in the reported bfloat16 runs.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Benefits target low-concurrency, latency-sensitive serving; shared GPU contention reduces drafting time and speedup. Parallel-stream architectures require a different formulation. Talker synthesis is excluded (Limitations).
- **Analyst inference:** The pooled silence-match rate hides substantial model dependence. Realtime-Venus matches only **37.9%** of user-speech chunks in the probe, compared with much higher rates for the other models; the pooled statistic should not be assumed universal (Table 7).
- **Analyst inference:** Sampling and concurrent-serving analyses cover Streaming SLM on FDB v3, limiting evidence for those settings across model families (Figure 4).
- **Analyst inference:** Small judge-score differences support similar measured quality, but do not establish statistical equivalence or universal output identity. No uncertainty estimates accompany Table 6.
- **Analyst inference:** Fixed-audio replay demonstrates decoding behavior under controlled inputs; it does not establish how earlier responses alter live human interaction (§4).

## Open research questions
1. **Can better input hypotheses improve difficult chunks?** The authors leave alternatives to digital silence for future work (§6). Compare silence with streaming-acoustic hypotheses, measuring acceptance, drafting cost, and latency.
2. **Where does concurrency erase the advantage?** Tests reach only four concurrent sessions on one A100 (Appendix B.4). Sweep larger concurrency and batching configurations while measuring latency, throughput, and deadline misses.
3. **How much benefit reaches audible conversation?** Talker timing and live interaction are outside the main evaluation (Limitations; §4). Run integrated speech synthesis and live-dialogue experiments measuring user-turn-end to audible response.
4. **How robust is practical losslessness?** Float32 matches “nearly all” sessions, while bfloat16 can change outputs (§5.4). Report exact mismatch rates across precisions and tasks, with paired quality evaluation.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported decoding improvements within the evaluated scope; **medium** for broader deployment and user-experience conclusions.

Material information unavailable in the supplied text includes release URLs, exact token-mismatch rates, statistical uncertainty, full speech-synthesis latency, energy and total-compute costs, hypothesis-pool memory growth, and results beyond the tested hardware and concurrency range.
