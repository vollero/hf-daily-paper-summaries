# Signed but Steerable: Measuring and Closing the Text-Vector Integrity Gap in Offline Retrieval-Augmented Generation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.06498) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06498)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors show that tolerance-based embedding checks permit predictable retrieval manipulation, while verifier-controlled code canonicalisation removes the publisher’s vector-steering freedom under trusted-computation and codebook assumptions (§§3, 5.3, 6.2).

## Problem and motivation

Signed knowledge packs authenticate their publisher but do not establish that supplied embeddings encode their accompanying text. A compromised publisher can therefore ship review-admitted text with vectors chosen to redirect retrieval. Recomputing embeddings and allowing numerical differences seems reasonable, but the authors identify that tolerance itself as an attacker’s movement budget (§§1–2.5).

The problem affects both offline RAG updates and integrity audits of compressed vector stores: legitimate inference differences and compression errors can exceed the margins separating retrieval candidates.

## Approach and contributions

- **Margin law:** Derive per-query rank-one hijack exposure from the distribution of angular margins between the closest relevant and non-relevant documents. For one competing vector rotated within angular budget α, strict hijacking occurs when the margin is below α, subject to stated edge cases (§3).
- **Compressed-store attacks:** Measure exposure across storage formats and execute a tolerance-compliant attack against a real FAISS product-quantisation index (§5.2).
- **Code verification with canonicalisation:** Recompute embeddings, determine acceptable neighbouring codes using only the verifier’s values and calibrated guard bands, then replace accepted codes with the verifier’s own codes. Canonicalisation eliminates the publisher’s remaining choices (§5.3).
- **Deployment evidence:** Measure numerical reproducibility, verification cost, downstream answer degradation, and negative results for abstention, context pruning and semantic update screening (§§5.3–5.6).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Margin law matches simulated attacks | Maximum absolute prediction–simulation difference was 0.0 across tested tolerances on the 300-question set. | §5.1 |
| Small tolerances still expose close competitors | Across five corpora and two embedding models, idealised FP32-noise tolerance exposed 0–5.5% of eligible queries; excluding exact-duplicate competitors from the exposed count reduced every corpus’s exposure to at most 1.2%, with denominators unchanged. | §5.1; Fig. 1 |
| INT8-derived tolerance permits extensive exposure | MiniLM CPU dynamic-INT8 versus FP32 calibration gave τ = 0.9040 at the empirical 0.1% cosine quantile over 1,745 windows. Applying that angular budget yielded 98.7–100% passage-level exposure across the five corpora. Applying it to BGE-M3 is explicitly a sensitivity analysis. | §5.1; Fig. 1 |
| Compression creates large audit budgets | With tolerance set to the largest honest reconstruction angle, exposure across corpus–model pairs was 2–15% for SQ8, 55–89% for SQ4, and approximately 100% for PQ and binary storage. | §§5.2, 6.1; Table 3 caption |
| A practical PQ attack passes the audit | On 57,638 FiQA documents, all 60 attacked carrier vectors passed the tolerance check; carriers ranked first under decoded-PQ inner-product scoring for 25% of targets, with 95% CI 16–37%. Code verification with canonicalisation detected and removed all 60 tampered codes. | §5.2; Table 4 caption |
| Canonicalisation removes residual code choice | Calibrated guards accepted every honest vector in the tested samples across three platforms. Before canonicalisation, ambiguity choices exposed no tested MiniLM query and at most 0.16% for BGE-M3 on SQuAD; canonicalisation removes those choices by construction. | §5.3; Table 5 caption |
| Downstream harm depends on payload delivery | For the same 100 answerable questions on Raspberry Pi 5/Qwen2.5-3B, the fixed-note attack at the INT8 reference tolerance reduced correct-answer rate from 0.75 to 0.39 and increased refusals from 0.13 to 0.50; payload delivery into the top-three context was 94%. At τ = 0.999, correctness was 0.74 and delivery 2%. | §5.5 |
| Verification competes with generation | On Raspberry Pi CPU, verifying eight pairs/second reduced concurrent LLM decoding speed by 23–31% and increased p99 inter-token latency by 108–167%, relative to the reported generation-only baseline. | §5.4; Table 6 caption |
| Auxiliary safeguards have weaknesses | Abstention-scorer rankings reversed between SQuAD and MS MARCO. Three-sentence pruning reduced latency from 25.1 s to 8.6 s but correctness from 0.75 to 0.44. The tested NLI update screen achieved AUROC 0.18. | §5.6 |

These results establish exploitable inconsistency between authenticated text and supplied vectors in the tested settings. Margin-law exposure is conditional on honest retrieval already placing a relevant unit first, and measures whether a separate admissible attack exists for each query—not whether one poisoned index hijacks every exposed query. Retrieval displacement also does not automatically imply harmful answers, especially for duplicate documents (§3).

The executed PQ attack uses a different scoring objective from the angular-margin analysis; its success rate should not be interpreted as validating or bounding that analysis (§5.2).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ scope limits:** Two embedding models, English corpora containing up to 57,638 documents, flat FAISS indexes, one edge board and one local language model limit generalisation. Update scenarios are synthetic (§6.7).
- **Calibration limits:** INT8 tolerance depends on model, runtime and batch configuration. Guard widths are empirical sample calibrations, not guarantees for unseen data or hardware (§§5.1, 5.3).
- **Security boundaries:** The defence assumes trusted model/configuration, codebook, identifier–text–code binding and post-audit storage protection. Model, query-channel and index-structure attacks are outside scope (§§2.5, 6.2).
- **Measurement limits:** End-to-end evidence uses 100 questions per condition and answer correctness relies on normalised gold-answer containment. The greedy PQ attack supplies a lower-bound existence result (§§4, 6.7).
- **Reproducibility caveats:** Results come from one reference verifier; corrected implementation/numerical-library issues are disclosed. “Pre-registered” means dated before measurement, without public registry deposition (§§4, 6.7).
- **Analyst inference:** The reported canonicalisation guarantee is stronger than the empirical acceptance evidence: removing publisher choice follows from the procedure, while reliable acceptance of future honest inputs remains calibration-dependent.

## Open research questions

1. **Can guard bands be justified beyond sampled platforms?** Current widths are empirical. A next experiment could derive numerical-error bounds and test their coverage on held-out documents, runtimes and hardware (§§5.3, 6.9).
2. **How should integrity extend to approximate index structures?** Only flat indexes were tested. Compare code verification plus authenticated IVF assignments or graph structures against attacks on both codes and index organisation (§6.4).
3. **Can edge accelerators reduce verification interference while preserving reproducibility?** Dedicated accelerators were not measured. Test cross-device output consistency, honest acceptance and concurrent generation latency on pinned accelerator toolchains (§§6.5, 6.9).
4. **How much harm can one persistent poisoned store cause?** The margin law is per-query, and the device experiment constructs a context per question. Optimise a single constrained malicious pack and evaluate delivery and answer harm on held-out queries (§§3, 5.5).
