# Decoupled Predictive Decision Making: A Shared Conditional Predictor for Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-06.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.02075) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02075)


## One-sentence takeaway
DPDM combines frozen language-model representations with a shared frozen conditional predictor, improving labeled decision accuracy over few-shot prompting across 50 benchmarks, including comparisons using identical histories and queries.

## Problem and motivation
Agent decisions depend on relationships revealed by historical feedback, such as when a tool applies or whether a reasoning step is correct. The authors argue that language-model-centered agents usually couple semantic interpretation with inference from experience, making the latter difficult to improve independently (§1).

They distinguish insufficient evidence from ineffective use of available evidence. DPDM addresses the second problem through a replaceable conditional learner, while allowing additional history to address the first (§1; Appendix H).

## Approach and contributions
- **Decoupled architecture:** A frozen language model encodes each historical or query record independently. A shared conditional predictor receives these representations, historical labels, and the query, then predicts task-defined answer probabilities. A decision rule selects the highest-probability valid candidate (§§2.1–2.2).
- **Concrete shared implementation:** The same LimiX-2 checkpoint serves heterogeneous tasks and encoders. Features combine language-model hidden states compressed with PCA, lexical features, and available native fields. Historical labels remain separate from record encoding; feature transformations use historical inputs only (Appendix A).
- **Adaptation through evidence:** New labeled records change the predictor’s supplied history and potentially refitted feature transformations, while both neural models remain frozen (§2.3).
- **Empirical examination:** Matched-history comparisons, history-growth experiments, imbalance analysis, and controlled feature/label interventions test whether gains reflect evidence use, additional observations, and semantic representations (§§3.2–3.5).

The contribution is the architecture and its evaluation; the paper uses an existing pretrained conditional predictor rather than introducing LimiX-2 itself.

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Higher overall decision accuracy | Qwen3.5-27B DPDM: **84.92 ± 0.14%**, versus **78.80%** few-shot accuracy across 50 equally weighted benchmarks, using respective completed history budgets. DPDM uncertainty is sample SD over three histories. | Table 1; §3.2 |
| Gains persist with identical evidence | On identical ordered histories, labels, and queries across all 50 benchmarks, Qwen3.5-27B DPDM scores **82.33%**, versus **78.80%** few-shot accuracy. | §3.2; Appendix C.1 |
| Large tool-assessment gains, with unequal histories | Qwen3.5-27B DPDM versus few-shot accuracy: ToolPRMBench BFCL **76.85% vs 42.59%**, with **233 vs 64** historical records; ToolSandbox **96.91% vs 68.04%**, with **228 vs 128** records. | §3.3; Figure 5; Table 8 |
| More history improves cohort-average accuracy | With fixed queries and neural weights, Qwen3.5-27B DPDM increases from **75.48% to 84.87%** as history grows from **32 to 8,192** records on 21 benchmarks/29 configurations. All four encoders improve at every evaluated size. | §3.3; Figure 4 |
| Better average discrimination under imbalance | Across five BIG-Bench Mistake tasks and four encoders, mean balanced accuracy rises from **59.86% to 74.37%**, using identical histories and queries within each pair; **18/20** pairs improve. | §3.4; Figure 6; Table 10 |
| Correct pairings and distinct examples matter | On six tasks with 128 history rows: original labels yield **80.71%** accuracy versus **50.13%** with permuted labels. Using 128 distinct records yields **80.71%**, versus **64.79%** for 16 records repeated eight times. | Tables 2–3; Appendix F |
| Semantic features contribute | Across eight tasks at a 512-record ceiling, full DPDM achieves **80.42%** accuracy versus **63.95%** without semantic features and **48.78%** with permuted labels. Actual histories range from 162–512 records. | Table 4; Appendix F |
| Lower measured GPU time in the tested setup | Across five tasks, with matched histories and 256 queries per task, DPDM averages **19.7%** of cached Qwen3.5-27B few-shot GPU time—an **80.3% reduction** including historical preparation. | Figure 7; Appendix G |

These results support improved use of supplied evidence and effective reuse of one predictor across evaluated decision roles. The matched-history comparison rules out extra records as the sole explanation. However, the experiments primarily measure individual labeled judgments; they do not establish improved end-to-end agent success in interactive deployment.

## Limitations and validity risks
The paper provides no dedicated limitations section. The following are analyst inferences grounded in its protocols:

- **Budget-dependent headline comparison:** Main results use different completed history sizes. Matched-history results address this concern, but show a smaller advantage for Qwen3.5-27B (§3.2).
- **Restricted prompting baseline:** Qwen thinking is disabled, and prompting uses candidate-letter next-token probabilities. Comparisons with extended reasoning or alternative prompting strategies are not established (Appendix C.1).
- **One conditional predictor:** Multiple encoders are tested, but all use LimiX-2. Interchangeability with other conditional predictors remains an architectural proposal (§2.2).
- **Uneven improvements:** Balanced-accuracy gains can accompany lower ordinary accuracy, notably on arithmetic; two encoder–task pairs lose balanced accuracy (Table 10).
- **Limited deployment evidence:** History growth uses supplied labeled pools and fixed query sets. Feedback collection, nonstationarity, and interactive consequences are not tested in the main benchmarks (Appendices B–C).
- **Timing scope:** Measurements use dedicated Pro6000 GPUs, exclude loading and warm-up, and process baseline queries individually. They do not establish general serving-cost advantages (Appendix G).

## Open research questions
- **Does the predictor interface generalize beyond LimiX-2?** Only one predictive core is evaluated. Swap in compatible predictors while holding features, histories, and queries fixed.
- **Do judgment gains improve complete agents?** Main outcomes are labeled decisions. Integrate DPDM into an interactive tool agent and measure task completion and accumulated errors.
- **How robust is adaptation to changing or unreliable feedback?** Evaluations mainly expand fixed historical pools. Introduce temporal distribution changes and controlled label noise, then compare retention and history-selection strategies.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported benchmark findings and architecture; **medium** in broader deployment implications.
