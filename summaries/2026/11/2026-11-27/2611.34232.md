# OS-Omni: Benchmarking Generalist Computer-Using Agents on Long-Horizon Workflows Across Platforms

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.34232) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34232)


## One-sentence takeaway

On OS-Omni v1, Claude Opus 5.5 led the screenshot-only agents at 68.5% macro-average final-state success versus a 77.3% human reference, while the best agent scores on iOS and macOS remained more than 25 percentage points below their respective human references (Table 1, §4.2).

## Problem and motivation

An agent completing a multi-step user request must carry information across applications, adapt to different controls, and verify that files or records were saved correctly. The authors argue that existing web, mobile, and desktop benchmarks mostly test one software surface at a time, making it hard to tell whether strong performance on one platform carries over to others (§1, §3.7).

## Approach and contributions

- **Versioned benchmark.** OS-Omni v1 contains 300 tasks, with 48 each on Windows, Ubuntu, macOS, iOS, Android, and Web, plus 12 desktop–Android workflows. The authors describe future releases as fixed task sets with broader application coverage and harder workflows (§§3.3–3.4, Figure 5).
- **Controlled applications.** The authors built 18 Open-series applications used in 180 of the 300 v1 tasks. Seeded data and inspectable local state make service-backed workflows reproducible without live accounts or payments (§3.2, Appendix C.3, Table 4).
- **Executable evaluation.** A shared runner resets the environment, applies task-specific setup, executes actions through platform adapters, logs trajectories, and checks the resulting files or application records. Oracle runs and representative failure states are used to validate evaluators (§§2.1–2.4, §3.5).
- **Baseline study.** The paper evaluates nine models under a screenshot-only protocol, compares them with a human reference, and uses saved trajectories to illustrate failure mechanisms (§4, Table 1, Appendix E).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| The leading model depends on the averaging rule | Across the seven v1 tracks in screenshot-only runs, Claude Opus 5.5 led the macro-average at 68.5% versus 77.3% for humans, an 8.8-point gap. Weighting all 300 tasks equally, GPT-6-Astra led at 67.0% (201 solved), followed by Claude at 66.7% (200 solved), versus a task-weighted human reference of 76.5%. | Table 1, §4.2 |
| Human–agent gaps vary by platform | On v1, all three frontier models exceeded the 74.6% Linux human reference, with Gemini 4 Argon highest at 95.8%. Claude had the best agent scores on iOS (41.7% versus 75.2% human) and macOS (54.2% versus 79.8% human). | Table 1, §4.2 |
| Claude matched the reported cross-device human reference | On the 12 single-screen desktop–Android workflows, Claude and humans both scored 83.3%, versus 58.3% for GPT-6-Astra and 50.0% for Gemini 4 Argon. One additional solved workflow changes a model’s score by about 8.3 points. | Table 1, §4.3 |
| Human workflows took more steps and time than the cited OSWorld references | Across five OS-Omni desktop and mobile platforms, human completion averaged 67.54 interaction steps and 200.97 seconds. The paper cites 15.0 steps and a median completion time of 111.94 seconds for OSWorld. | §1, Appendix A, Figures 6–7 |
| Plausible GUI progress can still leave incorrect records | Trajectory examples include a duplicate transfer and a booking workflow that duplicated one booking and omitted another. The broader taxonomy also includes grounding, state-tracking, timing, and persistence errors. | §4.3, §6, Appendix E, Table 8 |

The results establish how often the evaluated configurations reached the required final state on v1. The trajectories illustrate ways those runs failed. The evidence leaves open how much of each platform gap comes from the task mix, interface conventions, action backend, or agent configuration. The OSWorld timing comparison uses different task sets and compares an OS-Omni mean with an OSWorld median.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope.** The authors caution that the cross-device split is small and that accessibility metadata varies by platform and application. The cross-device workflows all use a mirrored Android device paired with Ubuntu or Windows (§2.3, §4.3, Appendix C.7.7).
- **Analyst inference — platform comparisons.** The tracks use different task inventories and overlapping application families. Step-based difficulty calibration helps align task burden, but without matched workflows the observed score gaps cannot isolate a platform effect (§3.5, Appendix C.2, Table 5).
- **Analyst inference — transfer to live services.** Controlled accounts, fixed seeds, and local stores support reproducibility. Transfer to live services with changing data and external dependencies remains unmeasured (§3.2, Appendix C.3).
- **Analyst inference — attribution.** The paper supports accessibility trees and Set-of-Mark overlays, but gives no quantitative comparison with the main screenshot-only results. The qualitative taxonomy also gives no error frequencies, so it cannot establish which mechanism accounts for the largest share of failures (Table 1, Appendices D.4 and E.2–E.5).

## Open research questions

1. **How much of the iOS and macOS gap comes from platform interaction?** The v1 tracks differ in task and application mix. Run matched goals using controlled apps with common seeds and equivalent final-state checks across platforms (§4.2, Appendix C.3).
2. **Would structured observations reduce grounding failures?** The benchmark supports accessibility trees and Set-of-Mark, but the supplied results give no quantitative comparison. Run the same models on the same tasks with screenshots alone, screenshots plus accessibility trees, and screenshots with Set-of-Mark, then compare final-state success and first-failure locations (Appendices B.2, D.4, E.2).
3. **Does explicit progress tracking improve durable completion?** The traces show forgotten and duplicated subgoals, but their prevalence and responsiveness to intervention are unclear. Compare the default recent-history protocol with a persistent subgoal checklist and a final verification step, holding models, tasks, and budgets fixed (§4.3, Table 7, Appendix E).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the benchmark design and reported point estimates. The supplied text gives no human cohort size or task-assignment breakdown, model rerun counts or uncertainty intervals, per-model and per-track invalid-run and timeout rates, manual-override counts, or quantitative observation ablations. The OSWorld step reference is reported without enough detail to verify that both benchmarks counted interactions comparably.
