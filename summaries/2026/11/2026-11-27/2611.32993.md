# Decision-Native Multimodal Clinical Modeling via Anatomical Grounding and Confidence Calibration

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.32993) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32993)


## One-sentence takeaway

Radev combines reusable 3D organ features with question-selected 2D slice evidence to score structured abdominal CT answers in one pass, outperforming the reported baselines on both CT-Verdict cohorts and improving confidence calibration on the in-distribution cohort (Tables 1–2, 6).

## Problem and motivation

Structured CT questions require both whole-organ context and fine slice detail, with the relevant anatomy changing from question to question. The authors argue that autoregressive models add decoding overhead, can yield poorly calibrated candidate probabilities, and can learn shortcuts when supervised only on answer correctness. They identify a gap in direct candidate prediction for volumetric imaging, where confidence could help flag uncertain decisions for review (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Organ-grounded candidate prediction.** Radev uses a frozen 3D encoder to build a reusable sequence of organ features plus a global feature. A deterministic question-to-organ mapping selects which organ tokens attend to 2D slice patches within predicted anatomical bounds. Qwen3.5 then scores single-token candidate labels with a restricted softmax in one forward pass. The contribution is the integration of this existing decision interface with query-conditioned volumetric evidence (§§2.1, 3.1–3.3, 5.1, Fig. 2).
- **Counterfactual-Calibrated Training (CCT).** After alignment and verdict fine-tuning, CCT supervises the same reference answer on intact, target-plus-global ablated, and unrelated-organ ablated views. A separate hinge penalizes confidence on a fully image-blind view only when blindness worsens the decision score (§3.4, Eqs. 7–8).
- **CT-Verdict benchmark.** The authors report releasing a benchmark of report-derived binary, multiple-choice, and ordinal CT questions, with a study-level in-distribution split and a separate external cohort. LLM readers check question labels against source reports, supplemented by human spot checks (§§4.1–4.2, App. A.2).

The matched-training and no-image comparisons support the value of visual evidence for this benchmark. The calibration and rejection results show that confidence tracks correctness in aggregate and helps rank errors in the reported evaluation. External KRPS measures overall ordinal probability quality, which can improve through discrimination as well as calibration. Table 2 gives no external top-label ECE, multiclass Brier score, or rejection curve, leaving the authors’ broader claim about calibrated clinical abstention under shift unverified.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope and assumptions.** The conclusion identifies broader anatomy and other imaging modalities, particularly MRI, as future work. Appendix B.2 acknowledges that unbound organs may retain information about the answer and that the theoretical link to factual confidence depends on readout smoothness and approximate optimization.
- **Analyst inference — reference standard and external coverage.** Human audits checked agreement with source reports, so they establish report fidelity rather than independent image-level diagnostic accuracy. The external cohort contains only 59 ordinal items and no acute-triage or laterality items, limiting conclusions about transfer for those tasks (§4.2, App. A.2, Table A2).
- **Analyst inference — intervention specificity.** Target ablation removes all bound organs and the global slot, whereas control ablation removes one other organ and preserves the global slot. The comparison cannot isolate target-organ evidence from global context or the amount of information removed (§3.4).
- **Analyst inference — comparison and reporting.** Baselines use different readout interfaces, and GPT-5.4 supplies no candidate probabilities, precluding a direct calibration comparison (§5.1, Tables 1–2). Figure A2 gives binary predictions with purported confidences below 0.5, which is incompatible with the maximum of a normalized two-candidate distribution defined in Eq. 5. The plotted values need clarification before the case study can support its abstention claim.

## Open research questions

1. **Do fixed deferral thresholds transfer across cohorts?** Figure 4 reports a rejection curve, while Table 2 omits external calibration and deferral results. Choose thresholds on held-out in-distribution validation data, then measure external coverage, retained accuracy, and ECE by task.
2. **How much of the CCT effect comes from removing the target organ itself?** Target and control interventions differ in both global context and removal size (§3.4). Compare target-only, global-only, target-plus-global, and size-matched unrelated-organ interventions while measuring confidence and decision changes.
3. **How well do report-derived verdicts agree with independent image review?** The human audit used source reports as the reference (App. A.2). Have radiologists independently reread a stratified sample of scans, adjudicate disagreements, and recompute performance and calibration against the resulting labels.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the architecture and tabulated benchmark comparisons, **medium** for anatomical attribution and calibration under shift. The supplied text leaves unavailable independent image-adjudicated labels, external top-label calibration and risk–coverage results, prospective workflow outcomes, measured end-to-end latency, and confirmation of access to model weights or training code. The confidence convention used in Figure A2 is also unresolved.
