# Boundary-Aware Policy Learning for Agent-Orchestrated Robot Manipulation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.32238) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32238)


## One-sentence takeaway

Training a visuomotor policy on contact segments from varied start poses raised task success to 98.4% in randomized RoboTwin 2.0 scenes, and retraining on curated system rollouts enabled earlier handoffs that reduced execution cost and time without a reported drop in success (§§4.3, 4.5).

## Problem and motivation

A multimodal agent can plan a manipulation task, but the learned policy it calls may only execute reliably from a limited set of starting observations. The authors call this set the *handoff boundary*. Whole-task demonstrations leave mid-task handoffs difficult to specify, so even a retry after the agent repositions the gripper can start outside the policy’s reliable region. The authors identify a gap in approaches focused on agent memory, exploration, or verification: those mechanisms can change when the agent calls the policy, while training data shape where the policy can succeed (§§1–3.1).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Boundary-Explicit Training.** Fine-tune π₀.₅ on contact-only demonstrations starting from varied poses near objects. Give the agent a shared protocol for approaching a suitable pose, invoking a short skill, verifying the result, and repositioning before a retry (§3.2, Appendix B).
2. **Boundary-Expanding Self-Improvement.** Curate successful system rollouts to remove retries and redundant adjustments, re-execute edited trajectories for continuity, then retrain on both contact demonstrations and curated rollouts. Update the protocol and re-explore each task so the agent can try a whole-task handoff early and fall back to contact skills when progress stalls (§3.3, Appendix C).

The comparisons support the reported benchmark gains for the combined policy and protocol. The distance analysis uses gripper position as a proxy for the handoff boundary and leaves orientation and other state variables unmeasured. The efficiency comparison covers the combined update to policy, protocol, and exploration memory, so the savings attributable to each change remain unknown. The reported savings apply to execution, with rollout collection costs reported separately (Appendix C).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Curation discards behavior that could teach recovery, and the rule-based protocol gives the agent no policy confidence estimate for a proposed handoff (§5).
- **Author-stated scope.** The main RoboDojo evaluation excludes long-horizon and precision tasks, where the authors report that policy training rarely succeeds. The real-robot evidence shows one successful episode on one task, and quantitative hardware evaluation is deferred (Appendices A, E.2).
- **Analyst inference.** The policy-swap ablation changes the policy backbone along with its training data, so it does not isolate the effect of start-pose diversity. The Harness VLA distance analysis also uses official demonstrations as a proxy for undocumented LingBot-VLA training data, and its retry episodes may be harder because a prior call left the task unfinished (§4.5, Appendices A, D).

## Open research questions

- **Can a learned confidence estimate improve handoff decisions?** The protocol uses pose rules and the distance analysis omits orientation. Compare rule-only and confidence-gated handoffs with the same policy under held-out position and orientation shifts (§§4.4, 5).
- **Can discarded segments teach recovery?** The curation pipeline removes retries and adjustments. Compare equally sized curated-only and recovery-augmented training sets on episodes with induced slips or failed grasps (§3.3, §5).
- **Which part of self-improvement drives the efficiency gains?** Retraining, longer policy calls, and re-exploration change together. Vary these factors independently on the same held-out scenes and measure success, calls, cost, and time (§4.3, Appendix C).
