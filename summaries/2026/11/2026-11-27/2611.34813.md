# Solve Vision with Code: Coding Agents Are Stronger Visual Reasoners Than Video Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.34813) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34813)


## One-sentence takeaway

On VBVR-Pro-Bench, three closed-model coding agents wrote and executed programs that scored 0.893–0.923 on the official visual-reasoning evaluator, ahead of the strongest published video-model score of 0.670 (Table 2).

## Problem and motivation

Video reasoning benchmarks ask systems to show a solution unfolding in a clip given an initial frame and a text prompt. The authors ask whether generating those frames directly is the more reliable way to solve the task. They identify a missing comparison with program synthesis under shared inputs, output specifications, and scoring, and argue that most VBVR-Pro tasks can be solved from the first frame and prompt alone (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Comparison protocol.** Off-the-shelf coding agents inspect the first frame, write code, and execute it to render the required video. Each instance permits one attempt with up to 30 minutes of iteration in a CPU sandbox. The agents lack access to task metadata, the generator, and ground truth, and may not call image or video generation models. The study compares three closed-model agents and 23 runnable open-weight models with published video-model results using the unmodified official evaluator (§§2.1–2.3, Table 2).
- **Failure and efficiency analysis.** The authors separate failures to deliver a video from low-scoring delivered videos and track tool calls, tokens, and elapsed time (§§2.4, 3.3, Table 4).
- **Program behavior analysis.** The authors distinguish *depictive* programs, which derive answers from pixels at runtime, from *propositional* programs, which render answers fixed during the agent session. They test the distinction by re-executing successful programs on another instance from the same family and retaining an intermediate category for ambiguous behavior (§3.4, Figures 3–4).

The evidence establishes a benchmark-level advantage for the tested closed-agent systems under the reported protocol. The shared evaluator makes task scores comparable, while differences in models, tools, and inference budgets leave the contribution of program synthesis itself unresolved. The re-execution analysis characterizes how emitted programs respond to changed inputs. It provides limited evidence about transfer across a whole task family or about how the model originally inferred the answer.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Flat colors, exact geometry, and deterministic scoring favor pixel operations such as color filtering and connected-component analysis. The authors highlight this concern for Perception and caution that textured, occluded, variably lit scenes may behave differently (§§3.2, 4).
- **Author-stated.** Agents can iterate with tools for up to 30 minutes, while the video comparison uses published single-sample scores. Token counts are only indicative across agents because tokenizers and caching differ (§§2.3–2.4, 4).
- **Analyst inference.** The closed agents use vendor-specific CLIs, while the open-weight models share OpenCode. Without a cross-harness ablation, the study cannot isolate model capability from harness behavior, especially for tool invocation and delivery (§§2.1–2.2, 3.3).
- **Analyst inference.** Strategy frequencies apply to successful closed-agent programs. The classification can count a program that fails while measuring its new input as depictive, so the depictive share is not a successful-transfer rate (§3.4, Figure 3, §D.4).

## Open research questions

1. **How much of the gap persists under matched inference budgets?** The reported comparison gives agents iterative tool use and uses published single-sample video scores. Rerun both routes on the same instances and compare score-versus-budget curves under a common accounting scheme (§4).
2. **Does the advantage survive more realistic rendering?** The evaluated scenes use procedural, visually clean inputs. Rerender matched tasks with varied textures, lighting, and occlusion while preserving symbolic ground truth, then compare how each route’s score changes (§4).
3. **How reliably do depictive programs transfer within a task family?** Each program was tested on one alternative, and the depictive label can include runtime failures. Rerun each program on all available alternatives and report successful transfer, crashes, and stale answers separately (§3.4, Figure 3, §D.4).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the reported benchmark rankings and protocol, which are documented in the supplied tables and methods. The text does not provide repeated-run variability or uncertainty intervals, video-model delivery and resource measurements comparable to the agent logs, exact decision thresholds for the transfer classifier, or confirmation that the study’s harness, generated programs, and run logs were publicly released.
