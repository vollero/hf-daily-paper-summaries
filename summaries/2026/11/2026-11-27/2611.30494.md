# Revisiting LLMs as Context Engineers

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-27.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.30494) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30494)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across the tested models and tasks, no automated context engineering method consistently beat chain-of-thought or in-context learning, and gains over starting prompts tended to be larger when initial failures were less diverse (§§4–5).

## Problem and motivation

Automated LLM context engineering (LCE) uses offline task attempts to improve prompts or distill reusable advice without updating model weights. This offers a way to adapt models without the data and rollout demands of parameter training. The authors identify a comparison gap: prior methods were evaluated on largely disjoint datasets and different models, making it hard to tell when their gains transfer or what produces them (§1, §4).

## Approach and contributions

The paper unifies existing methods as a three-stage process: generate task rollouts, extract feedback or experiences from them, and consolidate that material into an updated system prompt or experience pool (§3, Eq. 1).

- **Unified benchmark.** It compares TextGrad, GEPA, TF-GRPO, and ACE during validation, then tests the latter three against chain-of-thought (CoT) and in-context learning (ICL) across three model families and five datasets (§§3–4).
- **Scaling study.** It varies the size of the model that proposes context updates and the model that executes tasks, then tests joint size scaling and successive Qwen generations (§4, Figs. 3–4).
- **Mechanism analysis.** It measures the diversity of diagnosed rollout failures, checks how answer formatting affects scores, and uses TF-GRPO ablations to examine what gets lost when rollouts are distilled into experiences (§5, Appendices E, G, and I).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Validation gains transferred unevenly to test. | GEPA, TF-GRPO, and ACE improved Qwen3-8B validation scores over their own initial contexts on all four validation datasets. At test time, the best LCE beat the best CoT/ICL baseline by more than one task-score point in 7 of 15 model–dataset settings, with significant gains in 5 (`p < 0.05`, as reported in the prose). | §4, Fig. 2, Table 2 |
| Some apparent wins came from the starting prompt. | On IFBench with Gemma4, optimized GEPA scored 6.94 points above CoT on the official IFBench metric and 1.22 points below its own initial prompt. | §4, Table 2, §B.1 |
| Larger or newer models gave inconsistent gains. | In Qwen self-reflection scaling, GEPA and TF-GRPO beat CoT on MiddleMatch across tested sizes, with no reliable gains on MATH500 or HotpotQA. Successive Qwen generations showed no consistent trend in LCE gains. | §4, Figs. 3–4 |
| More diverse failures were associated with smaller gains. | Across model–dataset–method settings, the Vendi Score of diagnosed initial failures correlated negatively with the change in task score after optimization (`r = −0.5`, `p ≤ 0.05`). The association remained negative in the mixed-effects analysis (`β = −0.793`, `p ≤ 0.05`). GEPA and TF-GRPO showed significant method-level associations, while ACE did not. | Abstract, §5, Figs. 5–6, Appendix G, Table 8 |
| Answer formatting explained some score gains. | On HotpotQA with Min3, fewer correct answers were rejected by exact match after optimization. On MATH500 with Gemma4, better compliance with the boxed-answer format accompanied gains that nearly vanished after LLM-based answer extraction. | §5, Figs. 7–8, Appendix I, Table 10 |
| Distilling rollouts into experiences could help or hurt. | In the TF-GRPO ablation, randomly sampled extracted experiences scored below ICL on Qwen3 MiddleMatch and Gemma4 MATH500, and above ICL on Min3 MATH500 and Gemma4 IFBench. The comparison used the respective task scores. | §5, Table 3 |

The test results establish that baseline choice and starting prompts matter for judging gains in these settings. The diversity analysis supports an association, while the format checks show that some benchmark gains reflect more reliably extractable answers. A causal effect of failure diversity and a general improvement in reasoning remain unestablished.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-identified limitations.** Starting prompts differ by method, so the authors report pre-optimization scores separately (§B.1). The authors also identify experience extraction as a bottleneck and report little change in experience diversity over optimization for ACE and TF-GRPO (§5, Fig. 5; §6).
- **Analyst inference.** The diversity measure depends on LLM-generated diagnoses and their embeddings. The generator self-labeling check reproduced the negative association, but the supplied text does not report human validation of the diagnoses (§B.2, Fig. 17).
- **Analyst inference.** TextGrad was screened out before test, AppWorld was the sole interactive benchmark, and the detailed extraction ablation used TF-GRPO. The test rankings and mechanism findings therefore have different scopes (§§4–5).

## Open research questions

1. **Does failure diversity itself reduce LCE gains?** The paper measures an association across existing settings. A next experiment could vary the mix of failure types within one task while holding the model, initial prompt, rollout count, and optimization budget fixed (§5, Appendix G).
2. **Can experience extraction preserve useful details from heterogeneous rollouts?** The TF-GRPO ablation shows losses in some settings, and the tested variants also differ in how they select context. A next experiment could compare raw rollouts, standard summaries, and summaries grouped by failure type at matched token budgets (§5, Table 3, Appendices E–F).
3. **How much of the gain survives format-independent grading across tasks and models?** Enhanced extraction is reported for Gemma4 on MATH500. A next experiment could score the same outputs with both the original evaluator and blinded semantic grading, while measuring format compliance separately (§5, Appendix I).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the main reported patterns within the evaluated settings. The supplied text includes captions without the full numerical contents of Tables 2, 8, and 10 or the plotted values in the figures, so exact per-setting scores, uncertainty, and normalized-answer gains cannot all be recovered. It also leaves the common failure-diversity subsample size `N` unspecified and does not confirm which artifacts are available through the linked code repository.
