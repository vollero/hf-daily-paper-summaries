# Heave-Ho: Decentralized Long-Horizon Multi-Humanoid Collaboration

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.22275) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22275)


## One-sentence takeaway

Heave-Ho combines a shared whole-body controller with leader-selected, speech-synchronized skills to coordinate humanoids without inter-robot networking, achieving 94.41% low-level success across 12 simulated tasks and completing 43 of 50 full-system planning trials across real and simulated settings (Tables 1–2).

## Problem and motivation

Large or awkward loads can exceed one humanoid’s capabilities. Carrying them together couples the robots mechanically, so a mistimed step can destabilize both the load and a partner. Tasks such as passing through a narrow doorway also require the team to choose and sequence maneuvers from visual observations. The authors identify a gap between centralized approaches that gather team-wide observations and decentralized controllers that must coordinate from local observations while keeping skill starts aligned (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Shared residual whole-body control.** The authors adapt frozen SONIC motion tracking to physically coupled teams by learning a shared joint-target residual with multi-agent PPO. Training uses a centralized critic and privileged teacher distillation. At execution, each robot uses its own proprioception, reference motion, and nominal object specifications. Skills share compatible resting poses so they can be chained (§§3.1–3.2, Appendix D.2).
- **Visual planning and acoustic synchronization.** A LoRA-tuned Qwen3-VL-2B planner selects the next team skill from the leader’s camera image and the task instruction. The leader speaks the command, followers recognize it locally, and robots start on a shared timing grid. A person can also take the leader role (§3.3, Fig. 2).
- **HH-BENCH.** The authors introduce a 12-task benchmark for physically coupled collaboration, spanning constrained transport, precise placement, handover, shoulder carrying, pulling, and pushing (§4, Table 3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| The shared residual controller led the simulation comparison. | Across 12 tasks with three seeds of 512 teams per task, mean success was 94.41%, versus 28.56% for Multi-HDMI, 0.22% for pure SONIC, 16.78% without SONIC, and 22.05% with independent actors. Heave-Ho had mean body and object position errors of 18.62 cm and 19.59 cm over the full reference horizon, including post-failure frames. | §5.4, Tables 2 and 7 |
| The learned planner led the automated comparisons. | Across five tasks with 10 trials each, two on hardware and three in simulation, success was 43/50 for Heave-Ho, 21/50 for zero-shot GPT-6 Astra, 6/50 for adapted CHORUS, and 49/50 for the human operator. Heave-Ho matched the operator on all three simulated tasks and scored 6/10 on the physical door and 7/10 on the physical corridor. | §5.3, Table 1 |
| Heave-Ho had the lowest reported automated decision latency. | Per decision, Heave-Ho took 52.5 ms on one RTX 4090 using 5.3 GiB, versus 78 ms for CHORUS and 6.46 s on average for GPT-6 Astra via API. | §5.3, Fig. 6a, Table 5 |
| Hardware trials demonstrated collaboration across several team configurations. | Across the hardware experiments, which included human-led runs, teams succeeded in 92/100 trials. The G1–human stair carry succeeded in 7/10. Four table-carry team configurations of varying size and composition accounted for 37/40 successes. | §§5.2, 5.5, Table 4 |
| The G1 controller transferred to larger teams on table carry. | The controller trained with two G1s was demonstrated with four G1s on hardware and with four, eight, and ten G1s in simulation without G1 retraining. The paper gives no size-specific success rates for those simulation demonstrations. | §5.5, Fig. 7, Table 4 |

The simulation ablations support the value of the frozen tracker and actor sharing under the tested skill chains. The planner results compare complete decision-making setups, so they cannot isolate planning quality from synchronization or the effect of task-specific fine-tuning. The 92/100 hardware result includes human-led runs and therefore cannot quantify autonomous planner reliability by itself. Simulation and hardware success rates use different starting distributions (§4).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Speech synchronization requires a reasonably quiet setting and reliable sound pickup. Extending the planner to a new real-world task requires time-consuming manual demonstrations (§6).
- **Analyst inference.** CHORUS executes asynchronously, so its result leaves open how much of the gap comes from timing versus skill selection. The latency comparison also mixes local GPU inference and API access (§5.3).
- **Analyst inference.** Larger-team transfer is demonstrated on table carry, with the largest teams in simulation. Heterogeneous trials use a separately trained PM01 controller and human partners from the research team, limiting claims about transfer to new embodiments or unfamiliar partners (§§5.1, 5.5, Appendix A).

## Open research questions

- **How robust is acoustic synchronization to noise and missed commands?** The paper identifies quiet conditions as a requirement but reports no controlled noise sweep. Vary background noise and speaker distance, then measure command recognition, start-time skew, and task success (§§3.3, 6).
- **How much of the planning advantage comes from synchronized starts?** The CHORUS comparison changes both the decision maker and execution timing. Give adapted CHORUS and Heave-Ho the same start scheduler, then compare skill agreement and task completion (§5.3).
- **Does larger-team transfer extend beyond table carry?** The team-size demonstrations cover one transport task. Evaluate the unchanged G1 controller across team sizes on another feasible benchmark task, such as constrained transport, and report success and tracking error by size (§5.5, Fig. 7).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the reported aggregate results and method description. The supplied text does not establish public availability of code, checkpoints, or training demonstrations. It also gives no acoustic grid interval, missed-command recovery procedure, real-world trial-by-trial leader assignments, or success rates by simulated team size for the scaling demonstrations.
