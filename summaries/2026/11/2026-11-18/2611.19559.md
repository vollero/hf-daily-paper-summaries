# ResWAM: Learning What Changes, Not What Stays, for World-Action Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.19559) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19559)


## One-sentence takeaway

ResWAM jointly predicts robot actions and changes in frozen DINOv3 features, improving RoboTwin 2.0 average success by 2.9 percentage points over full-future feature prediction at matched compute and achieving the lowest measured inference latency among the compared policies in all four settings (§1, Fig. 7, Tables 1–4).

## Problem and motivation

World-action models use visual foresight to help robot policies anticipate the consequences of their actions. The authors argue that over a short action chunk, full-future prediction can reward copying unchanged scene content, weakening supervision for robot and object motion. Current and future DINOv3 features already have a mean cosine similarity of 0.960 on LIBERO. The authors also identify a deployment cost from large video generators and separate world models (§§1, 4.1, 6.3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Visual residual supervision.** Subtract the current frozen DINOv3 patch tokens from their future counterparts at the end of the demonstration action chunk. The authors characterize the resulting target using effective rank, stable rank, and spatial visualizations (§4, Figs. 2–3).
- **Joint action and residual prediction.** A 2.3B-parameter model couples a fine-tuned RynnBrain vision-language backbone with a 0.5B dual-branch Diffusion Transformer. The branches exchange information through attention and learn normalized actions and visual residuals via conditional flow matching. Inference jointly integrates both branches over 10 Euler steps in latent space (§5, Fig. 4).
- **Controlled target comparison.** On RoboTwin 2.0, the authors compare the proposed target with full-future DINOv3 features, residuals from the vision-language backbone’s vision tower, and an action-only policy (§6.3, Fig. 7).

The matched-compute ablation supports a benefit from changing the supervision target on RoboTwin 2.0. The benchmark tables compare complete policies, so gains elsewhere may also reflect architecture, backbone, or training differences. Rank, cosine similarity, and saliency characterize feature behavior. They leave open whether the residual distinguishes the consequences of alternative actions from other scene changes.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported scope.** The physical evaluation covers three tabletop tasks on one FR3 setup. Each benchmark has a separately trained policy, and timing excludes CPU-side preprocessing (§6.1, Table 4, Appendices C–D).
- **Analyst inference.** The action-only ablation removes the residual branch during training. It leaves the value of running that branch at inference unresolved. The target ablation is confined to RoboTwin 2.0 (§6.3, Fig. 7).
- **Analyst inference.** The supplied success tables and ablation omit confidence intervals and variation across training seeds, limiting interpretation of smaller margins. Figure A1 reports variability for latency.

## Open research questions

- **How much does joint residual prediction contribute at deployment?** The action-only ablation changes training and inference together. Train with the same residual objective while making the branch optional at test time, then compare success and end-to-end latency with the branch enabled and disabled (§5.2, Fig. 7).
- **Does residual supervision still help when unrelated motion is prominent?** The saliency examples emphasize robot and object motion, while the subtraction uses all patch changes. Compare residual and full-future targets under controlled camera movement and independently moving distractors (§4.3, Fig. 3).
- **Does the target advantage transfer to physical manipulation?** The matched-compute comparison appears only on RoboTwin 2.0. Repeat it with matched architectures and multiple training seeds on FR3 tasks, including Stack (Table 4, Fig. 7).
