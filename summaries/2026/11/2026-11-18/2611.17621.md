# AscendGYM: A Cost-Aware Kernel Optimization Benchmark for Real-World Workloads on Ascend NPUs

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.17621) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17621)


## One-sentence takeaway

AscendGYM measures how quickly coding agents find correctness-validated improvements to existing Ascend NPU kernels, and about 70% of evaluated model–harness pairs achieved less than 1.2× geometric mean speedup over the original implementations on the 17 Batch 1 tasks (§4.2.1, Figs. 5–6).

## Problem and motivation

Improving kernels used in LLM training and inference requires hardware-specific tuning, repeated execution, and numerical validation under deployment-relevant inputs. The authors identify a gap in Ascend benchmarks, which primarily assess kernel generation. AscendGYM starts from runnable implementations and asks how much agents can reduce latency while preserving correctness, and how much search effort those gains require (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Workload-derived benchmark.** The suite contains 48 tasks across three difficulty levels, with runnable baseline code and test cases based on real workload shapes, data types, and execution parameters. Speedup is measured against each task’s baseline under the same input conditions (§§3.1, 3.4.2, 4.1).
- **Execution and evaluation infrastructure.** AscendForge compiles candidates, checks correctness, profiles passing implementations, and returns feedback for iterative search. The benchmark supports both complete model–harness pairs and models evaluated with the built-in AuraKernel harness (§§3.2, 3.4, Figs. 1–2).
- **Budget-aware scoring.** Speedup–Token Score (STS) rewards final validated speedup and penalizes gains reached later in the token budget. Cost-to-Gain (CTG) measures the additional tokens needed to reach a target gain. Separate metrics track speedup coverage, correctness, interaction rounds, and elapsed time (§3.3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Most evaluated pairs made modest gains | About 70% of pairs finished below 1.2× geometric mean speedup over the shipped baselines across 17 Batch 1 tasks. Claude Code/Opus 5 and Codex/GPT-5.6 sped up all 17, with seven and three tasks, respectively, exceeding 2×. | §4.2.1, Figs. 5–6 |
| Gain timing and coverage distinguished harnesses using the same model | Opus 5 had similar STS with Claude Code and OpenCode at 10M tokens per task, and Claude Code led at 50M. At 50M and a 10% CTG target, median costs among attaining tasks were 6.6M tokens for Claude Code and 3.8M for OpenCode. Claude Code attained the target on all 17 tasks and OpenCode attained it on a subset. | §4.2.1, Figs. 3–4 |
| Correctness coverage and speedup captured different outcomes | GPT-5.6 produced a passing candidate on all 17 tasks with each of the three harnesses. Its geometric mean speedup over the shipped baselines was approximately 1.69× with Codex and 1.05× with Claude Code. | §4.2.1, Figs. 5 and 7 |
| Similar gains could entail different turnaround times | On Batch 1, Qwen-35B achieved approximately 1.06× geometric mean speedup with OpenCode and 1.05× with Codex, with comparable mean token use and mean elapsed times of 11.1 and 2.2 hours per task, respectively. | Appendix E.2, Figs. 5, 10 and 12 |
| Warm led the fixed-model comparison | With GPT-5.6 Luna on Batch 1, warm had the highest STS at all three per-task token budgets of 10M, 20M, and 50M under the configuration-specific protocols. Code only ranked above no lessons and below cold at each budget. | §4.2.2, Fig. 8, Appendix B |

The results show differences in validated kernel speedup, the timing of gains, and correctness coverage under the reported setup. The CTG medians summarize different subsets of tasks, leaving same-task token efficiency unresolved. The measurements establish isolated kernel gains on tested inputs. End-to-end training or inference gains and performance on the harder batches remain unmeasured in the report.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope.** Experiments cover only Batch 1. The authors defer results for the remaining 31 tasks and plan to broaden task and input coverage (§3.1, §5).
- **Author-stated comparison caveat.** Warm uses prior lessons, including lessons from cold runs of the same kernel on three tasks. Some warm measurements also use more timed executions. The warm result characterizes the full configuration under its protocol, leaving the isolated effect and total preparation cost of lesson reuse unresolved (§4.2.2, Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference.** The model–harness token-budget comparisons use trajectories from searches with a candidate-count cap and no hard token or wall-clock cap. An agent told to stop at a hard token limit could allocate its search differently (§4.1, Fig. 3).
- **Analyst inference.** The latest-record selection and repeated timing of the final candidate leave run-to-run optimization variability unquantified. The final timings repeat measurement of the selected implementation (§4.1, Appendix E.2).

## Open research questions

- **Do system rankings hold on harder kernels?** The report evaluates Batch 1 only. Run the same pairs and scoring protocol on Batches 2 and 3, then compare rankings, attainment, and correctness by batch.
- **How much does prior experience itself improve search?** Warm and cold differ in experience and some timing settings. Repeat matched runs with identical profiling settings, vary only lesson initialization, and report both search-only and preparation-inclusive token costs.
- **Which kernel gains carry over to workload performance?** The report measures isolated latency. Install validated candidates in their source workloads and measure end-to-end latency or throughput against the original implementations under the corresponding deployment configurations.

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported benchmark design and text-described Batch 1 results. The supplied Markdown contains captions but omits the cells of Tables 1–2 and the plotted values beyond those described in prose. Those omissions leave the full configuration matrix, exact software versions, and complete per-pair and per-task results unavailable. The report also supplies no independent search-repeat statistics, harder-batch outcomes, or end-to-end workload measurements.
