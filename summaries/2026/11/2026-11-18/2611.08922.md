# Glyph: An Interpretability Engine for AI-Driven Scientific Discovery in Clinical Data

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.08922) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08922)


## One-sentence takeaway

Glyph extracts interpretable candidate risk markers from frozen clinical foundation-model embeddings, and in retrospective abdominal CT cohorts many associations replicated across sites while selected concepts improved prediction over evaluated literature-derived markers.

## Problem and motivation

Manual phenotype annotation is difficult to scale, and automating a predefined marker list still limits which associations researchers can discover. The authors identify a gap between the prognostic information encoded in clinical foundation-model embeddings and the ability to isolate, interpret, and test individual signals across patient populations. (§1)

## Approach and contributions

- **Clinical concept extraction.** Glyph trains a separate Matryoshka sparse autoencoder on frozen embeddings of abdominal CT volumes, radiology reports, and lab histories. It freezes the resulting extractors for use in held-out cohorts. An LLM names features by contrasting high- and low-activation examples, then uses those descriptions to classify held-out examples. (§§2.1, 4.2–4.4, Fig. 2)
- **Prognostic discovery and replication.** Glyph screens individual concepts against future coded outcomes, retests discovery associations in held-out cohorts, and uses double machine learning to estimate which associations persist after adjustment for measured covariates and selected concepts. (§§2.2–2.3, 4.5–4.8)
- **Comparisons and reusable tools.** The authors compare Glyph with reconstructed literature markers and ICD-derived Phecodes. They report releasing the library, pretrained concept dictionaries, and an MCP interface, illustrated with user-guided hypothesis testing and concept queries. (§1, §§2.4–2.5, 4.11)

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

The authors report that Phecodes yielded more significant associations overall. | §§1, 3, referenced Appendix A.2 |

The results support cross-site reproducibility and incremental discrimination for selected outcomes in these retrospective cohorts. Gains varied by outcome, with no significant improvement for dementia or Alzheimer’s disease in the literature comparison. The evidence leaves causality, clinical benefit, and whether apparently underexplored associations are absent from the wider literature unresolved. (§§2.3–2.4, 3)

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ limitations.** Selection for abdominal CT likely enriched the cohorts for abdominal disease. ICD coding can miss cases or record existing disease late. Adjustment depends on measured covariates and a correlation heuristic to which the results were sensitive. Some concept labels describe several co-occurring findings, leaving their precise meaning uncertain. (§§2.2, 3, 4.8)
- **Analyst inference.** The screen prioritizes outcomes already predictable from the embeddings and carries forward only positive associations, so its yield applies to that selected search space. Treating repeat CT studies from the same patient as independent may understate uncertainty if those observations are correlated. (§§4.1, 4.5–4.6, 4.8–4.9)
- **Comparison ambiguity.** §2.4 describes literature-marker replication using \(p<0.05\), while §4.9 specifies BH-adjusted \(q<0.05\). The supplied text leaves that discrepancy unresolved. The comparison also covers only markers the authors could reconstruct or proxy, and excludes External Site #2. (§§2.4, 4.9)

## Open research questions

1. **Do the systemic risk markers transfer beyond patients referred for abdominal CT?** Referral may enrich the relevant disease patterns. Apply frozen concept extractors in cohorts with different CT indications, and test lab concepts in a broader cohort without requiring CT. (§3)
2. **How often do the concepts predict true incident disease?** First recorded codes can follow actual onset. Compare associations against blinded longitudinal chart adjudication using prespecified diagnostic criteria and onset dates. (§3)
3. **What does the mixed hepatobiliary concept measure?** Its interpretation combines several findings. Have radiologists score those findings separately in clinically matched high- and low-activation scans, then test which findings account for activation and prognostic signal. (§§2.3, 3)
