# Attention at Absolute Zero

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.11937) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11937)


## One-sentence takeaway

The paper proves a tight worst-case temperature–margin bound for detecting whether a witness exists and shows that layers designed around walks or card pairs can generalize to larger inputs on graph search and SET.

## Problem and motivation

Transformers can represent graph search and SET, yet the reported models struggle to learn search as graphs grow and require many distinct deals to learn SET. The author asks why higher-order attention helps on some tasks and yields little on others. The proposed explanation is that a layer’s learning behavior depends on which objects it directly aggregates, such as tokens, pairs, or walks, and whether its readout can distinguish a true witness from a growing number of false candidates (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Witness-family framework.** Expresses several attention and graph layers as partition functions over task-relevant objects, with readouts for averaging, counting, existence, or free energy. Matching the witness family to the task and using a closed form where available are hypotheses tested experimentally (§2, Table 1).
- **Size and depth laws.** Proves the tight worst-case condition \(\beta\Delta \ge \log |F|\) for thresholding a finite exponential sum to decide existence. Adapts known logical locality to two-step pair layers, with a Lean-checked construction and a paper proof of the lower bound for anonymous initial states (§2, Theorems 1–2; Methods, Lean).
- **Three layers.** The propagator sums walks through a linear solve with learned, row-capped gates. Newton attention uses power sums to aggregate subsets. Exponential Newton combines those identities with a finite Fourier expansion to compute pair softmax exactly for the specified clock-based scores (§§3–4).
- **Sequential graph outputs.** Proves that selecting the representative required by CLRS-30’s strongly connected components task is P-complete and tests how additional iterations affect errors on that task and depth-first search (§6, Proposition 4; Appendix A).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Sharp depth and size bounds | \(L\) two-step pair layers can decide reachability up to \(2^L-1\) steps from a candidate child, with a matching worst-case bound for anonymous initial states. For a finite family \(F\) with at least two candidates and scores free within the stipulated margin, thresholding the exponential sum succeeds on every input iff \(\beta\Delta\ge\log |F|\), using a strict threshold at equality. | §2, Theorems 1–2; Appendix A |
| One propagator transfers to larger search graphs | Trained on balanced graphs of up to 49 vertices, all three runs answered every balanced test question at all 124 lookaheads on 250-vertex graphs, with 200 questions per lookahead. Accuracy on the benchmark’s separate random graphs at that size was 99.0–100%. | §3, Fig. 2; Supplementary Table 2 |
| Pair aggregation improves SET data efficiency and size transfer | On new deals, the 2-simplicial join and four-clock exponential Newton first exceeded 90% with 8,192 training deals, versus 524,288 for the larger Transformer. Six-clock exponential Newton trained on 8-card tables achieved at least 97% on both 16- and 20-card tables with unseen hand cards across three seeds in both same-seed repeats, with the learning rate selected on held-out hand cards. | §4, Fig. 3; Methods, SET |
| Small looped block is competitive on CLRS-30 | Trained without hints on graphs of up to 16 nodes and tested at 64 nodes, the roughly 186,000-parameter block matched within half a micro-F1 point or beat published no-hint FloydNet scores on 9 of 12 tasks on the official file. The paper reports 8 of 12 on fresh graphs, where it counts Dijkstra’s 0.003-point shortfall as a miss. FloydNet’s smallest reported configuration has about 73 million parameters. | §6, Fig. 5a; Supplementary Table 3 |
| More loops help DFS more than component representative selection | At 64 nodes, extending the training loop range and using 34 test loops raised mean DFS accuracy from 80.5% to 90.7% across three seeds. The analogous change raised accuracy on order-dependent component representatives from 69.0% to 72.8%, within the reported seed spread. | §6, Fig. 5b; Methods, CLRS-30 |

The theorems establish guarantees for their specified update and score classes. The experiments show transfer on the tested graph and SET distributions. The witness-matching and closed-form principles remain empirical hypotheses, and the depth lower bound does not apply to the trained models that start from vertex names (§§2, 5).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported scope.** The main positive results use small models on synthetic and algorithmic tasks. The language-model experiment used one scale, budget, and join window. Chess joins yielded little improvement even on the selected motifs involving pairs of pieces (§§1, 6).
- **Author-reported computational limits.** Exponential Newton assumes a known group, its expansion costs \(O(3^d n)\) for \(d\) ternary clocks and \(n\) table cards, and the paper gives no long-sequence timings for it. The reported propagator speed and memory comparison used the uncapped variant (§§3–4; Methods, Models).
- **Analyst inference on comparison validity.** CLRS-30 block design used the official test graphs, and FloydNet’s published scores came from a different training protocol. Fresh graphs reduce the effect of test-set design selection, while the protocol difference limits attribution to architecture (§6; Methods, CLRS-30).

## Open research questions

1. **When does exponential Newton become faster than explicit pair attention?** The paper gives an operation count and reports that enumeration is cheaper at SET training sizes. Benchmark both forms across sequence lengths and clock counts with matched precision and hardware (§4; Methods, Models).
2. **How far does learned propagation transfer under graph distribution shift?** The search benchmark uses directed acyclic graphs, while the row cap guarantees convergence on cyclic graphs. Train on the original distribution and test on cyclic graphs with independently varied density and path length (§3; Methods, Search benchmark).
3. **What limits learning of order-dependent component representatives?** Extra loops helped DFS but gave only a small improvement on these representatives. Sweep width and loop count independently, then score component membership and representative selection separately (§6, Proposition 4).
