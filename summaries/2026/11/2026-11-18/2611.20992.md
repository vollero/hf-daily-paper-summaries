# MedJev: A System One Vision-Language Decision Model for Fast and Effective Medical Image Understanding

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.20992) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20992)


## One-sentence takeaway

MedJev-4B scores supplied answers in a single forward pass and, on four closed-choice medical imaging benchmarks, improves unweighted mean accuracy by 7.93 percentage points and delivers a 6.04× end-to-end speedup over off-the-shelf Qwen3.5-4B on one NVIDIA H20 at batch size one (Table 1, §4.1.3).

## Problem and motivation

Medical image classification and multiple-choice visual question answering often require selecting one supplied answer. Generative vision-language models (VLMs) spend time decoding a response, and the evaluation parser can reject a response that fails to identify a unique option. The authors identify an opportunity to score candidates directly while retaining the full visual token sequence produced within the configured input budget (§§1–2, Appendix C).

## Approach and contributions

- **Unified decision interface.** MedJev packages the images, question, and candidate texts into one prompt with learned markers. One shared head scores all candidates in one pass for both classification and multiple-choice VQA (§§3.1, 3.4).
- **Language-side depth reduction.** The Qwen3.5-4B implementation retains the vision encoder, connector, and first 23 of 32 language layers. It reuses the final layer’s query and key branches and removes the vocabulary output pathway (§4.1.3, Appendix C).
- **Residual decision head.** The head matches a decision marker’s query against each option marker’s key. A shared MLP with a zero-initialized output adds a learned correction. The authors combine this head with depth reduction and train one model using LoRA and weighted candidate cross-entropy (§§2.2, 3.3–3.4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Higher average accuracy and lower latency | Across four benchmarks, MedJev-4B scored 69.06% vs 61.13% for Qwen3.5-4B and took 113.44 vs 685.03 ms in mean end-to-end latency. Both metrics are unweighted benchmark averages, measured on one H20 at batch size one. MedJev led the 20 off-the-shelf VLMs in average accuracy and latency, and was fastest on three benchmarks. | §1, §§4.1.2–4.2, Table 1 |
| Accuracy gain was uncertain on PneumoniaMNIST | Paired 95% bootstrap intervals for the gain over Qwen3.5-4B excluded zero on HAM10000, MedThinkVQA, and PMC-VQA. The PneumoniaMNIST gain was 1.44 percentage points, with an interval of −0.80 to 3.69 points. | §4.3.2, Fig. 4c |
| Depth reduction saved time and the residual added a small accuracy gain | In the four-benchmark ablation, depth reduction cut mean end-to-end latency by about 16 ms, with no measured drop in unweighted mean accuracy under either matching head. At reduced depth, adding the residual to query–key matching raised mean accuracy from 68.70% to 69.06% and added 1.4 ms. | §4.3.1, Fig. 4a–b |
| Accuracy lead over released decision models | With only the first image per case, MedJev scored 69.02% unweighted mean accuracy across four benchmarks vs 57.82% for the strongest of six released comparators, d3-flash-9B. MedJev averaged 56.8 ms over the three single-image benchmarks and ranked second in latency. | §4.3.5, Tables 2 and A2 |
| Shorter latency tail on multi-image cases | On 150 timed MedThinkVQA requests per model with thinking off, p99 end-to-end latency was 625 ms for MedJev-4B vs 27,814 ms for Qwen3.5-4B on one H20 at batch size one. | Table A4, Appendix D.5 |
| Stable aggregate accuracy masked changes in individual answers | With three candidate-order permutations per example, unweighted mean accuracy fell from 69.06% in native order to 68.10%, with both losses and gains among individual decisions. | §4.3.3, Table A3 |

The evidence supports a favorable benchmark accuracy–latency trade-off for the trained system under the reported protocol. The authors attribute both gains to removing decoding (§4.2). **Analyst inference:** the main comparison measures the combined effects of medical fine-tuning, architecture, and answer interface, so it cannot isolate the accuracy effect of removing decoding. The reported tests also leave clinical effectiveness and transfer to unseen datasets open.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported scope.** Evaluation uses train and test splits from the same four benchmark sources, with a supplied candidate set and exactly one matching reference. Appendix C specifies image and request token caps. The authors leave broader vision-language decisions and complex perception tasks for future work (Appendices A and C, §5).
- **Analyst inference — baseline comparability.** The generative baselines use off-the-shelf weights and a fixed parser, while MedJev receives benchmark-specific training. The paper reports parser rejections and context overflows for some baselines. Relative accuracy therefore depends partly on the evaluation interface, and the first-image comparison cannot establish a multi-image advantage over other decision models (§§4.1.2, 4.2, 4.3.5, Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference — robustness and deployment.** The residual head’s gain is reported without uncertainty across training seeds. Candidate order changes individual outcomes, and pooled calibration and selective-answering results leave subgroup reliability and real-world referral performance untested (§§4.3.1–4.3.3, Table A1, Appendix D.2–D.3).

## Open research questions

- **How much of the accuracy gain comes from the decision interface?** The backbone baseline is off the shelf. Fine-tune a generative Qwen3.5-4B on the same examples, constrain its output to valid options, and compare it with full-depth and reduced-depth decision variants.
- **Does the residual head improve accuracy consistently?** Its reported gain is small and the training configuration lists one seed. Repeat the query–key-only and residual-head runs across seeds and report paired test intervals alongside run-to-run variation.
- **When does reading additional images improve a decision?** The released-model comparison uses only the first image. Evaluate first-image, all-image, and image-subset inputs on the same cases, stratifying accuracy and latency by case image count.
- **Does confidence support referral on unseen data?** Selective answering is evaluated on pooled benchmark test examples. Fix a threshold on held-out validation data and measure accuracy, coverage, and error rates by class on an external cohort.

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the architecture and reported benchmark comparisons. The supplied text does not establish checkpoint availability, uncertainty across training seeds, or performance on external cohorts. It gives an answer-extraction rule but does not fully specify each generative baseline’s decoding settings. It also does not explain how preprocessing enforces the per-request visual-token cap on long multi-image cases.
