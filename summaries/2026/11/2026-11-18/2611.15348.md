# Breath-Dx: A Time Series Classification Benchmark for Disease Screening with eNose

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.15348) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15348)


## One-sentence takeaway

Breath-Dx benchmarks electronic-nose screening on held-out collection weeks, where the selected comparisons score lower than on random splits and the strongest classifier varies by condition (Tables III–V).

## Problem and motivation

Electronic noses offer a non-invasive way to screen for disease from exhaled breath, but sensor responses can change over time. The authors identify a lack of public, multi-condition clinical datasets and standardized temporal evaluation. They argue that small private cohorts and inconsistent splitting protocols make it difficult to compare classifiers or estimate how they will perform on later samples (§§I–II).

## Approach and contributions

- **Clinical dataset.** Scent of Health (S-OH) contains one 17-channel breath time series per participant for 1,234 participants across eight disease groups and healthy controls. The authors report releasing the dataset and its temporal splits (§IV, Table I).
- **Benchmark protocol and baselines.** Breath-Dx uses one-vs-rest classification with patient-separated, disease-specific held-out weeks. It compares 10 methods across four families plus a raw-signal logistic regression baseline. The authors report releasing the evaluation protocol and code (§§III, V, VIII).
- **Temporal and demographic comparisons.** The study compares random and temporal splits, tests how well raw signals predict measurement day, and reports demographic-only and subgroup baselines (§§V-F–G, VI-B–C).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

| Different models lead on different tasks | On fixed temporal tests, mean ROC AUC ± SD across 16 training splits was 0.746 ± 0.030 for InceptionTime on lung cancer, 0.767 ± 0.061 for Graphormer on COPD, and 0.698 ± 0.011 for CatBoost + Stats_5 on hepatitis. | §§III-C, V-A, VI-A, Table III |
| LSTM leads the reported cross-task ranking | LSTM has mean temporal ROC AUC of 0.598 across nine tasks and is reported in the top three on five. Table IV omits the logistic regression baseline. | §VI-A, Table IV |
| Breath-only and demographics-only performance varies by condition | On the temporal benchmark, age-only CatBoost scores 0.678 for lung cancer and 0.803 for tuberculosis, versus best listed sensor-only means of 0.746 and 0.688, respectively. For gastritis and fatty liver disease, best listed sensor-only means are 0.718 and 0.686, versus 0.575 and 0.497 for the best listed demographics-only models. | Tables III and VI |
| Raw signals encode measurement time | CatBoost predicts measurement day with a reported median absolute error of 2–3 days. The text describes training on the full dataset and leaves the evaluation split unspecified. | §V-G |

The selected split comparisons show lower discrimination on held-out weeks. They leave the share attributable to sensor drift uncertain because recruitment also changes over time (§IV-A). The demographic comparisons show that breath-only models can outperform demographic-only models on some tasks. Establishing incremental value requires comparing a combined model against a demographics-only model. Prospective triage performance remains untested (§VII-E).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ stated limitations.** Both sites used the same device, cross-device and cross-batch validation remain open, and the benchmark includes neither active drift correction nor prospective validation. Ensemble tuning was limited (§§V-B, VII-B, VII-E).
- **Analyst inference — site and time are entangled.** Tuberculosis cases came from the second site in the final collection period, where recruitment was limited to tuberculosis cases and healthy controls. Site, timing, and cohort composition could all influence the tuberculosis results (§IV-A).
- **Analyst inference — several narrative claims exceed the displayed evidence.** Table V shows four selected comparisons, leaving the all-method degradation claim incompletely documented. Table III lists a tuberculosis ROC AUC of 0.688 for logistic regression despite the broad near-chance description. The older-cohort row in Table VI is malformed in the supplied text, so the column assignment behind the reported older-cohort lung cancer result cannot be verified (§§VI-A–C, VII-A).

## Open research questions

1. **How much of the temporal performance drop is recoverable through drift correction?** The benchmark leaves active correction untested. Compare corrected and uncorrected pipelines on identical held-out weeks, with correction fitted using training data only (§§IV-D, VII-B).
2. **Do breath signals improve prediction after accounting for age and gender?** Separate sensor-only and demographic-only models leave incremental value open. Compare demographic-only and combined models on the same held-out patients, with paired uncertainty estimates (Tables III and VI).
3. **Do the lung cancer and COPD results transfer to new sites and hardware?** The study used one device and did not report a site-held-out evaluation. Prospectively evaluate fixed models in a cohort that includes target cases and controls at each site and uses multiple devices or batches (§§IV-A, VII-E).

## Confidence and missing information

**Medium.** The benchmark design and the main per-condition scores are explicit. The supplied text lacks the full random-versus-temporal results and detailed random-split protocol, a clear test procedure for measurement-day prediction, and reliably aligned cells in the rendered Tables II and VI. It also lacks prospective or cross-device results and a combined breath-plus-demographics comparison.
