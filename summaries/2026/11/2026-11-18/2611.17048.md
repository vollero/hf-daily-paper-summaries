# Beyond Answering: Learning to Speak First and Act in Streaming Video

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.17048) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17048)


## One-sentence takeaway

AURA 2.0 learns when to start an unprompted conversation from streaming video and prior dialogue, with targeted evaluations showing useful openings on a small internal benchmark, higher video QA accuracy with tools enabled, and fewer missed bounding boxes on a still-image diagnostic.

## Problem and motivation

Streaming video assistants can wait for evidence before answering, but the authors identify a different decision as largely unaddressed: whether an unfolding event warrants starting a conversation without a pending question or standing instruction. An assistant must choose a topic and a moment to speak, while using prior dialogue to avoid repeating itself or interrupting unnecessarily (§§1–2.1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Conversation initiation.** AURA 2.0 extends AURA’s chunk-wise architecture, initialized from Qwen3.5-9B. For each video chunk, a control token determines whether the model stays silent or responds. Model-Initiated Conversation (MIC) uses the observed video and dialogue history to choose when and what to say on its own initiative (§§3, 5).
- **Tool use and grounding.** Stream-Aware Tool Calling (SATC) selects tools and forms arguments from the current scene and dialogue, then incorporates returned results. Interactive Visual Grounding (IVG) interprets user-marked regions and can return frame-aligned bounding boxes (§3.2).
- **Data and training.** The authors extend AURA’s training mixture with time-aligned, model-verified examples for the new tasks. A three-stage supervised curriculum establishes streaming QA and tool use, adds MIC and IVG with replay, then trains on combinations of capabilities. Class-balanced focal loss addresses the dominance of silence labels (§§4–5).
- **Memory and serving.** Long-Horizon Interaction Memory (LHIM) compresses older visual events into timestamped text summaries that become coarser over time. Session caching, fast paths for silent turns, and early speech synthesis reduce response latency (§6).

The public benchmarks support performance on streaming QA and user-specified monitoring. Direct evidence for unprompted conversation comes from MIC-Bench, where the two settings use different scoring units and aggregation rules. Their scores cannot isolate the effect of prior dialogue. The same-model tool comparison supports an accuracy gain on the tested questions. The grounding diagnostic supports improved box coverage on still images, while temporal tracking and user-marked inputs remain unverified by that test.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** The usefulness of an opening depends on individual preferences for topic, timing, and frequency. The cascaded speech recognition, video-language, and speech synthesis pipeline lacks simultaneous listening, viewing, and speaking. PhoStream also shows a remaining missed-response problem (§§7.2.4, 8).
- **Analyst inference.** MIC-Bench uses 25 videos and model-judged quality scores reviewed by annotators. In the dialogue setting, messages outside the annotated window contribute to speaking frequency but are excluded from quality scoring. These measurements leave user tolerance for interruptions uncertain (§7.3).
- **Analyst inference.** The reported experiments do not isolate the contributions of LHIM, the staged curriculum, or focal weighting to interaction quality. Benchmark comparisons also mix author-run and previously reported results, and IPIBench uses an offline protocol for the Qwen backbone configurations (§§7.1–7.2).

## Open research questions

1. **Does preference conditioning improve when the assistant speaks?** MIC-Bench uses different protocols across context settings, and the authors identify individual preferences as unresolved. Run a within-user study with matched clips and a common rubric for helpful openings, unwanted interruptions, and missed opportunities (§§7.3, 8).
2. **How much does LHIM improve recall as memories become coarser?** StreamArena reports historical accuracy for the full system. Compare LHIM enabled and disabled with the same checkpoint and visual window, then measure recall by event age and detail required (§§6.1, 7.2.2).
3. **Can IVG maintain the same target through motion and occlusion?** The quantitative diagnostic uses still images. Evaluate marked targets in streaming clips with occlusion, re-entry, and target switching, scoring frame-level localization and abstention (§§4.1, 7.5).
