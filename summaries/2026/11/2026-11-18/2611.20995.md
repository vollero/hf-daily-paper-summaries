# CONGA: Compiling Gaussian Fiber Geometry for Structured Bayesian Inference

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.20995) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20995)


## One-sentence takeaway

CONGA uses model-proven conditional Gaussian structure to sample and learn transports on a smaller posterior core, improving minimum bulk effective sample size per second by 12.5× for NUTS on the Stock–Watson inflation model (§3.1, Appendix B.7).

## Problem and motivation

Structured Bayesian models often contain a large latent block that is Gaussian conditional on a smaller nonlinear core. Generic transport methods can spend warmup learning geometry already specified by the model, and fixed whitening cannot track conditional moments that vary with the core. The authors identify an implementation gap: the same exact Gaussian marginalization can have very different costs depending on the precision graph, elimination order, and balance of score evaluations and posterior reconstructions (§1, §2.2).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Exact factorization and core-only learning.** Whitening with the conditional mean and precision makes the Gaussian block standard normal and independent of the core. Symmetric Brenier and triangular Knothe–Rosenblatt (KR) maps are members of the same affine family. The paper shows that KL and relative Fisher objectives for residual transports reduce to the core, and extends the reduction to compatible population gradient flows (§2.1, Proposition 1, Corollary 2, Proposition 3).
2. **Discovery with model-level validation.** CONGA uses score secants and colored probes to propose a block and recover its precision structure. Marginalization requires a global identity established by model algebra or symbolic analysis. A numerically supported candidate can be used with the full change-of-variables density, and a failed validation returns the original target (§2.2.1, Appendix A.3–A.4).
3. **Workload-based implementation selection.** The compiler weighs construction, score, and reconstruction costs across temporal Kalman programs, cached spectral Brenier maps, sparse triangular KR maps, and the original target. Graph coloring, elimination fill, and bandwidth inform the decision (§2.2.2–2.2.3, Table 1).

The authors define a pass as rank-\(\widehat R\leq1.05\) in every seed. The inflation NUTS comparison supports an efficiency gain under that criterion. The electricity-load comparison and the WALNUTS comparison support a change in convergence diagnostics, with raw-chain ESS serving as a failure diagnostic. The matched spectral experiment isolates implementation cost under a fixed collapsed target and sampler. The Fisher result measures transport fit in the tested DLM. How well these gains transfer across workloads, models, and flow architectures remains open.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Finite probes cannot prove global conditional Gaussianity, and greedy discovery does not guarantee the largest eligible block. The implementation uses supplied temporal and graph templates, cannot infer commuting matrix pencils, and reconstructs temporal draws sequentially. Non-Gaussian and discrete blocks require different maps. The population Stein result also requires compatible geometry and has a finite-particle qualification (§4, Appendices A.1, A.4).
- **Author-stated.** Main comparisons use three seeds, and the largest electricity-load extension was tested only for kernel evaluation. The authors also caution that the core-size crossover extrapolation is model-specific and rests on three tested core sizes (§4, Appendices B.4.2, B.8).
- **Analyst inference.** The core-size sweeps change both dimension and the amount of data informing each scale. They therefore leave the independent effect of core dimension unresolved. The graph study likewise leaves the separate effects of fill, degree, and spectral gap unresolved (Appendices B.4.2–B.5).
- **Analyst inference.** Raw and transformed NUTS use different warmup budgets in the real-data and Student-t checks. These comparisons evaluate the reported configurations, so attribution to the map alone remains limited (Appendices B.6, B.9).

## Open research questions

1. **Can automatic discovery and backend generation scale to much larger targets?** The largest load model uses a supplied algebraic contract, and temporal templates are hand-written. Compare a sparse discovery and code-generation pipeline against supplied contracts across increasing model sizes, measuring peak memory, agreement with reference scores, and end-to-end sampling cost (Appendices A.4, B.8).
2. **When does core-only learning lose its advantage?** The sweeps vary dimension alongside per-scale information, and the observed trends differ across model families. Vary core dimension and posterior dependence independently while holding the Gaussian block and training budget fixed, then compare held-out Fisher divergence and convergence-gated ESS per second (Appendices B.4–B.5).
3. **How reliably does the pilot select the lowest-cost valid program?** The case studies show workload-dependent trade-offs, while the supplied text gives no aggregate selection error against all eligible backends. On held-out targets, run every eligible backend across reuse counts and reconstruction budgets, then compare the pilot’s choice with measured end-to-end costs (§2.2.2, Appendices B.1.1, B.6).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the stated method and headline comparisons. The supplied text includes theorem statements, protocols, and explicit result summaries. Several cited tables appear only as captions, leaving exact per-arm values and seed ranges unavailable where the prose gives only summaries. The supplied text also leaves the experimental code revision and dependency versions unspecified. Section 3.4 summarizes failures of learned sampling arms, while Appendices B.4.1–B.4.2 report passing hybrid runs under their stated protocols. The digest treats those results as protocol-specific.
