# Recursa: Toward Recursively Self-Improving Foundation Model Systems

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-04.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.04035) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04035)


## One-sentence takeaway
Recursa improves a fixed-model agent’s reusable instructions, skills, and memory through corpus study and multi-agent learning, raising held-out legal-task all-pass rates from 30.0% to 55.0% with DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and from 35.0% to 46.0% with Qwen3.8-Flash under revised rubrics (Table 3).

## Problem and motivation
The authors distinguish a **knowledge gap**—missing domain facts, terminology, and document locations—from a **behavior gap**—incorrectly applying available information. New domains may lack sufficient expert data or reliable rewards for further model training, while long-form legal work requires both finding evidence and judging its relevance.

The identified gap is a system that accumulates knowledge across tasks and improves its own learning process using the same model. The authors argue that prior approaches often address only one gap, rely on stronger models, or evaluate tasks with readily checkable answers (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Two-phase repository learning.** Bootstrapping creates source-linked navigation skills and procedural lessons from corpus exploration and self-generated practice. Subsequent task experience revises instructions, skills, and memory (§§4–5).
- **Recursive multi-agent learning.** Parent learners split training tasks among child learners, merge their revisions, validate merged components, and reflect on learning traces. Leaf learners repeatedly solve, diagnose, edit, and validate, including checks on previously passing tasks (Algorithm 1; Figure 3).
- **Supporting harness.** A persistent Python REPL composes tools and retains intermediate data; an external event log supports selective history retrieval; isolated subagents distribute work (§3).
- **Empirical contribution.** The paper evaluates repository adaptation on evidence-intensive legal tasks and documents benchmark-rubric corrections (§6; Appendix B).

The contribution combines these mechanisms into a learning system; several harness and corpus-navigation mechanisms explicitly build on prior work. **Only the repository changes in the experiments: model weights and harness remain fixed** (§2.2).

The results support transfer from training-task experience to held-out tasks **within the same corpus**, with larger improvements in strict task completion than in average criterion satisfaction. Figures 5–6 illustrate better terminology matching and inclusion rules, but do not isolate causal mechanisms.

They do not establish autonomous improvement of weights or harness, sustained gains over successive rounds, or generalization to other domains. Although the same model writes revisions, **Qwen3.8-Max judges every criterion** (§6.1); self-authored improvements therefore should not be interpreted as evaluator-free learning.

## Limitations and validity risks
**Author-stated limitations:** dependence on corpus quality and availability; substantial computation; propagation of incorrect or biased information; dependence on underlying model capability; limited domain coverage; and privacy/security risks from autonomous execution and memory retention (§8).

**Analyst inferences from the reported design:**

- Evaluation covers one synthetic legal environment. Held-out tasks share its corpus, so broader domain transfer remains untested (§6).
- Rubric revisions were developed while analyzing failures and include test tasks. Reporting both rubric versions helps assess sensitivity, but independent adjudication is not described (Appendix B).
- The rule for choosing highlighted learning rounds is unclear; all-round results fluctuate, making checkpoint selection a material uncertainty (Tables 3–4).
- Repeated-run variability, confidence intervals, total adaptation cost, and compute-matched learner comparisons are not reported. Multi-learner superiority cannot therefore be attributed solely to decomposition.
- Separate ablations for self-practice, memory, merge validation, and deeper recursive learning are absent. The reported decomposition uses a parent with leaf learners, leaving deeper recursion empirically unestablished (§6.4).

## Open research questions
- **Does decomposition help at equal cost?** The learner comparison lacks compute matching. Next experiment: compare single- and multi-learner systems under identical token and wall-clock budgets.
- **How can later-round regressions be controlled?** Table 4 shows unstable progress. Next experiment: select checkpoints using a separate validation split and evaluate rollback rules before one final test evaluation.
- **How dependent are gains on judge feedback?** All experiments use Qwen3.8-Max scoring. Next experiment: vary feedback availability and evaluator capability, with independent expert assessment of final answers.
- **Which components transfer beyond this workspace?** Only one domain is evaluated. Next experiment: separately transfer procedural skills and corpus-specific navigation to a new firm corpus, measuring adaptation cost and accuracy.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly documents the method and benchmark results, but supports narrower conclusions than fully recursive system improvement.

Material missing information includes implementation and learned-asset availability, exact prompts and inference settings, total learning budgets and runtime, repeated-run uncertainty, checkpoint-selection rules, independent judge validation, and isolated component ablations.
