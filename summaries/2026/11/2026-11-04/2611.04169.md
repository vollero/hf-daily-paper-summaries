# Length Is Cheap, Competition Is Not: Attention Predicts Context Rot in Retrieval

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-04.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.04169) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04169)


## One-sentence takeaway
In synthetic key-value retrieval, competing statements degrade performance far more than visible neutral filler, while evidence attention predicts loss better than context length without establishing a causal mechanism (§§4–6).

## Problem and motivation
Long-context retrieval failures often accompany increases in both length and distracting content. The author asks which drives degradation, arguing that earlier evaluations insufficiently separate them and lack trial-level measurements connecting evidence attention to retrieval loss (§§1–2). This matters for deciding whether to shorten contexts or remove conflicting information.

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled content comparisons:** Hold context length and evidence position fixed while comparing neutral filler, competing statements, and masked filler against a short-context baseline. The task retrieves a four-digit value associated with a noun (§3).
- **Attention diagnostic:** Select eight heads using their evidence attention in the short context, then measure their mean attention mass on evidence, \(a^*\), from the final query token. Compare loss predictions from \(\log(1/a^*)\) and log length (§3).
- **Conflict analysis and masking controls:** Separate same-key rival values from format-only distractors, and replicate masked-filler degradation on MMLU. The claimed novelty is jointly measuring content, behavior, and attention on the same trials—not discovering distraction or attention dilution (§§2–4).
- **Claimed release:** The abstract states that the probe and trial-level measurements are released; no asset link appears in the supplied text.

## Evidence and main results

Here, loss is \(\Delta=\log(p_{\text{short}}/p_{\text{condition}})\), measured in nats (§3).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Content strongly changes loss at matched length and position | Qwen2.5-1.5B at 32K tokens: median loss is 0.004 nats with visible neutral numeric filler, 1.20 with masking, and 4.76 with competing statements. | §4 opening |
| Neutral filler is usually inexpensive, with an exception | Qwen2.5-1.5B at 8K–32K: median loss ≤0.006 nats across three neutral fillers; accuracy 0.96–1.00. OLMo-2-1B at 2,048 tokens loses 0.57 nats, with accuracy 0.91. | §4.1 |
| Same-key conflicts dominate measured competition | Across five models at lengths ≤2,048: mean loss is 0.02–0.34 nats without conflicts versus 1.38–3.24 with at least one; accuracy is 0.97–1.00 versus 0.25–0.52. | §4.2; Table 3 caption |
| Attention predicts loss better than length | Across 126,473 trials, the five-model analysis at 512–2,048 tokens reports R² of 0.35–0.58 for \(\log(1/a^*)\), versus 0.02–0.08 for log length; bootstrap intervals for the differences exclude zero. | §4.3; Table 4 caption |
| The predictor advantage extends to longer contexts | At 8K–32K, attention versus length R² is 0.32 versus 0.02 on Qwen2.5-1.5B and 0.33 versus 0.03 on Llama-3.2-3B. | §4.3 |
| Masking is materially different from visible irrelevant text | Qwen2.5-1.5B at 32K: masking numeric filler costs 46 accuracy points; across three fillers, visible accuracy is 0.96–0.99 versus 0.48–0.54 masked. | §4.4; Table 2 discussion |
| Reasoning-task results limit a universal “length is free” claim | Qwen2.5-1.5B MMLU at 4,096 tokens: accuracy is 59.1% unpadded, 57.5% with visible neutral filler, 53.9% masked, and 36.0% with visible whitespace. | §4.4 |

These results support a strong content effect in this retrieval setup and show that masking cannot substitute for a visible neutral-text control. They do not establish that attention loss causes retrieval failure, that length never matters, or that conflict filtering improves deployed systems.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Attention and loss come from the same forward pass; no attention intervention establishes causality (§6).
- **Author-stated:** Evaluation centers on one synthetic retrieval task and models no larger than 3B; the 7B model appears only in the MMLU replication (§6).
- **Author-stated:** Conflict counts grow with length, and all competing contexts at 4,096 tokens or longer contain conflicts. Format-only competition above 2K therefore remains unmeasured; conflict analysis was not preregistered (§6).
- **Author-stated:** Attention uses eight selected heads and one query token, retaining attention sinks. Different value tokenizations prevent direct cross-model loss comparisons (§6).
- **Author-stated:** MMLU lacks a visible-filler arm beyond 4K, preventing attribution of longer masked-context degradation solely to positional gaps (§4.4).
- **Analyst inference:** The supplied task description does not explain how a uniquely authoritative statement is distinguished from contradictory statements in the same template, limiting interpretation of conflict errors as retrieval failures (§3).

## Open research questions
1. **Does preserving evidence attention improve retrieval causally?** The paper measures association only. Intervene on selected heads or attention scores while holding content fixed, then measure accuracy and loss (§6).
2. **Can format-only competitors cause degradation at long lengths?** Long competing contexts always contain same-key conflicts. Generate conflict-free competing contexts across the tested length range (§6).
3. **Does the diagnostic transfer to realistic documents and larger models?** Current retrieval evidence is synthetic and limited to small models. Repeat matched content controls on document retrieval with larger open models (§6).
4. **What explains long-context MMLU degradation?** Visible controls stop at 4K. Extend paired visible-neutral and masked conditions to the longer replication settings (§4.4).
