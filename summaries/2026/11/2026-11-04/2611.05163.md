# RoboTryOn: Synthesizing Dexterous Robot Demonstrations from Human Videos via Virtual Try-On

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-04.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.05163) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05163)


## One-sentence takeaway
RoboTryOn translates human manipulation videos into robot videos with inferred actions, raising reported average performance on tasks without task-specific robot demonstrations from 2.1% to 60.0% across two robots under a fixed-camera tabletop setup (§§4.1–4.2; Appendix A).

## Problem and motivation
Adapting robot policies requires costly teleoperated demonstrations, especially for dexterous hands. Human videos are easier to collect, but differ from robot demonstrations in appearance and executable actions. The authors argue that masking or rendering robots leaves visual gaps, while retargeting reconstructed human hands produces unreliable grasps because of reconstruction errors and different hand geometries (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Embodiment translation:** Autoregressively inpaint selected human keyframes using a target robot reference image, then synthesize intervening video conditioned on hand skeleton motion. Shared masks and skeletons let the translation models train on robot demonstrations without paired human–robot videos (§§3.2–3.3).
- **Action labeling:** A depth-pretrained inverse dynamics model predicts joint commands from synthesized frames. Reconstructed human wrist positions subsequently correct arm translations through inverse kinematics; predicted hand joints remain unchanged (§3.4; §§B.2.6–B.2.7).
- **Policy-learning demonstration:** The authors combine these generated observations and pseudo actions to fine-tune robot policies, showing transfer to tasks absent from task-specific teleoperation and deployment on raw camera observations (§§3.5, 4.2).

The contribution is the integrated embodiment-translation and labeling pipeline; keyframe-based synthesis, flow matching, and inverse dynamics individually build on prior work (§2).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Large gains on tasks without task-specific robot demonstrations | With π₀.₅, adding synthesized demonstrations raises seven-task average scores from **3.1% to 73.0%** on OpenArm and **1.0% to 46.9%** on RB-Y1. EgoMimic achieves **5.1%** on each; Masquerade achieves **8.2% / 7.7%**. Each policy receives 28 trials per task. | Table 1; §4.1 |
| Synthetic-only policy training also transfers | Without teleoperated episodes in policy fine-tuning, seven-task averages are **48.5% / 40.3%** on OpenArm/RB-Y1. The synthesis models and IDM still use teleoperated training data. | Table 1; §B.2.8 |
| Bimanual transfer is partial and embodiment-dependent | With combined data, three-task bimanual averages are **63.1% / 28.6%**, despite single-arm task demonstrations for policy training. RB-Y1 Air Fryer remains **0.0%**. | Tables 1, 11; §F.4.2 |
| Synthesized frames are closer in policy feature space | Across all eleven tasks, Fréchet SigLIP distance to real four-task PnP frames is **32.7**, versus human **125.5**, EgoMimic **129.0**, and Masquerade **58.2**; analysis samples 5,000 frames per corpus. | Table 7; Appendix D |
| Generated action labels replay more successfully | Open-loop OpenArm simulation replay on 66 episodes across three PnP tasks succeeds at **69.7%**, versus **34.8%** for retargeting and **63.6%** without wrist correction. | Table 8; §C.2 |
| Motion guidance and wrist correction matter | OpenArm out-of-domain average falls from **73.0%** to **21.4%** without skeleton flow and **48.0%** without wrist correction. | Table 3(a) |
| Benefit extends to another policy backbone | On OpenArm, RLDX-1-Img out-of-domain average rises from **0.0% to 40.3%** when adding RoboTryOn data. | Table 3(b) |
| Lower collection effort in the measured setting | On four OpenArm PnP tasks, 100 translated human videos/task yield **51.8%** success at **1.87 person-hours**, versus 50 teleoperated demonstrations/task yielding **47.3%** at **5.26 person-hours**; 112 evaluation trials per condition. | Table 9; §F.1 |

These results support useful task transfer within the evaluated setup. However, the reported “success rate” includes **half credit for each successful hand action on bimanual tasks**, so aggregate scores do not uniformly measure complete-task success (§C.1). Feature distance and simulated replay provide supporting diagnostics rather than direct proof of physical fidelity. Collection savings measure person-hours and do not establish lower total cost after synthesis compute and embodiment preparation.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Each added embodiment still needs several hours of teleoperated video. Pseudo labels cannot capture forces or sufficient precision for tasks such as tight insertion; wrist correction requires camera calibration (Appendix A).
- **Author-stated:** Human videos match the robot camera position, with fixed neck and base. Moving-camera footage is outside the video model’s and IDM’s training distribution, and infeasible human motions cannot be repaired (Appendix A).
- **Observed failures:** Objects can disappear or fingers penetrate handles during synthesis; inaccurate action labels cause misplaced grasps. RB-Y1 fails Air Fryer because of fine-grained grasping requirements and hand morphology (§F.4.2; Figure 15).
- **Analyst inference:** Generalization evidence is bounded by two physical robots and tabletop tasks. GR-1 translation is qualitative, without policy evaluation (Appendix A; Figure 10).
- **Analyst inference:** Baseline results apply to the authors’ adaptations: Masquerade uses a different inpainter and extends its original pipeline to bimanual dexterous hands (§B.3). The text provides no repeated-training-seed variability or confidence intervals.

## Open research questions
1. **Can the pipeline handle moving cameras?** Fixed viewpoints leave this untested. A next experiment could compare static and moving-camera demonstrations with corresponding moving-camera robot training data (Appendix A).
2. **Can integrated wrist conditioning improve precision?** The current pipeline replaces IDM wrist estimates afterward. Compare that correction with an IDM conditioned directly on reconstructed wrists, including fine-grasp tasks (Appendix A).
3. **When do collection savings outweigh compute costs?** The cost study reports collection effort, while synthesis requires substantial processing. Measure total preparation and compute costs across tasks with different teleoperation difficulty (Tables 5–6, 9–10).
4. **How well does transfer extend to new embodiments?** Shared models support the evaluated robots, but an unseen embodiment still requires data. Vary adaptation-data quantity for another physical robot and measure downstream task scores (Appendix A).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported setup and numerical findings; broader applicability remains uncertain.

Material missing information includes public code/model/data availability, repeated-seed uncertainty, detailed task composition of additional teleoperated recordings, total monetary cost including model training and synthesis, and quantitative evaluation on moving-camera footage or further physical embodiments.
