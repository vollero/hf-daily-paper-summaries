# LOLPlay: Benchmarking Real-Time Visual Agents in League of Legends

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-04.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.03685) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03685)


## One-sentence takeaway
LOLPlay evaluates screenshot-driven agents on 36 continuously running League of Legends tasks, finding at most 25.0% task success and no benefit from high versus low reasoning effort in the tested configurations (§§3.2, 3.5).

## Problem and motivation
Visual agents must act on observations that can become outdated during inference. The authors target sustained control: completing several jointly required objectives while gameplay continues. Their identified gap is champion-specific MOBA evaluation combining uninterrupted interaction with checks of states, event participants, timing, and order; they explicitly acknowledge that real-time evaluation and executable scoring have precedents (§§1, 4).

## Approach and contributions
- **Benchmark:** 36 champion-specific tasks with 82 subgoal checks and 5–20-minute budgets, covering economy, development, sustain, combat, cooperation, and map objectives (§2.1).
- **Execution and evaluation framework:** Automated fresh-match setup; screenshot observations and mouse–keyboard actions; independent API-based scoring of full objectives and partial progress. Evaluator state is withheld from agents (§§2.2–2.4).
- **Empirical study:** GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, and DeepSeek-v4.1-Flash at three effort settings, totaling 324 runs. Analysis examines completion, subgoals, response delay, execution budgets, and selected trajectories rather than introducing a new agent architecture (§3).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Best completion remains limited | Astra low and medium each pass 9/36 tasks (25.0%); Astra medium has the highest task-averaged subgoal success, 40.3%. | §3.2 |
| Partial progress frequently stops short | Across nine configurations, 40/324 runs pass (12.3%); 108/284 failed runs (38.0%) satisfy some required subgoals. | §3.2 |
| Combat and cooperation requirements remain unmet | Across evaluated configurations: 0/45 personal-kill checks and 0/27 allied-assist checks pass; sustain, cooperation, and map-objective families have no full success. | §§3.2–3.3 |
| High effort brings no measured gain over low | Task passes at low/medium/high: Astra 9/9/6, Sol 5/4/4, DeepSeek 2/1/0, each over 36 tasks; high effort also has lower subgoal success than low for every model. | §3.5; Figure 5 |
| Longer requests coincide with fewer interactions for Astra | Low to high effort: mean request latency rises from 7.47 to 16.67 seconds, mean responses per episode fall from 43.9 to 27.1, and task success falls from 25.0% to 16.7%. | §3.4; Figure 4 |
| Objectives are feasible for experienced players | Two players, each with more than 200 prior matches, collectively validate completion of all 36 tasks under the same configurations, budgets, and evaluator. | §2.4 |
| A trajectory demonstrates stale observations | During a 144.641-second Astra-medium request, Warwick’s encounter ends; the next frame shows an inactivity dialog. Only the personal Voidgrub-kill check passes. | §3.6; Figure 7 |

These results establish difficulty under the reported protocol, including a gap between individual checks and complete objectives. They do not isolate latency as a cause of failure, establish competitive gameplay strength, or show that additional reasoning is generally harmful. Subgoal success averages fractions within tasks; it is not the pooled fraction of successful checks (§§3.1, 3.5; Appendix B.2).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** One objective per champion and custom matches provide limited coverage of MOBA play. Most tasks contain no bot champions; cooperation means participation in allied-bot kills, not communication with learned teammates (§§2.2, 5).
- **Author-stated metric caveat:** Passive progress, avoidance, and state preservation can satisfy checks without demonstrating active mastery. Ability allocation does not establish casting proficiency (§3.3; Appendix A.4).
- **Author-stated causal limit:** Effort changes are not controlled delay interventions; latency includes service, network, retries, and parsing. Selected cases do not estimate error frequencies (§§3.1, 3.5–3.6).
- **Analyst inference:** The reported design supplies one run per task–configuration pair, without reported repeat-run uncertainty, limiting confidence in small ranking differences (§3.1).
- **Protocol sensitivity:** Response caps can terminate episodes before required duration; final-state versus deadline/event-based evidence differs by task. Client-specific creep-score binning may change with updates (Appendices A.4, B.4).

## Open research questions
1. **How much failure is caused by observation delay?** The comparisons confound delay with model behavior. Hold the model fixed and inject controlled delays while repeating tasks (§3.5).
2. **Can refreshed observations improve objective composition?** Warwick completes an initial objective but misses the follow-up. Compare the current sequential loop with observation refresh and replanning before action execution (§3.6).
3. **How strongly does the response ceiling affect performance?** Some capped episodes retain wall time or fail duration requirements. Repeat those settings with larger response caps under unchanged time budgets (Appendix B.4).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported descriptive findings; **low** for causal explanations.
