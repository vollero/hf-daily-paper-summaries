# Kierston &amp; KierstonBench: Simulation of Allentown, PA and Evaluation of Language Models in Crime Investigation and Surveillance Tasks

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-04.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.04785) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04785)


## One-sentence takeaway
KierstonBench evaluates investigative agents against a simulated city’s hidden ground truth, finding that none of sixteen models identified a perpetrator in the five sampled suspect-identification tasks, while selective abstention improved some models’ overall scores (§9; Tables 15–17).

## Problem and motivation
Investigations require linking records across organizations and distinguishing evidence of presence or association from evidence of responsibility. The authors argue that real investigative records rarely provide complete ground truth, while independently synthesized tables lack a shared behavioral explanation. They identify a gap in evaluating whether language models can actively retrieve records and draw supported conclusions from them (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Kierston simulation engine:** Models approximately 125,000 Allentown residents using public population and geographic inputs. Programmed behavior and reproducible random draws produce a shared activity timeline, from which telecommunications, financial and plate-reader records derive, including incomplete observations and measurement errors (§§3–3.11).
- **KierstonBench:** Provides 32 task families covering routine reconstruction, incident investigation and deceptive accounts. Models query separate providers without simulation identifiers or hidden event histories; incident tasks enforce evidence cutoffs (§4; Tables 9–13).
- **Evaluation and scoring:** Tests sixteen models through the same coding-agent harness, with a 20-minute investigation allowance. Signed scoring penalizes incorrect identities, permits partial credit and assigns zero to abstention; retained queries and scripts support behavioral analysis (§§4.6–8).

## Evidence and main results

Scores below use the paper’s −1 to 1 scale; abstentions and missing or invalid answers count as zero in all-task means.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Evaluation is small despite broad family coverage | Sixteen models; 32 tasks comprising 22 technical, seven real-time and three deception tasks; one sampled instance per family and one attempt per model. | Tables 15–17; §9 |
| Highest reported overall mean was 0.55 | GPT-6 Sol scored 0.55 across all 32 tasks, versus 0.52 for Claude Fable 5.1 and 0.46 for GPT-6.1 Sol, under the shared investigation allowance. | Table 15; §4.6 |
| Technical performance exceeded real-time investigation performance | Claude Fable 5.1 had the highest technical mean, 0.75 over 22 tasks, but −0.38 over seven real-time tasks. GPT-6 Astra had the highest real-time mean, 0.14. | Table 15 |
| No sampled perpetrator-identification task was solved | No model identified a perpetrator in mass-shooting, shots-fired, explosion, school-shooting or abduction; answers were penalized, abstained or missing. | Table 17; §9 |
| Abstention materially changes comparisons | GPT-6 Sol answered 19 tasks with mean 0.93 and explicitly abstained on 13; GPT-6 Astra answered 17 with mean 0.80 and abstained on 15. Their all-task means were 0.55 and 0.43. | Tables 15–16 |
| Direct linkage was easier in the sampled cases | Every model scored 1.00 on account-resolution, plate-reconciliation and counterparty-reconstruction. | Table 17 |
| Some simulation behavior is calibrated, but validation is partial | Simulated median commute was 19 minutes versus 20 in the ACS sample; messaging volume and call-answer rates were tuned for plausibility rather than fitted to measurements. | §3.11 |

The results establish failures on these sampled synthetic investigations, including unsupported identity attribution. They do not establish stable model rankings or real-world investigative competence. High answered-task means reflect selective coverage as well as answer quality. The authors suggest both uncertainty recognition and general refusal behavior may explain abstention differences (§8).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated sampling limitation:** One attempt on one instance per family makes small score differences unstable; repeated samples are needed (§9).
- **Mixed scenario versions:** Seven models’ disappearance results use an original staging defect in which an abandoned handset still communicated; nine use corrected staging. That comparison is not uniform (§7).
- **Simulation gaps:** Travel uses one mode per person, excludes carpooling, and simplifies routing; weather, holidays and indoor distributed antenna systems are absent. Communications calibration is incomplete (§3.11).
- **Evidence availability:** Conversation classification lacks its intended platform-thread evidence path, yet remains scored (§4.5; Table 11).
- **Analyst inference—external validity:** Clean identifier normalization, aligned timestamps and complete valid-query results simplify record handling. Performance may differ with messier provider data (§§4.3, 6; Appendix D.1).
- **Analyst inference—reproducibility:** Released code supports regeneration, but withholding generated outputs and evaluated tasks limits direct reproduction of the reported instances (§10).
- **Analysis coverage:** Detailed Claude behavior analysis covers Opus 5 and Fable 5.1, excluding Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 (§8).

## Open research questions
1. **Do rankings and failure patterns persist across instances?** Single attempts leave variability unresolved (§9). Run repeated seeds and sampled days, reporting uncertainty for scores and incorrect identifications.
2. **Does abstention track evidence sufficiency?** The paper cannot separate uncertainty recognition from general refusal (§8). Compare matched incidents with progressively stronger evidence and measure correct answers, wrong identifications and abstentions.
3. **Can better location reasoning prevent candidate exclusion?** Inspected runs discarded relevant handsets through narrow temporal filters and sector point estimates (§8; Appendix D.4). Compare these procedures with methods that retain spatial ranges and neighboring-time observations.
4. **How sensitive are findings to simulation assumptions?** Communications and mobility contain acknowledged simplifications (§3.11). Vary those assumptions and sensor errors while holding incident roles fixed, then assess performance stability.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the description and reported sampled outcomes; **low** in broad deployment conclusions or fine model rankings.

Material missing information includes repeated-run uncertainty, independently validated real-world investigative performance, per-instance evidence-sufficiency labels, controlled ablations of simulation and interface choices, and the unreleased evaluated tasks and generated records.
