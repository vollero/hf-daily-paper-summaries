# MoLRSI: A Recursive Self-Improving AI agent for Molecular Discovery

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-04.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.39714) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39714)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors report that persistent cross-task memory improves molecular-workflow search, with resulting workflows increasing observed antibiotic screening yield and producing eight pMHC-binding hits under the tested experimental conditions.

## Problem and motivation

Molecular discovery requires coordinating models, training procedures, screening rules and candidate selection while balancing activity, toxicity, novelty or binding specificity. The authors identify a gap between automating an expert-defined workflow and learning how to develop better workflows across successive projects: isolated campaigns discard experience that could guide later searches (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Executable resource libraries:** An Explore Agent converts relevant methods and code into reusable skills, checks their interfaces and validates execution upon first use (§4.1.1; §S1.1).
- **Memory-guided workflow search:** Experience-guided Monte Carlo tree search selects workflow families and parent workflows, proposes modifications, and executes and evaluates candidates. Persistent solution, refinement and execution memories retain scores, successful and unsuccessful edits, resource requirements and repairs (§4.1.2–4.1.3).
- **Cross-task policy adaptation:** Historical performance influences search priorities, while prior edits and repairs guide subsequent actions. The paper’s “recursive self-improvement” denotes this memory-conditioned change in the effective search policy; the supplied description does not establish updating the backbone language model’s weights (§4.1.3; §S1.3).
- **Evaluation across two discovery settings:** Matched memory ablations, direct workflow transfer and prospective experiments assess antibiotic screening and peptide–MHC binder design (§2.2–2.3).

The central contribution is the integration and evaluation of experience-guided workflow development, rather than new underlying molecular prediction or protein-generation models.

The computational ablations support a benefit from prior-task experience in these settings. Experimental results support complete-workflow discovery yield and pMHC enrichment with preference over tested off-targets. They do not establish clinical efficacy, broad proteome-wide specificity or which individual memory component caused the experimental gains.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ stated scope:** Transfer must remain useful as targets and evaluation criteria change; biological validation remains necessary. Incorporating experimental outcomes into policy updates is proposed as future work (§3).
- **Historical antibiotic comparison:** The campaigns used different candidate-selection procedures and nominal cytotoxicity thresholds, including documented exceptions. The authors align the MIC threshold without assuming identical assay protocols and report no between-group hypothesis test (§S3.4–S3.6.3).
- **Analyst inference—limited replication:** Antibiotic MIC and cytotoxicity measurements used technical replicates within single experiments, limiting evidence for independent experimental reproducibility (§4.2.4).
- **Analyst inference—limited causal attribution:** The supplied ablations compare pooled prior memory against none; they do not separately establish the contributions of solution, refinement and execution memories.
- **Analyst inference—evaluation boundaries:** Protein ranking uses sampled negatives, and prospective pMHC validation covers two targets sharing one HLA allele. Enrichment and tested off-target preference do not establish binding affinity or therapeutic function (§S4.1; Table S14; Fig. 5).
- **Comparison qualifications:** Agents retained different native tools and search spaces; some backbone models differed. Fixed BoltzGen, BindCraft and PXDesign comparisons evaluate internal ranking objectives rather than complete design pipelines (§S2.1; §S4.2).
- **Source limitation:** The supplied source was truncated, preventing complete coverage of methods and supplementary evidence.

## Open research questions

1. **Which memory components drive improvement?** The pooled-memory ablation leaves attribution unresolved. A next experiment could remove each memory type separately while matching source experience and compute.
2. **Does transfer remain useful under larger task shifts?** The authors explicitly identify changing targets and criteria as a challenge (§3). Test prospectively routed workflows on increasingly dissimilar tasks, including negative-transfer measurements.
3. **Do recovered pMHC hits retain specificity beyond the screening setting?** Current evidence establishes enrichment over tested off-targets. Measure purified binding affinities and screen a broader peptide-swapped panel across additional HLA alleles.
4. **Does experimental feedback improve later campaigns?** Laboratory-informed policy updates remain future work (§3). Compare sequential campaigns with and without experimental outcomes added to memory.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** Main outcomes and several matched ablations are clearly documented, but the source is truncated and does not support complete coverage.

Material gaps include omitted supplementary protocol details, independent search-run variability, separate memory-component ablations, complete compound-selection exception records, broader pMHC specificity and affinity measurements, and an explicit public release location for MolRSI code and memory artifacts.
