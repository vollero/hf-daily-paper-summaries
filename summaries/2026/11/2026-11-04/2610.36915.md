# Do We Need an LLM to Know Why Sources Conflict? Decision Models for Knowledge-Conflict Typing in RAG

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-04.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.36915) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36915)


## One-sentence takeaway
On CONFLICTS, most tested decision models offer cheaper, faster conflict typing than the rerun Gemini 2.5 Flash baseline, while confidence routing narrows the accuracy gap to Gemini 3.8 Flash without establishing equivalence (§6.1–6.3).

## Problem and motivation
Retrieved sources can disagree because answers are complementary, opinions differ, information is outdated, or a source is false; each cause calls for different answer behavior. The authors investigate whether probabilistic decision models can classify these causes cheaply enough to precede every RAG answer. They identify a gap in evaluations of decision models on relationships among multiple retrieved documents, rather than individual texts (§1–3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Paired model evaluation:** Compare 11 hosted decision models from seven providers with rerun Gemini baselines on identical CONFLICTS inputs. The study introduces an evaluation, rather than a new trained model (§5–6.1).
- **Question formulation:** Compare a direct five-option question with five batched yes/no questions combined through two decision trees. The revised tree was selected using one model’s development results (§4, §6.2).
- **Confidence routing:** Escalate the least confident fraction of queries to Gemini 3.8 Flash, using label-free confidence quantiles and random escalation as a control (§6.3).
- **Diagnostics and downstream evaluation:** Test dates, source ordering, planted misinformation, and generated-answer behavior. The authors report releasing code, prompts, splits, cached responses, and per-query outputs (§6.4–6.5; Appendix B).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Decision models are competitive with the older rerun baseline, but generally trail the newer one. | On 406 test queries, flat decision-model accuracy is 50.0–67.0%, versus Gemini 2.5 Flash’s 61.3% and Gemini 3.8 Flash’s 70.0%. Against Gemini 2.5, eight are nonsignificantly different, two significantly better, and one significantly worse; Gemini 3.8 significantly exceeds eight. | §6.1; Table 2 |
| Classification cost and latency are substantially lower. | Among the ten models matching or exceeding Gemini 2.5 statistically, billed models cost 4–39× less and all are 2.6–6.0× faster. Two were free during the runs. Costs and mean request latency come from one gateway. | §6.1; Table 2 |
| The initial yes/no decomposition consistently hurts. | Tree v1 is significantly less accurate than flat classification for all 11 models. Development-selected tree v2 is significantly better than flat for none, nonsignificantly different for seven, and worse for four. | §6.2; Table 4 |
| Confidence routing improves the cost–accuracy trade-off. | Escalating the least confident 25% to Gemini 3.8 produces no significant accuracy difference from that LLM for eight models, at 25–37% of its classification cost. Liquid d1 Ultra’s cascade reaches 70.9%, versus 70.0% for the LLM alone, p=0.63. | §6.3; Table 5 |
| Date perturbations have little measured effect; source ordering can matter. | Removing dates or adding computed recency ranks yields no significant accuracy changes in 22 decision-model tests. Two source shuffles produce label-flip rates of 5.4–42.1%; GPT-6 Luna loses 4.7 and 5.7 accuracy points on those shuffles. | §6.4; Table 6 |
| Misinformation probabilities contain signal despite weak hard labels. | On 96 paired clean/planted queries, decision-model misinformation-probability AUROC is 0.66–0.86. After accounting for clean-copy false alarms, eight models have significantly worse hard-label detection than Gemini 3.8; three are nonsignificantly different. | §6.4; Table 7 |
| Downstream replacement appears feasible, but the benefit of predicted types depends on the judge. | With Gemini 2.5 generating answers on 406 queries, ten decision predictors have no significant adherence difference from the Gemini 2.5 predictor under the primary judge; all eleven do under the second judge. Pipeline versus no-type adherence is 91.6% versus 87.9% with the primary judge (p=0.032), but 89.7% versus 91.1% with the second (p=0.47). | §6.5; Table 8 |

These results support cheaper classification and useful confidence routing within this benchmark and hosting setup. Nonsignificant differences do **not** establish equivalence. Downstream evidence concerns judged behavior adherence and answer inclusion, not factual grounding. The authors’ explanation that models infer staleness from content is plausible, but the date interventions do not directly establish that mechanism (§6.4–7).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated limitations:** The test set is small and unrepresentative of real search traffic. Only four natural misinformation examples occur in the test split; the planted variant covers a single-source entity/value substitution (§3, §7).
- **Authors’ stated limitations:** Results cover one gateway’s model inventory on one day. Some identifiers are unversioned, pricing included temporary free service, training data and model sizes are undisclosed, and identical requests sometimes change predictions (§5, §7).
- **Authors’ stated limitations:** Downstream evaluation uses one generator and source-blind LLM judges. The primary judge shares the generator’s model, and factual grounding is unmeasured (§7).
- **Statistical qualification:** Tests are not corrected for multiple comparisons. The authors caution that isolated nominally significant results may occur by chance (§5).
- **Analyst inference:** The failure of these particular trees does not establish that hierarchical classification generally fails; both reuse the same batched yes/no answers and fixed combination rules (§4, §6.2).
- **Reproduction discrepancy:** The Gemini 2.5 rerun scores 60.7% on all 458 queries versus the previously published 65.3%. Hosted-model changes and unspecified original input assembly are proposed explanations, not verified causes (§6.1).

## Open research questions
1. **Can cascades meet a predefined equivalence margin?** The current sample cannot establish tight equivalence. Test frozen routing policies on a larger independently annotated query set with a prespecified margin (§6.3).
2. **Can misinformation probabilities support reliable detection?** Planted-source AUROC suggests signal, but natural misinformation is scarcely represented. Select thresholds on development data, then evaluate recall and false alarms on diverse natural conflicts (§6.4).
3. **Do cheaper types preserve grounded answer quality?** Current judges lack sources, and predicted-type gains vary by judge. Repeat the downstream comparison using source-aware human evaluation and additional generators (§6.5–7).
4. **Can uncertainty guide generation directly?** Only hard types are supplied downstream. Compare hard labels against probability-conditioned instructions on ambiguous queries, measuring adherence and factual grounding (§8).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported within-benchmark findings; broader deployment conclusions remain uncertain.

Material unavailable information includes provider training data and model sizes, verified causes of the baseline reproduction gap, natural-misinformation performance at useful scale, source-grounded downstream quality, and independently verified release contents.
