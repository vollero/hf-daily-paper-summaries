# Cross-Prompt Reuse of Entity-Replacement Responses

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-04.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.00352) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00352)


## One-sentence takeaway
Entity-replacement activation changes partly reproduce another question’s answer-score response, but overall fidelity can conceal weak relation selectivity and depends strongly on input format and intervention construction (§§4–6).

## Problem and motivation
Activation transfer can change an answer without showing how faithfully it reproduces the receiving prompt’s response. The authors use explicit entity replacement as a measurable reference: compare a transferred change with the change produced by replacing the entity in the target question itself. They distinguish shifts shared across questions from shifts specific to each queried relation (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Response-fidelity evaluation:** Measure absolute error in the replacement-induced log-probability contrast between two relations’ complete published answers, conditioning on known answer prefixes; separate shared and relation-selective components (§3.2).
- **Matched reuse routes:** Combine changes from two source questions using weights derived from unedited states. Hidden reuse adds aligned activation differences at half depth and continues the target’s layers; direct reuse adds source output-score differences (§3.1).
- **Controlled empirical comparisons:** Vary source relation, replacement identity, model scale, format, depth and row coverage. Identical relation-neutral injections test receiving-question dependence; comparisons with tuned single vectors assess construction dependence (§4).

The contribution is principally a quantitative evaluation framework and empirical characterization, rather than the general idea of activation transfer (§§2, 5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Substantial but incomplete overall transfer | Original 35-block plain-format cohort, other-relation sources, half-depth injection: Qwen 0.5B removes 45.2% of no-change error, reducing mean absolute contrast error from 2.869 to 1.572 nats. | §3.2; Table 7 discussion |
| Overall fidelity does not ensure selectivity | Original plain-format Llama 1B removes about 49% of overall no-change error, while selective-error reduction is −11.4%, with a descriptive 95% interval of [−42.7, 9.9]%. | §§1, 4.1; Table 24 |
| Encoding materially changes Gemma results | With BOS restored, Gemma 2B and 9B remove 58–69% of no-change error across the three cohorts; every comparison against direct reuse, zero prediction and different-replacement hidden reuse passes within its specified family. | §4.1; Tables 21, 23 |
| Replication has failures | On the second fresh plain-format cohort, none of the three small checkpoints passes against zero prediction; Qwen 7B and Llama 8B pass all three baseline comparisons. | Table 21 |
| Matched-source route advantage remains unconfirmed | With target-relation sources, pooled post hoc hidden-over-direct advantages are 0.24–0.90 nats in plain format; all six pre-specified matched-route tests fail on the second fresh cohort. | §4.1; Tables 18, 22 |
| Identical content can produce question-dependent selective effects | Over 99 blocks in default encoding, identical same-replacement neutral entity-row changes beat random and other-replacement controls on all six checkpoints within specified families; Llama 1B results fail pooled correction. | §4.1; Appendix G, Table 34 |
| Aligned matrices outperform tested single vectors | Best vector selected from 90 constructions on the original cohort, evaluated on 64 already-studied fresh-cohort blocks: span alignment wins on five of six checkpoints under family correction, four under pooled correction. | §4.3; Tables 3, 31 |
| Conditional scoring gives limited evidence about generation | One question-plus-cue capture with other-relation span alignment exactly reproduces the edited greedy answer string for 5–23% of 396 targets, generating up to 16 tokens. | §4.1; Appendix E, Table 29 |

The evidence supports partial portability of measured answer-score changes and receiving-question dependence. It does not establish improved factual accuracy, reliable free generation, or an identified hidden-state mechanism. Generation and candidate scoring differ in capture and construction, so their gap cannot isolate autoregressive error accumulation (§7; Appendix E).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ scope limits:** English, equal-length entity substitutions, six instruct checkpoints, and fresh entities mostly within familiar relation/template families. Relation semantics and wording vary together (§7).
- **Authors’ evaluation limits:** Known-prefix scoring uses literal answer groups with little absolute probability mass and omits equivalent aliases. Confirmatory interventions use one depth; scans are descriptive (§7).
- **Authors’ comparison limits:** Construction comparisons jointly vary direction, coverage and prefix information; norm-matched vectors borrow aligned-response norms. Source-relation matching changes both response content and read-out (§7; Appendix D).
- **Authors’ statistical cautions:** Protocols were internal, later designs reused findings and materials, and recurring labels may induce dependence. Pooling all 223 tests changes 26 conclusions; failed tests do not establish equivalence (§7).
- **Analyst inference:** Strong format sensitivity and conditional-score gains warrant caution when extrapolating these findings to practical steering or factual editing; those uses are not validated here.

## Open research questions
1. **Does transfer generalize to unseen relation and wording families?** Fresh cohorts largely reuse both. Next experiment: hold out complete relation/template families before constructing new-entity blocks (§7).
2. **Which format components drive selectivity?** BOS alone does not reproduce Llama 1B’s chat selectivity gain. Next experiment: factorially vary BOS, instruction, template and terminator on fresh cohorts (§7).
3. **How much does aligned row structure contribute independently?** Current constructions change several factors together. Next experiment: match coverage, prefix access and row norms while varying row-specific directions (§§5, 7).
4. **Can conditional fidelity predict generation fidelity?** Existing evaluations differ in capture and construction. Next experiment: hold those fixed while comparing known-prefix scoring with free continuation (Appendix E).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly defines the methods and qualifications, but many tables contain captions without numerical bodies. Full per-checkpoint estimates, intervals and test inventories are therefore unavailable. Code, identifiers, saved predictions and protocols are described as planned releases; their availability is not established (Appendix B.3).
