# Adversarial Speculative Reasoning Improves Long Horizon Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-04.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.05345) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05345)


## One-sentence takeaway
Training speculative drafts to invite rejection at selected reasoning decisions improves reported long-horizon coding performance, although the proposed explanation through more precise cached reasoning remains only partially established.

## Problem and motivation
Speculative decoding usually trains a small draft to maximize agreement with a larger target. The authors argue that this overlooks numerical precision: in their serving configurations, later verification slots are noisier, and cached differences can affect predictions many agent turns later (§3).

Their identified gap is whether draft rejection can allocate higher precision to consequential decisions while retaining ordinary acceleration elsewhere, without modifying target weights or the rejection-sampling rule (§§1, 4).

## Approach and contributions
- **Precision measurements:** Repeated forward passes on identical inputs measure noise by verification slot; a separate experiment follows identical token sequences written through verification or single-token decoding (§3).
- **Adversarial draft training:** The target labels alternative continuations sharing a decision-point prefix. Reverse-direction DPO teaches the draft to favor undesirable decision framings or skipping tests, increasing opportunities for rejection around preferred reasoning (§§4.1–4.3).
- **Deployment and evaluation:** Only selected draft parameters are trained. The authors compare adversarial, original, forward-preference, and unrelated-preference drafts on coding agents (§§4.4–5).

The intended mechanism is that rejection moves a replacement token to the head of a fresh verification block, improving its KV-cache encoding. Greater downstream persistence of preferred decisions is explicitly a hypothesis (§4.2).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Noise increases within verification blocks | Repeated identical-prefix forwards: mean log-probability deviation increases by 0.0195 nats/slot for GLM, 95% CI [0.0174, 0.0214], and 0.0094 for DeepSeek, [0.0078, 0.0110]; approximately 4% of each model’s initial-slot noise per slot. | §3.3, Fig. 1 |
| Long-range extreme deviations show a tentative excess | GLM teacher-forced cache experiment: verification/decode ratio for deviations above 4 nats is 1.16 in turns 51–70, CI [0.98, 1.36], returning to 0.99 in turns 71–90. | §§3.2–3.3, Fig. 2 |
| GLM improves on DeepSWE | Across five independent runs per configuration on 113 tasks, all-hidden-tests success rises from 60.6% to 67.6%; +7.0 percentage points, 95% CI [2.6, 11.4]. | §5.2, Fig. 5 |
| DeepSeek’s DeepSWE gain is uncertain | Same evaluation protocol: task success rises from 67.8% to 71.8%; reported gain +4.0 points, 95% CI [−1.3, 7.3]. | §5.2, Fig. 5 |
| DeepSeek improves on repository generation | Across five runs on 104 NL2Repo repositories, mean test pass rate rises from 65.0% to 66.4%; +1.4 points, 95% CI [0.4, 2.4], p=0.011. | §5.2, Fig. 5 |
| Preference direction matters | On DeepSWE, forward-preference drafts score 55.7% versus GLM’s 60.6% baseline, and 66.4% versus DeepSeek’s 67.8% baseline. | §5.2, Fig. 6 |
| Unrelated training does not reproduce the gain | DeepSeek’s 27 runs with other preferences average 67.5% DeepSWE success versus 67.8% baseline; Welch p=0.83. | §5.2, Fig. 7 |
| Estimated acceptance cost is small | Teacher-forced top-1 agreement under a greedy-chain approximation gives accepted draft tokens/block of 2.28→2.28 for GLM and 2.50→2.46 for DeepSeek; the latter is reported as a 1.8% difference. | §5.3 |

These results support an effect of draft training in the tested configurations, with statistically clearer gains for GLM on DeepSWE and DeepSeek on NL2Repo. Direction and unrelated-preference controls strengthen the interpretation, but do not directly demonstrate that particular decisions persist because of better KV precision.

The authors retain the standard exact-verification rule. **Analyst interpretation:** that establishes the stated sampling identity for a fixed target distribution; it does not itself demonstrate identical full-trajectory distributions when numerical computation and cached states vary—the phenomenon the paper investigates (§§3–4).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated uncertainty:** Long-range extreme-tail effects vary between independent builds; several reported confidence intervals include no excess (§3.3).
- **Analyst inference—mechanism:** No reported intervention directly tracks whether a preferred decision’s rejection, cache precision, and later reuse jointly explain task success. The persistence explanation remains hypothetical (§4.2).
- **Analyst inference—scope:** Evaluation covers two coding-agent systems. Preferences are target-specific, and decision-frame training does not improve DeepSeek despite learning that preference (§5.2).
- **Analyst inference—efficiency:** The speed claim relies on teacher-forced agreement and an acceptance approximation; measured wall-clock throughput or end-to-end task cost is not supplied (§5.3).
- **Reporting ambiguities:** DeepSeek’s deployed draft has held-out preference accuracy 0.70, while 0.85 describes the stronger preference-learning result. Fork/sample counts do not transparently reconcile, and DeepSWE gains are inconsistently rounded (§§4.3, 5.1–5.2).
- No dedicated limitations section or explicit release statement is supplied.

## Open research questions
1. **Does precision cause decision persistence?** The paper hypothesizes this link. Force identical decision tokens into different verification slots, then measure later decision reuse and task outcomes.
2. **Does the benefit survive different precision settings?** Current evidence comes from selected serving configurations. Repeat the intervention while varying weight precision, KV precision, and block width independently.
3. **Is selective rejection better than generic rejection?** Existing controls change preference content or direction. Compare against random rejections matched for frequency and accepted-block length.
4. **What is the actual operational cost?** Acceptance estimates leave runtime effects unresolved. Measure throughput, task latency, and total compute under matched serving loads.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the reported comparisons, but establishes the proposed causal mechanism less firmly.

Material missing information includes measured runtime costs, direct decision-level mediation evidence, training/evaluation benchmark-overlap details, judge-label validation, complete serving and harness configurations, and availability of code, training pairs, and trained drafts.
