# QFabric: Guarded Cross-Domain Task Recovery and Its Limits on a Linux-RTOS Board

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-04.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.01598) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01598)


## One-sentence takeaway
On the tested Arduino UNO Q, QFabric’s guarded recovery avoids harmful Linux-to-MCU migrations because MCU calls retain Linux dispatch interference while adding substantial transport and compute costs (§VII).

## Problem and motivation
A Linux–RTOS board appears to offer a recovery option: move a task to the real-time MCU when Linux timing deteriorates. However, transport overhead, shared dispatch dependencies, and task semantics can make that move slower or invalid. The authors investigate whether migration actually helps on a physical board and how a runtime should authorize, verify, and reverse placement changes (§I).

## Approach and contributions
- **Guarded recovery protocol:** Dual-domain task declarations use a bounded wire interface and effect-based eligibility. Empirical timing violations trigger analysis only after consecutive violating windows; admission checks precede a quiescent routing change, followed by probation and commit or rollback (§IV).
- **Matched-load characterization:** The authors measure Bridge transport and five kernels in both domains under idle, CPU-load, and higher-priority real-time contention, separating executor compute from residual latency (§§VI–VII-B).
- **Controller evaluation:** Ordered trace replay compares placement policies on a four-node chain; a hysteresis ablation exposes harmful moves, while separate hardware scenarios demonstrate switching and rollback mechanics (§§VII-C–D).
- **Correction of earlier evidence:** The paper withdraws its earlier recovery improvement after identifying per-call Linux process creation and mismatched load traces (§VI-F).

The authors claim novelty for the combined guarded, post-switch-verified protocol across a physical Linux–RTOS boundary, rather than for profiling, placement, feedback control, or timing contracts individually (§III-E).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Transport cost increases with payload. | Idle sequential echo under schedutil: p95 rises from 8.85 ms at zero bytes to 34.04 ms at 128 bytes; corresponding rates are 127.2 and 30.2 calls/s. Values aggregate three repetitions. | §VII-A; Fig. 3 |
| MCU execution is substantially slower at idle. | Ordinary-caller addition p95: 18.81 ms on MCU versus 0.075 ms on Linux, reported as a 251× gap. Idle median residual remains near 19 ms across workloads; FIR×512 MCU compute is reported as 34× slower. | §VII-B; Tables IV–V |
| MCU placement does not escape tested Linux interference. | Under RT contention, MCU-path p99 reaches 400 ms versus 17.0 ms for Linux. The narrative does not identify the exact workload/caller cell producing these maxima. | §VII-B |
| Guarded recovery makes no moves in chain replay. | Across 30 cells, each with 500 four-node-chain observations, QFabric makes zero switches. Worst ordinary-Linux p95 is 26.7 ms against graph deadlines of at least 96 ms; worst miss rate across Linux policies is 0.8%. | §VII-C; Table VI |
| Removing hysteresis causes harmful moves. | With k=1 rather than k=3 under RT contention, the controller switches in seven cells and raises p95 by up to 177×. Probation rolls back five moves; two remain unresolved when traces end. | §VII-D; Table VII |
| Decision overhead is small relative to MCU dispatch. | Across 1,000 in-process evaluations, decision p95 is 170.1 μs CPU time and 177.2 μs wall time; observed tracemalloc peak is 3,138 bytes, excluding interpreter baseline. | §VII-E |
| Earlier positive recovery evidence is superseded. | The earlier 36.6% replayed-chain p95 reduction disappears after replacing per-call Linux processes with a persistent executor and matching Linux/MCU load conditions. | §VI-F |

These results support a negative placement finding for the measured kernels and stock board stack, plus evidence that hysteresis prevents damaging reactions to isolated stalls. Hardware scenarios establish protocol mechanics, whereas chain performance comes from replay. They do not establish beneficial recovery in a complete deployed application, hard real-time guarantees, or a universal limit across heterogeneous architectures.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope:** One board, one software stack, and five small kernels limit generalization. Ordered replay cannot capture concurrent task interactions or replace a full application experiment (§VIII-D).
- **Timing validity:** Percentile contracts are empirical; the utilization admission gate is not a schedulability proof. Residual latency is calculated as a difference of medians rather than measured component by component (§VIII-D).
- **Comparisons and safety:** SCHED_DEADLINE, CPU isolation, and affinity remain unevaluated alternatives. Synthetic RT contention covers one interference pattern. Effect annotations and quiescent switching do not prove implementation equivalence; production concerns include state transfer and fault containment (§VIII-D).
- **Analyst inference:** Because deadlines deliberately accommodate the slower domain at idle, zero migration demonstrates correct behavior under those contracts, rather than recovery from demanding Linux-specific deadlines (§IV-B).
- **Analyst inference:** The supplied quantitative ablation isolates hysteresis; it does not separately quantify admission and probation through comparable ablations. Hardware rejection scenarios establish mechanics (§VII-D).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Hardware verification is described as occurring after four windows, while the main protocol specifies six probation windows; the supplied text does not reconcile these settings (§§IV-C, VII-D).

## Open research questions
- **Can removing Linux dispatch dependence make recovery beneficial?** The current MCU path retains that dependence. Compare matched-load recovery using stock Bridge, lower-latency transport, and dispatch outside Linux userspace (§VIII-A).
- **Do MCU-resident chains change the placement trade-off?** Replay charges each task visit its Linux-issued invocation path. Run a complete timer-triggered MCU chain that exchanges only inputs and final results (§VIII-A).
- **How do contracts and guards behave during sustained degradation?** Standard replay produces no guarded switches, and two ablated moves remain in probation at trace end. Test longer traces with controlled regime changes and varied contract, hysteresis, and probation settings (§§VII-C–D).
- **How competitive is recovery against stronger Linux configuration?** The authors identify untested Linux baselines. Compare affinity, CPU isolation, and SCHED_DEADLINE under the same workloads and interference (§VIII-D).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied narrative clearly supports the central measured finding, but most referenced tables contain captions without their data.

Missing information includes complete per-cell results and confidence intervals, full baseline definitions, detailed testbed configuration, configured admission headroom and cooldown, hardware-scenario settings, and quantitative isolated ablations of guards beyond hysteresis. Artifact contents and reproducibility are reported by the authors but were not independently checked.
