# RoboColosseum: Evaluating Physical Intelligence at Scale In-The-Wild

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-04.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.02497) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02497)


## One-sentence takeaway
RoboColosseum combines distributed physical trials with controlled adaptation tests, finding that robot-policy rankings depend strongly on task and embodiment and that competitive relative performance can coexist with poor task completion.

## Problem and motivation
Physical robot evaluation must balance reproducible comparisons with realistic variation in scenes, hardware, and tasks. The authors argue that small studies, costly resets, and inconsistent setups make performance estimates fragile, while existing distributed benchmarks offer limited embodiment coverage. RoboColosseum addresses this gap through shared infrastructure, dedicated operators, and complementary evaluation protocols (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Distributed evaluation infrastructure:** Standardized robot interfaces, policy serving, hardware configurations, and operating protocols across SO-101, DROID/Franka, Bimanual-YAM, and Unitree G1 (§3.1).
- **Complementary tracks:** The Open Track uses blinded, locally matched comparisons without task-specific adaptation. The Fine-tune Track supplies identical task demonstrations and predefined success/progress criteria, while allowing model-specific training recipes (§3.2).
- **Ranking and diagnostic framework:** Difficulty-Calibrated Rasch (DCR) ranking jointly estimates policy ability and task difficulty using success outcomes and human difficulty priors. Progress scoring exposes incomplete execution, while uncertainty-driven matchmaking allocates trials toward informative comparisons (§3.2).
- **Evaluation assets and findings:** The paper supplies task cards, training configurations, and agent interfaces, and states that task demonstrations are publicly released. Its contribution is primarily benchmark infrastructure and empirical evaluation rather than a new robot policy (§3.2.1; Appendices A–D).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Evaluation spans many physical settings | Open Track: 5,699 pairwise comparisons across 5,699 tasks and 124 sites. Fine-tune Track: five VLA policies, 20 tasks, 100 demonstrations per task, and 25 trial slots per policy–task pair. | §4.1; Table 2 |
| Frontier-agent progress is competitive but heterogeneous | Across 901 matched agent–VLA comparisons, agents make more progress in 394, VLAs in 265, with 242 ties; mean agent-minus-VLA normalized progress is +5.3 percentage points. Individual agent means range from −7.5 to +12.1 points. | §4.2; Fig. 4 |
| Harder tasks sharply reduce performance | Success falls from 30.7% for human difficulty ratings 1–4 to 0.4% for ratings 9–10. **Among unsuccessful executions**, mean normalized progress falls from 56.6% to 3.5%. | Fig. 6 |
| Fine-tuning leaders differ by embodiment | Equal-task average SR/PS: GR00T N1.7 leads SO-101 at 82.4%/89.0%; π0.5 leads DROID/Franka at 84.0%/91.2% and YAM at 93.6%/97.2%. Safety-stopped trial slots count as zero. | Table 2 |
| Binary success conceals humanoid differences | All five policies have 0% success across the five G1 tasks, while equal-task average PS ranges from 13.3% to 23.2%. | Table 2 |
| Rankings transfer poorly across evaluation settings | Open-versus-fine-tuned rank correlations for five VLAs are −0.20, −0.40, and 0.20 on SO-101, DROID, and YAM respectively. Fine-tuned PS rankings across four embodiment-specific suites have Kendall’s W = 0.24. | §4.4; Figs. 9–10 |
| Site-level reproducibility varies | Disjoint site subsets yield approximately 85% mean pairwise ranking agreement for DROID and YAM, versus 59% for SO-101; corresponding mean Spearman correlations are 0.82 and 0.30. | §4.5; Fig. 10b |
| Active sampling improves agreement efficiency | Approximately 90% agreement with the full-data DCR ranking requires 400–500 comparisons under active matchmaking versus roughly 1,000 under uniform sampling; curves summarize 200 runs. | §4.5; Fig. 11a |

These results support setting-dependent policy strengths and the diagnostic value of partial progress. They do not isolate morphology, architecture, or adaptation ability as causes: task distributions, checkpoints, training recipes, and metrics differ. Ranking agreement measures consistency with the collected-data leaderboard, not an independently established ground truth.

One reported conclusion is internally inconsistent: §4.2 says no system exceeds 13% success in any category, but Fig. 5c reports frontier-agent cells above that threshold, including 25% for Claude on deformable tasks, with a wide interval. The universal claim is therefore unsupported as written.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated coverage limits:** Model participation depends on hardware/checkpoint compatibility, and fine-tuning covers only five tasks per embodiment (§5).
- **Author-stated comparison confounds:** Fixed demonstration data do not imply equal optimization or compute. Cross-track differences also include starting checkpoints, task distributions, and ranking metrics (§§4.4–5).
- **Author-stated reference limitation:** Stability analyses compare against the complete collected dataset rather than an external capability standard (§5).
- **Analyst inference—human scoring:** Difficulty ratings and progress judgments influence evaluation, but the supplied text reports no inter-rater agreement or difficulty-prior sensitivity analysis (§3.2).
- **Analyst inference—control interface:** Agents act more slowly than VLAs because of API round trips. Their comparison measures complete deployed systems, including interface constraints (Appendix D.1).
- **Analyst inference—protocol ambiguity:** Table 1 specifies three DROID cameras, while Appendix D.1 describes two agent views. Appendix A also exempts SO-101 GR00T from the main text’s full-fine-tuning description.
- **Analyst inference—progress validity:** Equal criterion weighting need not represent equal functional achievement. Some task cards are ambiguous: G1 `hang_keys` says walking is unnecessary but includes walking among progress criteria (Appendix B.4).

## Open research questions
1. **What causes ranking reversals?** Tasks, checkpoints, metrics, and adaptation vary together (§4.4). Next experiment: evaluate shared feasible tasks with harmonized starting checkpoints and report both success and progress before and after adaptation.
2. **How robust is DCR to human difficulty judgments?** Ratings supply priors, but sensitivity results are absent (§3.2.2). Next experiment: compare rankings with independent raters, perturbed ratings, and no rating prior.
3. **Does active matchmaking generalize to new evaluation coverage?** Efficiency is measured against the existing full-data ranking (§5). Next experiment: prospectively compare active and uniform allocation on previously unused sites and tasks.
4. **Does partial progress predict useful task completion?** G1 scores differ despite uniformly zero success (Table 2). Next experiment: validate criteria with independent raters and test whether criterion-specific training improves held-out completion.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the principal findings, but reporting inconsistencies limit stronger conclusions. Material missing information includes exact DCR inference settings and prior sensitivity, task-order randomization and reset verification, inter-rater reliability, complete training durations/compute and pinned commit identifiers, detailed active-sampling replay procedures, and numerical DCR-versus-baseline robustness results. Public asset availability is stated by the authors but was not independently verified.
