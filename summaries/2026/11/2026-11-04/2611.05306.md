# You don&#x27;t need long context

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-04.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.05306) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05306)


## One-sentence takeaway
On the tested workloads, selective document reading and frequent state compaction largely preserve frontier-model accuracy with short live contexts, although some open-weight models suffer substantial losses (§§3–5; Appendix D).

## Problem and motivation
Long contexts increase decoding cost, latency, and memory use. The authors question whether long interaction histories and large source documents actually require keeping all available information in the model’s active context, rather than maintaining compact state and retrieving evidence when needed (§1).

The identified gap is empirical: how aggressively can context be reduced across realistic agent workflows and long-input benchmarks without sacrificing task success?

## Approach and contributions
- **Context-management comparison:** Compare full-history accumulation (FHA) with threshold-triggered end-of-conversation compaction (EoCC). Compaction preserves task requirements, relevant facts, progress, verification, and next steps rather than merely summarizing history (§2.4.1; Appendices A, G).
- **Selective input reading:** Store long documents in the filesystem and let agents search and read bounded excerpts through Bash. This changes long-input tasks into sequential retrieval workflows (§2.4.2; Appendix E).
- **Single-action compaction (SAC):** Reset the conversation after each environment action, carrying forward a natural-language semantic state; evaluate this on software-engineering and terminal tasks (§5).
- **Systems measurements and model concept:** Measure context-dependent decoding efficiency and propose Semantic State Space Models trained to maintain semantic state. SSSMs are a proposal, not a trained model demonstrated here (§2.5; §5.1).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Shorter contexts improve measured decode throughput | On identical H100 allocations, reducing configured context from 256 K to 16 K increases peak sustained decode throughput by 3.6× for GLM 5.2, 5.6× for Kimi K2.6, and 7× for Qwen 3 Coder Next; measurements use cached prefixes and concurrency sweeps. | §2.5; Figure 4 |
| Closed-weight long-horizon accuracy usually remains near baseline | Across six benchmarks and 22 EoCC configurations per model, changes versus FHA range from −3.7 to +5.6 percentage points for Opus and −3.3 to +4.4 for GPT; each model loses more than two points in at most five configurations. | §3; Figure 5 |
| Compaction can improve issue resolution | On SWE-bench Verified, Opus resolves 74.8% at 24 K/18 K versus 70.0% with FHA; GPT resolves 88.8% at 48 K/36 K versus 84.4%. Settings denote total limit/compaction threshold. | Table 3 |
| Retrieval accuracy can decline with compaction | On FRAMES at 24 K/18 K, Opus accuracy falls from 81.0% to 77.3%, and GPT from 90.2% to 86.9%. | Table 6 |
| Realistic long-input results are favorable but mixed | At 16 K/12 K, LongBench v2 accuracy changes from 68.0% to 67.6% for Opus and 70.8% to 72.6% for GPT; AA-LCR changes from 65.0% to 84.0% and 82.0% to 83.0%, respectively, versus full-input baselines. | Tables 11, 13 |
| Selective reading strongly improves a synthetic retrieval suite | On RULER, evaluated at 256 K and 500 K input lengths, mean recall rises from 62.5% to 95.7% for Opus and 65.5% to 96.4% for GPT with a 16 K/12 K agent setting. Median compactions are zero. | §2.2; Table 15 |
| SAC supports useful execution with very short live contexts | SWE-bench Verified accuracy: Opus 70.0% FHA → 70.2% SAC; GPT 84.4% → 82.4%. Terminal-Bench: Opus 47.2% → 51.8%; GPT 87.6% → 84.3%. SAC median peak contexts are 7–9 K tokens across these settings. | Table 2 |
| Robustness does not generalize uniformly across models | Quantized Qwen 3 Coder Next resolves 53.0% of SWE-bench Verified with FHA but only 3.6% at 16 K/12 K EoCC. Authors attribute this mainly to verbose trajectories leaving insufficient progress between compactions. | Appendix D; Table 9 |
| Large reported savings rely on estimated pricing | Estimated optimized cost savings span 2.2–8.8× for Opus and 2.4–5.0× for GPT on long-horizon workloads; long-input ranges are 6.5–54.6× and 4.4–233.3×. These use an extrapolated context-dependent pricing model. | §§3–4; Appendix B |

The evidence supports short-context execution as an effective alternative on these benchmarks, especially with strong workers and searchable external inputs. It does not establish universal equivalence to long-context execution or demonstrate the reported optimized savings as actual API bills. For example, GPT’s nominal SWE-bench Verified cost rises from $167.68 with FHA to $203.12 at 16 K/12 K, despite a lower estimated optimized cost (Table 3).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Compaction loses information, and repeated compaction can compound that loss. Some tasks require access to earlier records; selective history retrieval is proposed as a remedy (§3.1).
- **Author-stated:** The findings neither exclude tasks benefiting from long context nor prove every task solvable with short context (§6).
- **Observed boundary:** Tight compaction degrades several open-weight models substantially, limiting generalization from the principal closed-weight results (Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference:** Long-input comparisons change both input access and execution procedure. They establish the effectiveness of selective-reading agents, but do not isolate compaction’s contribution; several benchmarks require no compactions (Appendix F).
- **Analyst inference:** Optimized cost estimates transfer a rounded throughput ratio from open-weight deployments to closed-weight models and interpolate hypothetical prices. They do not establish end-to-end latency or actual provider savings (Appendix B).
- **Reporting gap:** Accuracy confidence intervals, repeated task-run variability, and controlled quantitative comparisons of compaction designs are not supplied. Appendix A describes exploratory findings qualitatively.
- **Unvalidated proposal:** SSSM performance and training-data efficiency remain conjectures (§5.1).

## Open research questions
- **What causes model-specific compaction failure?** Appendix D links one collapse to verbosity without isolating its cause. A next experiment could vary response verbosity and compaction budget independently on the same tasks.
- **When does selective reading cease to suffice?** The authors suggest tested tasks often need relatively little relevant evidence (§4). A next experiment could increase the fraction of source material required for an answer while holding document length fixed.
- **Can SSSM training improve state preservation?** No trained SSSM is evaluated (§5.1). A next experiment could compare a state-update-trained model with its original checkpoint under matched SAC budgets.
- **Do practical deployments realize the estimated savings?** The cost model extrapolates beyond measured deployments (Appendix B). A next experiment could measure task accuracy, wall-clock latency, and total serving cost on the same models and hardware, including retrieval and compaction overhead.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported benchmark patterns; **medium** in general claims about long-context necessity and economic benefits.

Material missing information includes task-level uncertainty and repeated runs, complete executable configurations and code-release status, controlled compaction-design ablations, measured end-to-end workload latency and serving cost, and experimental validation of SSSM training.
