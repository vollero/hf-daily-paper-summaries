# How Feedback-Free Self-Evolution Reshapes Agent Skills: A Systematic Study

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-23.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.25534) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25534)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across three benchmarks, repeated feedback-free rewriting degraded expert skills and preferentially removed content models rarely generated from the topic alone, while SkillDiff highlighted that content in the original skills and raised mean SkillsBench pass rate by 4.8 percentage points.

## Problem and motivation

Agents use expert-written skills to carry out procedures that a model may struggle to supply on its own. These documents need updates as tools, environments, and tasks change, often without reliable evaluators or task outcomes to guide the edits. The authors identify a gap in understanding whether repeated rewriting under those conditions improves model fit or erodes useful expert knowledge (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Measure degradation.** Six models recursively rewrite expert skill documents for 30 rounds using the current text and a one-sentence topic, with no task, outcome, or judgment guiding the edits. The same model then uses its rewritten skill (§2).
- **Diagnose selective loss.** For each skill and model, the authors sample ten topic-only drafts as a proxy for the model’s prior. They track document similarity and use an LLM judge to measure whether each original item survives in meaning (§3.1–3.2).
- **Test the effect of lost content.** The authors restore different groups of lost items to a common base built from the round-30 rewrite. A separate experiment deletes comparable amounts of each content type from the original skill (§3.3; Appendix B.6).
- **Introduce SkillDiff.** The method marks eligible items absent from all ten drafts and adds a note asking the agent to carry them into its plan. The original wording is preserved (§4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Rewriting degraded multi-step agent performance | Relative to original skills, mean SkillsBench pass rate fell 7.1 percentage points after one rewrite, 10.9 after five, and 14.6 after 30. All six agents declined at round 30, by 10.3–23.0 points. | §2.2, Fig. 1, Table 4 |
| Rewriting erased most of the original skill’s advantage in single-call evaluation | At round 30, mean LogicBench accuracy fell from 86.2% to 71.4%, matching the no-skill level. MedCalc-Bench fell from 89.7% to 78.7%, compared with 81.1% without a skill. | §2.2, Table 4 |
| Rewrites moved toward topic-only drafts and selectively lost original content | Across six models and three benchmarks, mean normalized TF-IDF cosine distance to the drafts moved from 1.37 for originals to 1.19 at round five and 1.22 at round 30. At round 30, mean retention was 23.1% for items absent from all ten drafts and 84.2% for items present in all ten. | §3.1–3.2, Figs. 2–3, Table 1 |
| Restoring rarely generated items recovered more performance | On SkillsBench, relative to round-30 skills, mean pass rate rose 8.8 points when restoring items absent from all ten drafts, 4.4 when restoring the other lost items, and 10.2 when restoring all lost items. The first group also recovered more on average than the second on both single-call benchmarks. | §3.3, Fig. 4, Table 2 |
| Similar amounts of original text had different effects when deleted | Across two SkillsBench models, deleting comparable amounts of text changed mean pass rate by −4.6 points for items absent from all ten drafts and +0.6 points for other items, relative to same-batch originals. | Appendix B.6, Table 17 |
| SkillDiff improved on the original skills | Adding the tags and note increased mean SkillsBench pass rate by 4.8 points against same-batch originals, with gains for all six agents. | §4.2, Table 3 |

The authors attribute much of the decline to the loss of rarely generated content. Restoration and deletion support a causal contribution in the tested settings. Whole-document similarity changed little after round five while pass rates continued to fall, limiting its value as a performance proxy. SkillDiff establishes a gain for the combined marking-and-note intervention, with the contribution of each component unresolved.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Each SkillsBench task uses one preselected skill, and rewrites affect only the document. Multi-skill retrieval and composition and joint updates to documents, scripts, and references remain untested (Appendix E).
- **Analyst inference.** Absence from ten drafts measures what a model tends to generate under a particular prompt and setup. The expanded sampling check covers GPT-5.6-Luna on SkillsBench, and the alternate-judge checks use a SkillsBench subset. Codex rewriters could also use web search in the main setup, with a search-disabled comparison reported for one model on MedCalc-Bench (§3.2; Appendices A.3–A.4, B.2, C.1).
- **Analyst inference.** Main evaluations run once per task and condition. Repeated SkillsBench evaluations support the rewrite and restoration findings for two models. SkillDiff is tested only on SkillsBench against unmarked originals, leaving the effect of generic emphasis and the note alone unresolved (§4.2; Appendices B.3–B.4).

## Open research questions

1. **Can protected rewriting preserve expert details while still making useful edits?** The paper tests restoration after rewriting and highlighting in the original. Compare ordinary rewrites, rewrites that lock items absent from all drafts, and unchanged originals on tasks requiring both updated and preserved procedures (Appendix D).
2. **Does SkillDiff benefit from which items it marks?** The reported comparison bundles targeted tags and an instruction note. Compare targeted tags with count-matched random tags and a note-only condition in the same evaluation batch (§4.2).
3. **How much task feedback is needed to prevent selective loss?** The study isolates rewriting without task feedback. Vary feedback frequency and measure both item retention and performance on held-out tasks (§2.1; Appendix D).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported direction of degradation and selective loss in the tested settings. The supplied text includes captions without many table cells, leaving exact per-agent SkillDiff gains and exact restoration scores for the two single-call benchmarks unavailable. It describes the rewrite and judge instructions without providing their full wording, and the abstract links a repository without specifying its contents.
