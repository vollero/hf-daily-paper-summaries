# AeroRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement for Aerial Vision-Language Navigation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-23.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.24077) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24077)


## One-sentence takeaway

On the simulated TravelUAV test set, three rounds of training an aerial navigation policy on repairs verified by its own flight continuations raised success from 46.4% to 67.5% and roughly halved the collision rate, without additional human labels or inference cost (§§3.1, 4.2, 5).

## Problem and motivation

A UAV following a natural-language instruction can drift into states absent from its training demonstrations, where a single bad action can end the flight. The authors identify a gap in action-level supervision for those states. Human corrections are expensive to collect, rule-based planners can ignore the instruction, and episode-level rewards give limited guidance about which action to change. Whether a correction works also depends on how the policy flies afterward, and early failures can hide later ones (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Refresh failure states each round.** Adaptive Failure Anchoring samples saved simulator states at exponentially spaced offsets before failures of the current policy. This targets both immediate mistakes and earlier decisions, and lets newly exposed failures enter the next training round (§3.3, Figure 2).
- **Verify repairs with the current policy.** Continuation-Verified Repair replays the original action and evaluates policy samples and rule-based proposals from the same restored state. The current policy flies on after each candidate. The procedure checks that the original action fails again or scores sufficiently worse, then requires the selected repair to qualify on a second flight (§3.4, Algorithm S1).
- **Learn repairs while retaining successful behavior.** Retentive Repair Learning imitates verified repairs and the current policy’s actions at states from successful flights. It also replays repairs from the previous round. The authors find that earlier repairs usually remain viable, failure locations shift, and actions resembling rule-based repairs become more common among later policy samples (§§3.5, 4.3, Figure 4, Appendix C).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Success improved over successive rounds | Overall TravelUAV test success rate (SR) rose from 46.4% at supervised initialization to 57.6%, 66.0%, and 67.5%. The collision rate roughly halved from initialization to round 3. The final 1.46-point SR gain fell below the authors’ paired significance threshold. | §4.2, Table 3, Figure 3, Table S4, §5 |
| AeroRSI outperformed AeroDPO from the same initialization and improved the AeroDPO checkpoint | After three rounds from the shared supervised checkpoint, AeroRSI led AeroDPO by 10.6 percentage points in overall test SR. AeroRSI alone succeeded on 649 routes, and AeroDPO alone succeeded on 330. Starting from AeroDPO instead raised its overall test SR by 13.0 points to 69.9%. | §4.2, Table 2, Table S4 |
| Gains varied by test split | From supervised initialization, round 3 exceeded AeroDPO in SR by 15.3 points on Seen and 15.7 on Unseen Object. On Unseen Map it improved on supervised initialization by 9.4 points, matched AeroDPO on SR, and had lower success weighted by path length (SPL). | §4.2, Table 2 |
| Longer continuations improved repair selection | In first-round ablations over all test routes, verifying rule-based proposals improved SR by 12.37 points over using them unverified. Scoring candidates after two actions increased collision rate by 4.23 points relative to full verification. | §4.3, Table 4(a), Appendix B.2, Table S4 |
| Fresh repairs helped more than reusing first-round supervision | On all test routes, round 2 reached 66.0% SR, compared with 60.3% when retraining on first-round repairs and 63.0% when collecting twice as many anchors from the initial policy and matching the two-round update count. | §4.3, Table 4(b), Table S4 |
| Most of the gain persisted under an altitude cap | On the same 999 test routes, the round-3 SR gain over supervised initialization was 13.21 points with a cap of 15 m above route start and 16.22 points without the cap. | Figure 4(f), Appendix C.4 |

The shared-checkpoint comparison and paired route results support a TravelUAV post-training benefit. The ablations support longer continuations and renewed repair collection in this setup. The separate contribution of the reproduction and confirmation checks remains unclear because removing both together yielded a similar first-round SR (§4.3, Table S4). Evidence for transfer is limited to TravelUAV test splits.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported evaluation variability.** Repeat evaluations of the same policy changed outcomes on about a quarter of routes and overall SR by up to about two percentage points. The authors treat the round-3 increment from supervised initialization as within this variability (§§4.1–4.2, Table S4).
- **Author-reported failure and efficiency trade-offs.** On the supervised-initialization test runs, timeouts rose from 1.2% to 6.8% of routes between rounds 0 and 3. Climbs and detours lengthened some routes, and SPL improved less than SR (§4.2, Figure 3).
- **Analyst inference — generalization is bounded by the setup.** Both initializations share the same architecture and use coarse target direction at inference. Training depends on exact simulator resets and privileged depth and target positions. The study leaves performance on other architectures, without direction guidance, and on physical UAVs unresolved (§§3.1, 4.1–4.2).
- **Analyst inference — verification trades coverage for reliability.** A repair only has to qualify over a finite horizon or land successfully. In the extended simulator re-flights from accepted first-round anchors, the initial policy reached the target in 57.2% of episodes. The reported cost is about 100 GPU-hours per round, so the unchanged inference cost covers only deployment (§3.4, Appendices A.5, C.3).

## Open research questions

1. **How well do the repairs transfer to physical UAVs?** All evaluations use TravelUAV simulation. A next experiment could compare the supervised and repaired policies on matched, controlled physical routes while measuring success, collisions, and timeouts.
2. **Can repair selection reduce timeouts while preserving collision gains?** Timeouts rose as collisions fell, and some repairs lengthened paths. A next experiment could penalize elapsed actions in the continuation score and compare SR, SPL, collision rate, and timeouts over repeated simulator runs.
3. **Which verification checks earn their rollout cost?** The reported ablation removes reproduction and confirmation together, leaving their individual effects unresolved. A next experiment could vary the checks independently at matched rollout budgets and measure repair repeatability and downstream SR.

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the summary of the method and headline TravelUAV results. The supplied text provides the key comparisons in prose and paired statistics in Table S4. Several other tables appear only as captions, leaving some per-split metric values, exact per-round collision rates, and the complete hyperparameter grid unavailable. The paper links project code, while the supplied text leaves the availability of AeroRSI checkpoints and collected repair data unclear.
