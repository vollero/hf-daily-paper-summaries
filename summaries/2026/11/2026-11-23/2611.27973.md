# MemOnce: A Scalable Memory Architecture for Large Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-23.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.27973) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27973)


## One-sentence takeaway
MemOnce retrieves compressed history once per query and reconstructs layer-specific attention caches on demand, improving Qwen3.5-4B’s average accuracy by 5.77 percentage points across five memory evaluations while cutting prefill from 286.86 s to 41.36 s at 1M tokens (Tables 3 and 14).

## Problem and motivation
Continual language models need to reuse past interactions without repeatedly processing the full history. Full-attention Transformers retain fine-grained context but incur growing GPU KV caches and quadratic prefill compute. Fixed-size recurrent states scale more cheaply but can lose details needed for later queries. The authors seek an architecture that preserves recall while keeping the active historical cache bounded and prefill time linear in context length (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Compressed memory with layer-specific decoding.** MemOnce stores the trailing fraction of each chunk’s final-layer hidden states in a single expandable bank. It keeps recent chunks on GPU, offloads older states, and reconstructs distinct KV caches for each attention layer on demand (§3.1, Fig. 2, App. B.1).
- **One retrieval per input.** A learned cosine-similarity index selects older chunks once per query. The selected chunks and the recent window supply history to every attention layer and subsequent decoding step (§3.2, App. B.1).
- **Two training paths and a supervision corpus.** The authors pretrain MemOnce from scratch and adapt frozen Qwen3.5 backbones using memory-specific LoRA, trainable indexing and KV projections, and contrastive evidence supervision. They construct MemOnce-1M, a million-example dialogue corpus with teacher-generated answers and evidence-turn labels. They also construct HotpotQA♣-1M and evaluate the learned index for zero-shot text retrieval and reranking (§§3.3, 4.2, Apps. B–D).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Competitive pretraining from scratch | On FineWeb-Edu-10B, at the roughly 1B tier, MemOnce validation perplexity was 9.65 with no temporal compression and 9.83 at 8× compression, versus 9.78 for the Transformer. Average zero-shot accuracy across eight tasks was 55.15%, 55.13%, and 55.12%, respectively. | Fig. 3, Table 11 |
| Higher average memory accuracy | Across five settings spanning about 4K–1M tokens, MemOnce-4B averaged 60.07% versus 54.30% for full-context Qwen3.5-4B and 52.87% for top-8 text RAG. At 2B, MemOnce scored 51.17% versus 49.33% for full context. MemOnce-4B scored 67.01% versus full context’s 69.55% on LoCoMo, and 47.40% versus RAG’s 52.40% on LongMemEval. | Tables 3 and 12, App. D.3 |
| Open-ended recall at million-token scale | On HotpotQA♣-1M, with an approximately 1.35M-token evidence bank, MemOnce-4B scored 59.40% with memory and 19.60% without it, a 39.80-point gain. RAG-4B scored 52.20% with memory and full-context Qwen3.5-4B scored 26.60%. | App. F.3, Table 13 |

MemOnce also used a 640.0 MB offloaded pool. At 32K, prefill took 1.38 vs. 0.87 s. | Fig. 4, Table 14 |
| General reasoning scores held under memory load | Under a 32K memory load, MemOnce-4B averaged 65.98% across MMLU, GSM8K, and GPQA, versus 65.88% for full-context Qwen3.5-4B. Its MMLU and GSM8K scores were slightly lower and its GPQA score was higher. | Table 4 |

The results support a better average recall–efficiency trade-off in the tested settings, with exceptions by benchmark and context length. The bounded cache figure covers selected historical states. The persistent bank grows with history, and the separately described GPU-resident index reaches 4.0 MB at 1M tokens (App. B.1). The measurements support approximately linear prefill over the tested range. Each query still scores the growing index, so cumulative retrieval cost depends on how often queries arrive.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated.** Scratch pretraining stopped at about 1B parameters. Long-horizon interactive coding and multi-step tool use remain unevaluated (§5.2).
- **Analyst inference.** The five-setting memory average combines exact multiple-choice accuracy with model-judged open-ended accuracy. LoCoMo excludes its adversarial unanswerable category, limiting what its score says about abstention (Apps. D.2–D.3).
- **Analyst inference.** The compression ablation uses downstream accuracy, and the routing-budget ablation varies how many chunks are retrieved. These tests leave open how much of the recall gain comes from learned compression versus evidence selection, and how well a fixed budget handles queries with widely dispersed evidence (Tables 8–9, App. G.2).
- **Analyst inference.** Efficiency ratios depend on the tested hardware, offload path, and baseline implementations. Table 14 uses multi-GPU evaluation for baselines that exceed single-GPU memory, and Table 10 measures SSD cold reads for off-pool retrieval.

## Open research questions
1. **Does the pretraining benefit persist at larger scales?** Scratch results stop near 1B. Train MemOnce and full attention at a matched larger scale with the same token budget, then compare perplexity, downstream accuracy, and long-context recall.
2. **When does fixed-budget routing miss required evidence?** Table 9 varies the retrieval budget without isolating the number or dispersion of supporting chunks. Vary both in a controlled multi-hop benchmark and compare learned routing with oracle evidence selection.
3. **How does memory cost accumulate in query-heavy sessions?** The paper reports prefill scaling and a retrieval breakdown at a fixed context length. Replay sustained sessions with different query frequencies and measure cumulative latency, GPU index growth, and storage traffic.

## Confidence and missing information
**High** confidence in the architecture description and tabled point estimates for the reported settings.
