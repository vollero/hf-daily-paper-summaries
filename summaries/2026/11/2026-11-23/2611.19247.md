# GameWM: Toward Next-Generation Video Games with Interaction-Centric World Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-23.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.19247) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19247)


## One-sentence takeaway

GameWM lets a video model generate different animations and effects for the same engine-resolved event, with held-out clips demonstrating the approach and smaller causal models showing timing or staging errors (§§4, 7).

## Problem and motivation

The authors argue that action-conditioned video world models struggle to maintain precise control and persistent state, while proxy-conditioned systems generally restyle motions an engine has already authored. GameWM targets the gap between those designs by generating detailed character–object and character–character interactions while the engine retains authority over rules, state, and outcomes (§§1, 3–4).

## Approach and contributions

- **Engine contract and authoring interface.** The engine supplies a coarse proxy video and event log. The video model uses the proxy, an opening frame, an optional character sheet, and timestamped skill text to generate articulated motion and effects for the event (§4, Figure 3).
- **Interaction-centric training pipeline.** The pipeline mines event-centered clips from engine recordings and processes screen recordings using estimated depth and semantics. It pairs proxies with source footage and multiple stylized targets, with vision-language screening of generated renders and evidence checks on captions (§5, Appendix A).
- **Shared conditioning across model tiers.** GameWM adapts H3 and Wan2.2 backbones to a common reference interface for packed depth and semantics (DUV), depth, semantic maps, or low-poly proxies. It applies an existing distillation pipeline with proxy conditioning on both teacher and student to produce four-step causal variants (§6, Table 2, §7.1).

The demonstrations support the feasibility of adding interaction detail to coarse proxies while visually following event cues in the examples shown. Engine state is authoritative by design. The experiments leave overall timing reliability, long-session visual consistency, end-to-end playable latency, and performance relative to external systems unmeasured (§§4.2, 7, Appendix B).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported.** The evaluation is mainly visual and uses recording-derived proxies. The authors omit external performance comparisons because proxy formats, prompt alignment, and weight availability differ (§7, Appendix B).
- **Author-reported.** Causal outputs can weaken effects, smaller students can mistime or restage events, and identity similarity degrades under side views and occlusion. The reported 33B setup decodes the full clip after rollout, so the first-chunk timing measures latent generation (§7.3, Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference.** The single-clip backbone comparison cannot isolate parameter count from differences in backbone, adaptation, and clip configuration. Figure 11 also displays the three seeds per model that best followed the proxy (§6.2, Table 2, Figure 11).
- **Analyst inference.** The reported ablations leave the separate effects of event-centric sampling, multiple targets per proxy, and caption verification unresolved. Appendix A says captions flagged as contradictory remain in training (§5, Appendices A and C.1).

## Open research questions

1. **How reliably do generated events follow the engine clock during live play?** The reported tests use short, pre-recorded proxies. Run a live engine integration and measure input-to-display latency, visual event-frame error, and missed events across repeated interactions and scene revisits (§7, Appendix B).
2. **When does a character sheet improve identity beyond the opening frame?** The identity test uses opening frames made from the sheet. Repeat the paired-seed sheet ablation with characters absent from the opening frame and measure identity after entry and reappearance (§7.2, Table 4).
3. **Which training choices improve adherence to the same event across visual variants?** The reported ablations leave data curation and target multiplicity unresolved. Train matched models while varying event-centered sampling and the number of targets per proxy, then compare timing error and requested-skill fidelity on held-out events (§5, Appendix C.1).

## Confidence and missing information

**Medium.** The design and reported measurements are clear, while broad reliability rests largely on visual examples. The supplied text gives no total held-out set size, aggregate event-timing or completion rates, live input-to-display measurements for GameWM, or explicit release status for model weights, the training corpus, and evaluation clips.
