# Seeing the Invisible: How Vision-Language Models Read, Predict and Trace Radio Fields

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-23.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.28123) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28123)


## One-sentence takeaway

On RadioVISTA, leading vision-language models ranked urban radio coverage much more accurately than they judged whether a direct or single-reflection path existed, and answer-only fine-tuning reproduced that gap in Qwen3.5-9B.

## Problem and motivation

Urban radio coverage is shaped by buildings, terrain, and materials, while the signal itself is invisible in scene imagery. The authors ask whether vision-language models can read a reference field, predict how coverage changes under a physical intervention, and infer individual propagation paths from geometry. They identify a gap between visual reasoning benchmarks centered on visible geometry or small-scale physics and radio datasets that lack the matched interventions and language queries needed to test these abilities together. Urban wireless digital twins motivate the work (§§1–3, Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Benchmark and training data.** RadioVISTA comprises twelve closed-choice tasks spanning spatial recognition, channel reasoning, and regional coverage ranking. The reported release covers 100 cities across six continents, with test cities held out from training (§§3–4, Fig. 2).
- **Automated simulation and labeling.** A coordinate-driven engine builds watertight urban meshes from open geospatial data and extends Sionna RT to generate matched fields across three environments and four propagation modes. It derives labels from mesh geometry or simulated fields and filters out cases near decision boundaries (§4.2, Fig. 1).
- **Diagnostic evaluation and post-training.** The authors compare models against a reference-copying baseline, separate cases where the winning region changes, examine path predictions by class, and test whether answer-only supervised and reinforcement fine-tuning close the gap (§§5–7).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Leading models scored higher on coverage than on paths | On 1,200 zero-shot questions from 17 held-out cities, GPT-6-Astra and GPT-6.1-Sol reached 94.9% and 93.4% physics-aggregation accuracy, respectively, versus 76.5% and 61.8% on binary G4. Uniform guessing scores 25.0% on aggregation and 50.0% on G4. | §§5.1–5.2, 6.2, Table 2 |
| Hidden-target scores were dominated by stable winners | The winner changed on 11 of 306 F2–F4 questions. Copying the visible reference winner scored 96.4% overall and got none of the 11 changes right. Astra and Sol each scored 94.8% overall and got 4 and 6 of the 11 changes right, respectively. | §6.1, Table 3 |
| Open baselines struggled with field tasks and retries | The best open model reached 47.4% overall zero-shot accuracy versus 27.4% for uniform guessing. Open models averaged 28.2% on kept-winner items versus 25.0% chance. With four samples at temperature 0.7, the best open Pass@4 was 68.3% versus 70.7% for four uniform guesses. | §§5.2, 6.1, 7.1, Tables 2–4 |
| Fine-tuning improved aggregation while G4 predictions collapsed to one label | On 17 held-out cities, Qwen3.5-9B went from 27.9% aggregation accuracy to 88.7% after SFT and 89.7% after SFT plus GRPO, versus Astra’s 94.9%. Both tuned variants predicted No on every G4 item, scoring 52.9%, equal to the negative-class prevalence. | §7.2, Table 2, Figs. 4 and 7 |

The results establish a performance gap between coverage questions and path reachability in this benchmark. The reference-copying baseline shows that high aggregate F2–F4 accuracy provides weak evidence of intervention tracking. Correct answers on changed-winner items show some ability to identify the new winner, but the small subset leaves the magnitude and reliability of that ability uncertain. The experiments also leave the models’ internal reasoning process unresolved.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ findings and scope.** Interventions rarely changed the winning region, most models strongly favored one G4 label, and neither tested post-training recipe produced class-sensitive path predictions. The benchmark filters geometrically borderline paths and close coverage contests (§§4.2, 6–7).
- **Analyst inference.** Exact labels are defined by the simulator and its scene data. The reported free-space comparison with Friis law leaves agreement with measured urban fields unverified (§4.2).
- **Analyst inference.** The training evidence comes from one base model and two sequential answer-only stages. It leaves open whether the result generalizes to other models or changes with intermediate geometric supervision (§§7.2, 8).
- **Analyst inference.** G4 pools direct and single-reflection reachability into a binary task. The reported class breakdown cannot show which path type drives the errors (§6.2, Fig. 4).

## Open research questions

1. **Can models predict coverage shifts when the reference winner is unreliable?** Only 11 test cases changed winners. Generate a larger held-out set balanced between kept and changed winners, then compare models with reference copying by intervention type (§6.1).
2. **Does intermediate geometric supervision improve path tracing?** The training experiments used final answers, although the engine computes reflection points. Compare matched training runs with answer-only labels versus reflection-point and path-clearance supervision, and report held-out performance separately for direct and reflected paths (§§4.2, 7.2, 8).
3. **Do the coverage results transfer to measured urban fields?** The reported solver check is in free space. Pair simulated scenes with measured coverage from the same transmitter layouts and compare regional rankings and model accuracy under both label sources (§4.2).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported benchmark comparisons, which are supported by task-level tables and class breakdowns. The supplied text lacks measured urban validation, uncertainty intervals for the changed-winner results, full prompts and visual input specifications for all tasks, detailed training hyperparameters and compute, and a clear inventory of released code and model checkpoints.
