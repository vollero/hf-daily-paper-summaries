# AgentRoboCritic: Understanding the Capability of Frontier Agents on Direct Robot Control

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-23.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.26613) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26613)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across 12 manipulation benchmarks, AgentRoboCritic found that GPT-6 Astra recovered from 57.7% of analyzed failed actions by episode end, with substantially lower recovery for alignment and failed contact effects than for grasping (§§4.1–4.2, Appendix C).

## Problem and motivation

Episode success rates hide which robot actions fail, whether the policy corrects them, and which physical errors persist. The authors argue that a global task instruction alone also leaves individual actions ambiguous because several valid trajectories can accomplish the same task (§§1, 3.2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Intent-grounded critique.** The policy states an immediate subgoal alongside each numerical control command. The critic uses the task goal, stated intent, and synchronized multi-view frames before and after execution, with follow-up frames where needed, to label the outcome and explain any failure (§3.2, Fig. 2, Appendix B).
- **Action-level analysis.** The authors apply the critic to GPT-6 Astra rollouts across 12 manipulation benchmarks to quantify failure types, grasp failure stages, and verified recovery (§4, Appendix C).
- **Critic data and model.** The authors report releasing a dataset of 12,844 annotated actions, a 400-action human-labeled test set, and a LoRA-fine-tuned Muse-Glimmer-30B critic (§§1, 5–6, Appendix F).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Local failures can coexist with task success | Across the 12 benchmarks, 49.8% of successful episodes contained a local failure. Episode success was 40.3% with one or two local failures and 75.1% with none. | §4.1, Fig. 5a–b |
| Recovery is frequent and varies by failure type | Of 12,163 analyzed failed actions in officially graded GPT-6 Astra episodes, 38.9% were resolved within 10 later actions and 57.7% by episode end. End-of-episode recovery was 63.7% for Grasp, 35.7% for Alignment, and 29.6% for Effect. | Figs. 5c, 7a, Appendix C |
| Physical execution dominates the failure profile | Grasp was the leading failure mode on 9 of 10 non-tactile benchmarks. Alignment led on vision-only UniVTAC and OpenViTac. Choice accounted for 0.2% of failed actions across the suite and at most 3.3% on any benchmark. | §§4.1–4.2, Fig. 4 |
| Zero-shot success came with longer decisions | The authors report higher zero-shot episode success for Astra than π₀.₅ and InternW0-Δ in Fig. 6. On matched tasks from five Embodied First Exam suites, Astra led π₀.₅ on each suite. Across 74 matched tasks timed for both, the median of run-level median decision intervals was 31.9 s for Astra and 0.11 s for π₀.₅. | §4.1, Fig. 6, §§H.3–H.4 |
| Local intent improves critique | Astra had 79.25% outcome accuracy against the initial human labels on the 400-action test set. Withholding intent sharply reduced outcome and failure-mode accuracy. Agreement exceeded 90% on both metrics after annotators re-reviewed disagreements and credited accepted critiques. | §5, Table 1, Appendix I |
| Fine-tuning improves the open critic | Muse-Glimmer-30B failure-mode accuracy rose from 32.58% to 74.24% on the 264 human-labeled failures. Overall accuracy, which requires both applicable labels to be correct, rose from 34.75% to 67.75% on the 400-action test set. | §5, Table 1, Appendix F |

The trajectory evidence documents later repairs and workarounds, and the critic ablation shows that stated intent helps Astra judge these recorded actions. The association between recovery and task success is observational. Rare Choice labels measure adherence to the policy’s own stated intent, so they leave open how often the policy selected the wrong subgoal for the task (§4.1, Appendices A, C).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ findings.** Grasping and tight-tolerance alignment remain bottlenecks. The authors also document actions that continue after an object has already been lost (§§4.2–4.3).
- **Analyst inference on generalization.** The main action-level analysis uses one policy model, and RoboDojo contributes 9,923 of the 12,163 failures in the recovery analysis. Action units also vary across benchmarks, so a fixed number of later actions represents different amounts of control across suites (§4, Appendices B–C).
- **Analyst inference on annotation.** Astra serves as both policy and primary critic. The authors check for failed actions mislabeled as achieved, which addresses one form of self-preference. The higher agreement after re-review reflects adjudication informed by the critic’s answers. Assigning a single failure label can also obscure compound failures (§5, Appendix I).
- **Analyst inference on intervention.** Critic feedback helped on one matched DexJoCo retry. That case leaves its average effect across tasks and seeds uncertain (§H.1).

## Open research questions

- **Does tactile feedback improve alignment recovery?** Alignment dominated the reported vision-only insertion settings. Run matched UniVTAC and OpenViTac trials with vision alone and with tactile feedback, then compare Alignment frequency, recovery, and episode success (§4.2).
- **How often does an agent faithfully execute the wrong subgoal?** Choice labels measure execution against self-reported intent. Have independent annotators judge whether each stated subgoal advances the global task, then compare those judgments with execution labels (Appendix A).
- **Does critic feedback reliably improve retries?** The paper reports one matched feedback example. Randomize feedback across tasks and seeds while holding the policy and retry context fixed, then compare official success and decision latency (§§H.1, H.4).

## Confidence and missing information

**Medium.** The supplied prose gives the core protocol, recovery denominators, and several critic scores. It omits the full Table 1 values, including exact intent-ablation scores and absolute inference costs, the plotted zero-shot success rates in Figures 6 and 16, and the split counts and fine-tuning hyperparameters referenced in Tables 9 and 10.
