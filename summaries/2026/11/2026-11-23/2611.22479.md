# Exploited Before It Was Fixed? Vulnerability Timelines from Open, Machine-Readable Evidence

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-23.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.22479) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22479)


## One-sentence takeaway

In one Vulnerability-Lookup snapshot, after excluding catalog batch dates and unordered pairs, 59 vulnerabilities had recorded patch evidence a median of 7.6 days after recorded exploitation evidence, a gap that mostly reflects catalog listings and downstream fixes (§6.3, Table 7).

## Problem and motivation

Defenders need to know whether a fix was available when exploitation began. Earlier studies relied on different evidence sources and mainly reported aggregate intervals, while the Vulnerability Timeline and Exposure Metrics framework (VTEM) calls for lifecycle dates that vendors and coordinators seldom publish. The authors ask how much of that timeline can be reconstructed from machine-readable records and traced back to the evidence for each vulnerability (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Evidence model.** Selects the earliest eligible dated statement for each lifecycle event from records that explicitly identify the vulnerability. It distinguishes exploitation observations, catalog listings, and public proof-of-concept releases, and preserves the source, field, date precision, and resulting interval bounds and ordering (§3, Tables 1–2).
2. **Reference implementation.** Adds per-vulnerability evidence and intervals to the Vulnerability-Lookup interface and timeline API. Signed intervals and VTEM-inspired metrics use defined endpoints, and exploitation-to-patch intervals remain open when dated patch evidence is missing (§§3–4, Table 3).
3. **Snapshot measurement.** Measures event coverage and source composition, then summarizes ordered intervals by sign after excluding catalog batch dates (§§5–6.3).
4. **Evidence corrections.** Uses provenance to diagnose and correct mislabeled exploit publications, misclassified honeypot detections, placeholder listing dates, and substituted advisory copies (§6.5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Dated patch evidence was sparse | In the 3 November 2026 snapshot of 3,758 identifiers with exploitation evidence, 98% had dated disclosure and 590 (15.7%) had both exploitation and patch anchors. No identifier had a vendor-report or acknowledgement anchor. | Abstract, §5, §6.1, Table 5 |
| Catalog dates dominated exploitation evidence | Of 3,756 exploitation anchors, 2,401 were direct catalog listings and 875 were first-hand detections. Across all anchors, 1,041 fell on a listing or import batch day. | §6.2, Table 6, §7 |
| Recorded patch evidence usually came first among ordered pairs | Of 590 paired identifiers, 135 fell on batch days and 89 of the remaining 455 had unknown order. In the ordered set, the signed gap was negative for 307 (median −188.3 days, IQR −1544.4 to −28.4) and positive for 59 (median 7.6 days, IQR 3.8 to 22.4). Of those 59, 50 exploitation anchors were catalog listings and 42 patch anchors were Red Hat fixes. | §6.3, Table 7 |
| Correcting sighting labels materially changed the initial estimate | In the 28 October run, Exploit-DB publications accounted for 2,225 of 5,620 exploitation anchors (40%). Retyping them as proofs of concept changed the positive-gap median from 39.5 to 10.6 days in that comparison. | §6.5 |
| Removing channel-post pairs affected the two signs differently | Removing 81 channel-post-anchored pairs from the 455 non-batch pairs, without replacing their anchors, left 56 positive gaps with the same 7.6-day median. The negative median shifted from −188.3 to −127.7 days across 229 remaining negative pairs. | §7, Classification |

The results establish which recorded dates can be ordered and how sensitive the summaries are to evidence classification. The authors caution that both anchors can lag the events they represent. The signed gap therefore cannot establish whether exploitation began before an installable fix was available or bound the true exposure period (§§4, 7).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** The snapshot inherits the coverage and selection choices of its catalogs, sensors, and advisory providers. First-hand detections cover what those sensors saw, and the precision of the language-model labels on channel posts is unmeasured (§7).
- **Author-stated:** Upstream release dates are often missing. Dated fixes can represent downstream packages, while undated patch references and version boundaries require verification. Fix availability alone gives no deployment date (§7).
- **Author-stated:** Timestamp bounds capture date granularity only. Other placeholder formats and unrecognized advisory revisions can distort anchors, and records without explicit identity fields are excluded (§7).
- **Analyst inference:** The ordered, non-batch pairs form a source-dependent subset of the snapshot. Given sparse patch coverage and its concentration among a few publishers, the reported medians have limited applicability to the full catalog population (§§6.1–6.3, 7).

## Open research questions

- **How accurately do channel-post labels identify exploitation?** Precision on posts that supply anchors remains unmeasured. Have reviewers independently label a stratified sample of posts, then recompute intervals using the adjudicated labels (§7).
- **How much do downstream package dates shift patch anchors?** Upstream release dates are often absent, and the surveyed version candidates are unverified. For a sample with a recorded downstream fix, verify the first installable upstream release and recompute the paired gaps (§7, §8).
- **Does structured publisher reporting improve coverage across instances?** This study measures one instance on one date. Repeat measurements across instances and dates, tracking coverage of vendor notification, acknowledgement, first observation, and fix availability as publishers adopt typed events (§5, §§7–8).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the paper’s reported snapshot counts, interval summaries, and documented corrections. Material information unavailable in the supplied text includes independently verified dates for first exploitation and first installable fixes, measured channel-post classifier precision, and replication across instances. The supplied Markdown also contains table captions without their full tabular contents, and the paper states that the source instance is not redistributed.
