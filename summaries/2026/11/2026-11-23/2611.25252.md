# Moving Generality from Model to Modeler: LLM Agents for PHM on a Deployment-Style Benchmark

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-23.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.25252) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25252)


## One-sentence takeaway

On NextUnit-Bench, frontier coding agents outscored the tested AutoML systems on held-out equipment and operating conditions, and swapping data-informed design plans shifted frontier agents’ scores toward the plan author’s while reducing the tested local agent’s delivery rate for the strongest agent’s plans (§§IV–VI).

## Problem and motivation

Prognostics and health management (PHM) predictors must work on equipment they have never seen. The authors identify a gap between published evaluations, whose results can depend on protocol choices such as capping remaining-life labels, and deployment, where a delivered predictor must run on a new unit. They ask whether a general-purpose coding agent can build a better predictor for each setting from labeled data on comparable machines (§§I–II).

## Approach and contributions

1. **NextUnit-Bench.** The authors introduce an open benchmark with 24 public datasets and 58 folds that hold out physical units or, where independent units are unavailable, whole operating conditions. Agents have two hours to deliver an executable predictor, which is sealed and run offline on hidden units (§III, Table I, Fig. 1).
2. **Deployment-style comparisons.** The study compares agent-built predictors with AutoML, fine-tuned time-series foundation models, and reimplementations of published methods. Selected published methods were also rerun under their original protocols to assess how evaluation choices affect reported performance (§IV).
3. **Plan-transfer experiments.** The authors extract code-free specifications from completed runs and swap them between Astra and Luna. They compare these with plans written without access to data values, simplified plans, and toolkit interventions, then test whether a locally deployable agent can execute the transferred designs (§§V–VI, Appendix A).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Frontier agents led the aggregate comparison | Across 24 datasets, normalized score \(S\) ranged from 30.8 for Luna to 43.0 for Astra. Both AutoML systems reached 18.7 with generic signal features, with MIMII DUE unrun and scored zero. MOMENT and Chronos-2 scored 12.5 and 11.2 after per-fold fine-tuning with a fixed pipeline. | §§IV-A, IV-C, Fig. 2 |
| Published-method comparisons varied by dataset and protocol | Under deployment-style evaluation, 6 of 30 reimplementations beat Astra’s own design on the same fold. Astra led the published-method average on three of four datasets and tied on Tennessee Eastman. The best published methods beat it on Tennessee Eastman and N-CMAPSS. On C-MAPSS FD004, three methods achieved 13.7–18.7 cycles RMSE with capped remaining life. Under the uncapped protocol, all four tested methods had 31–37 cycles RMSE on the same engines. | §IV-B, Fig. 3, Table IV |
| Data-informed plans transferred performance in both directions | Across 24 datasets in OpenCode, Luna rose from \(S=31.0\) to 36.8 with Astra’s plans, while Astra fell from 44.2 to 31.5 with Luna’s. Holding the implementer fixed, Astra-authored plans led Luna-authored plans by about 1.5 points on the first runs when written without data values, versus 5.8–12.7 points for data-informed designs. | §§V-A–C, Fig. 4, Table III |
| Simpler designs retained much of Astra’s score | Across 24 datasets, Astra scored 42.6 executing its simplified plans versus 44.2 in independent OpenCode runs. Toolkit interventions changed \(S\) by −0.7 points on average across 15 agent–toolkit combinations, each evaluated on its applicable datasets. | §§V-D–E, Fig. 5, Table VI |
| Astra’s plans reduced Qwen’s delivery rate | Within two hours, Qwen delivered predictors on 32% of runs with Astra’s plans versus 57% without a plan in the delivery comparison. Its benchmark-wide \(S\) was 9.3 with Astra’s plans versus a four-run no-plan mean of 12.6. On folds where it delivered in both conditions, scores differed by about one point. | §§VI-A–B, Fig. 6 |

The plan swaps support the authors’ interpretation that choices made using development data explain a substantial share of the score gap between these two frontier agents. Here \(S\) measures error reduction relative to a fixed random-forest and CNN reference, with failures and results at or below the reference scoring zero. The benchmark measures performance on its held-out splits. Live plant performance, cold starts without labels, and superiority to human engineers remain untested (§III-C, §§VII-A, VII-C–D).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported.** Most configurations ran once, and bootstrap intervals reflect variation across datasets rather than across repeated runs. Per-dataset scores can vary substantially. The detailed local plan-transfer analysis centers on Qwen and a fixed two-hour budget (§§IV-A, VI, VII-D).
- **Author-reported.** Agents recognized public datasets, leaving pretraining exposure unresolved. Internet restrictions were enforced by instructions, and the authors’ scan found one lookup of a hidden-test duration that lowered the agent’s score. AHU, Petrobras 3W, and CARE contained collection or preparation shortcuts. Excluding these datasets preserved the direction of the reported comparisons (§§VII-C–D).
- **Author-reported.** A single LLM judged implementation fidelity, and the original Paderborn splits were unavailable. Astra summarized both agents’ completed designs, and the frontier plan swaps used two models from the same developer. The main frontier and local comparisons also used different harnesses (§IV-B, §§V-A, VII-D).
- **Analyst inference.** The original-protocol reproduction attempts also received a longer time budget, which limits attribution of their overall reproduction rate to protocol alone. Likewise, Qwen’s similar scores on jointly completed folds leave predictor quality on the timed-out folds unknown (§IV-B, §VI-B).

## Open research questions

- **Do plans transfer across frontier model developers?** The frontier swaps used Astra and Luna. A follow-up could cross authors and implementers from several developers in a common harness and repeat each condition (§§V-A, VII-D).
- **Can advice written without data values improve on-site delivery?** The paper tests those plans with frontier implementers and gives Qwen plans distilled from completed runs. A follow-up could compare metadata-only advice with a no-plan control on a private rig, measuring both delivery and prediction error (§§V-C, VI-A, VII-E).
- **Does customization help when a new machine type has no labels?** The benchmark supplies labeled units of the same type. A follow-up could compare designs adapted from related machines against independently generated designs on an unpublished rig whose labels are reserved for evaluation (§§VII-D–E).

## Confidence and missing information

**Medium.** The supplied text reports the protocol and principal aggregate results, but omits the numerical bodies of Tables II, III, IV, and VII, limiting verification of detailed comparisons. Exact provider model snapshots were unrecorded, the original Paderborn splits were unavailable, and most configurations lack repeated runs for estimating run-to-run uncertainty (§IV-B, §VII-D, Appendix B).
