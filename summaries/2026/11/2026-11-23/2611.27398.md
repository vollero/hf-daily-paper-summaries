# Taking Apart and Putting Together: Tracking Objects Through Physical Transformations

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-23.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.27398) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27398)


## One-sentence takeaway

TIPO uses per-frame mask geometry and selective multimodal verification to track physical splits and merges, achieving higher structural state-graph F1 than TubeletGraph on both full and disassembly-only VISTA with either tested tracker.

## Problem and motivation

Transformation Tracking and Understanding (TTU) asks a system to segment prompted objects through assembly and disassembly, assign new identities when their physical composition changes, and record the participating objects and actions in a labeled state graph. The authors identify two gaps: standard trackers preserve identities across physical changes, and TubeletGraph relies on track loss to discover disassemblies and cannot represent assemblies. A graph of what is attached or separate could support robotic step verification and augmented-reality assembly guidance (§§1, 3.1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Task formulation.** TTU represents object states as masks and names in a directed graph. Edges connect adjacent frames, preserve identity during ordinary motion, and link old identities to new ones at splits and merges (§3.1, Appendix A).
- **TIPO.** This training-free pipeline extracts connected components from tracked masks and ensembles single-point mask-decoder queries. It proposes partitions of components for splits and of overlapping tracked objects for merges. Inner recall rewards agreement within a group, while external recall penalizes overlap across groups. A multimodal judge checks the top proposals that pass the threshold, and a separate labeler names the new objects and actions. Accepted changes update the tracker and graph (§3.2, Fig. 2, Appendix B).
- **VISTA and evaluation.** VISTA pairs dense masks with annotated input-to-output state graphs. The paper introduces structural \(T_{F1}\) and holistic \(H_{F1}\), which adds semantic correctness to structural matching (§3.3, Appendix C).

The disassembly-only comparison shows that TIPO’s VISTA advantage persists when both methods are evaluated on events TubeletGraph supports. VISTA’s structural metric checks event timing, input and output counts, and the masks of participating objects. The tracking IoU pools masks per frame and therefore cannot detect identity swaps that preserve their union. VOST-TAS lacks the input-to-output annotations needed to replicate the structural comparison. The authors attribute the VOST-TAS reversal with SAM 2.1 to the scoring protocol and TubeletGraph’s track-loss trigger. The reported results leave the relative contribution of those explanations unresolved (Appendices C.3, E.2–E.3).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Missed small parts and segmentation artifacts can produce missing or spurious graph events. Visually ambiguous parts can receive generic names. The streaming graph cannot revise past events, although later predictions can recover a scene state consistent with the current frames (Fig. 17, Appendices D.5 and F).
- **Analyst inference.** Generalization to dense or non-rigid interactions remains uncertain. VISTA centers on rigid objects, its annotated events have at most two assembly inputs or three disassembly outputs, and the non-rigid demonstration is a single qualitative example with spurious events (Appendix B.1, Fig. 15, Appendix F).
- **Analyst inference.** The headline judge comparison measures a configuration trade-off because it changes the proposal threshold alongside judge usage. Semantic scores also depend on an LLM evaluator whose human agreement was measured on labels from structurally matched VISTA events (Appendices B.3 and C.3).
- **Analyst inference.** The FPS comparison is specific to the reported setup. Deployment throughput may vary with the number of interacting components and the frequency of multimodal queries (Appendices B.4 and D.3).

## Open research questions

1. **Can targeted re-detection recover small parts after a split?** Missed screws and wheels remain visible failure cases. Test a post-split re-detection pass and measure last-frame object recall, false positives, and structural F1 (Fig. 17, Appendix D.5).
2. **How much does future evidence improve the graph?** The streaming system cannot retract a committed error. Compare it with a bounded-lookahead variant and measure event precision, recall, timing error, and added latency (Appendix F).
3. **How well does the method handle deformable objects and higher-arity events?** The current benchmark offers limited evidence for either regime. Annotate input-to-output graphs for both and report structural F1 and runtime by object type and event arity (Appendix B.1, Fig. 15, Appendix F).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the task, method, and benchmark comparisons reported in the supplied text. The supplied text leaves public access and licensing for the TIPO implementation and VISTA assets unconfirmed. It also leaves independent full-graph validation on another dataset, aggregate non-rigid or high-arity results, and end-to-end throughput on other hardware unreported.
