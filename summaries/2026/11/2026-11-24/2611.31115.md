# RUIBench: What Makes a Good Robot-Use Interface?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.31115) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31115)


## One-sentence takeaway

On RUIBench, changing only the robot-use interface moved Claude Code’s simulated task success from 45.3% to 80.7%, while the relative advantage of program execution reversed on the five tasks tested on a physical robot (§§5.2, 5.4, Tables 3 and 5).

## Problem and motivation

A robot-use interface determines which actions a model can command, which decisions it delegates to the robot, and when it receives feedback. Requiring a model reply for every movement can slow execution and introduce errors, while delayed feedback can let physical errors accumulate. The authors identify a gap between embodied benchmarks that hold interfaces fixed and interface studies that compare selected designs without systematically crossing action abstraction with execution form (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **RUIBench** provides 30 manipulation tasks across five suites, with held-out missions, sensor-based observations, and an environment that continues to evolve during model reasoning. Independent scoring compares physical outcomes with agents’ completion claims (§3, Table 2).
- **RUIKit** crosses six action abstractions (Joint, Delta, Step, Pose, Point, and Skill) with per-action and Python-program execution, then adds a free-choice interface for 13 configurations. For a fixed agent and task, the harness, perception, control, and scoring stay fixed (§4, Fig. 3).
- **Controlled experiments** compare interface choices across two frontier agents and a zero-shot or interface-tuned 4B model, test multi-action batching with and without in-program feedback, and examine physical transfer and completion judgment (§5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Interface choice substantially changed simulated success | Across 30 tasks and five held-out missions per task, Claude Code averaged 45.3% with per-action Step and 80.7% with Program-Delta. The two frontier agents’ averages across all interfaces differed by 2.1 percentage points. | §5.2, Table 3 |
| Program execution benefited the frontier agents unevenly | Across the six fixed abstractions in simulation, Claude Code’s mean success rose from 64.0% to 75.0%, median replies fell from 30 to 12, and mean API cost fell from $1.40 to $0.77 per episode. Codex’s mean success rose from 70.1% to 72.8%. | §5.2, Table 3 |
| Batching and in-program feedback each improved success in the tested ablation | For Claude Code on Delta, Step, and Point in simulation, using one test seed per task, success rose from 57.8% with per-action execution to 65.6% with sequences lacking intermediate observations and 75.6% with programs allowing observations and branching. Median replies were 29, 16.5, and 14, respectively. | §5.3, Table 4, App. C.2 |
| Fine-tuning improved Qwen3.5-4B, with substantial variation by interface | Across 12 fixed interfaces in simulation, mean success rose from 1.9% zero-shot to 27.3% after interface-specific fine-tuning. Fine-tuned per-action Pose reached 46.7%. | §§5.1–5.2, Table 3, App. C.1 |
| The simulation ranking reversed for Claude Code on hardware | On five matched tasks, programs led per-action execution by 23.3 percentage points on one simulation seed. On the Franka, Claude Code’s program and per-action success rates were 36.0% and 42.0%, respectively, in a study totaling 650 physical episodes across both agents. | §5.4, Table 5, App. C.3 |
| Completion claims often disagreed with physical outcomes | In the one-seed simulation diagnostic across 30 tasks, 82.1% of Claude Code’s failed episodes and 70.9% of Codex’s ended in a completion claim. On the 23-task subset selected for final-frame judgeability, a fresh judge detected 70.2% and 80.9% of their false claims, respectively. Automatic retries in the grasp-and-place check experiment reduced completion claims among failed episodes without improving task success. | §5.5, Tables 4 and 6, App. C.4 |

The matched comparisons support an interface effect within the tested harness. The authors attribute part of the program benefit to batching and intermediate feedback. The ablation varies observation access and conditional execution together, so their separate effects remain unresolved. The physical trials show that interface rankings can change under the tested conditions, and the offline judge shows that existing images can reveal many failures on the selected tasks. Whether an online verifier would improve task success remains open.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** The physical study covers five tasks on one arm. Time-critical and contact-rich tasks remain difficult with the available feedback and control, and action-level checks leave task-level verification unresolved (§§5.4–5.5, 6).
- **Analyst inference.** Physical uncertainty is a plausible contributor to the ranking shift, but its effect is entangled with differences in objects, timing, and scoring. The matched simulation comparison also uses one seed (§5.4, Apps. A and C.3).
- **Analyst inference.** The offline judge’s detection rates apply to tasks selected for visual judgeability. Outcomes that depend on events earlier in the episode may require trajectory evidence (App. C.4).
- **Analyst inference.** The interaction-form ablation uses one frontier agent, three abstractions, and one seed per task. The fine-tuning study uses one small model and a separate adapter and trajectory set for each interface, leaving transfer across interfaces untested (§§5.1–5.3, App. C.1).

## Open research questions

1. **Which physical conditions change the preferred interface?** The hardware study varies several conditions together. Repeat matched layouts across multiple arms while independently varying calibration error and response delay, then compare per-action and program success (§5.4, App. C.3).
2. **How much of the program gain comes from observation access versus conditional replanning?** The existing ablation enables both together. Compare fixed sequences, sequences that can observe but cannot branch, and programs that can do both across several agents and seeds (§5.3, App. B).
3. **Can online task-level verification improve physical success?** The fresh judge was offline, and action retries did not raise task success. Compare a final-frame verifier with a trajectory-aware verifier that can request another observation or correction before completion, then measure physical success and false claims (§5.5, App. C.4).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the benchmark design and results explicitly reported in the supplied text. Material gaps include the unreadable table bodies for several comparisons, including Tables 4–6, episode-level logs and uncertainty intervals for the headline differences, and release or licensing details for the benchmark code, training trajectories, and adapters.
