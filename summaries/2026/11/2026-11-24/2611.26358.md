# Beam Model Technical Report

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.26358) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26358)


## One-sentence takeaway

Beam is an open-weight sparse language model for coding, reasoning, and tool use whose agentic expert reached 41% DeepSWE pass@1 at the final reinforcement-learning checkpoint, up from 35% at the 79%-of-run checkpoint at the same requested reasoning effort (§1, Fig. 16).

## Problem and motivation

The authors aim to make long-horizon coding and tool use practical in an open-weight model with lower inference compute. They identify a gap between foundational knowledge and executing multi-step tasks with tools. The training recipe also addresses two concrete obstacles: generic web filters discard much of the technical material the authors want to retain, and widely varying rollout durations make synchronized reinforcement learning costly (§1, §§2.2.1, 4.1.2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Architecture and pretraining.** Beam uses a decoder-only sparse mixture-of-experts Transformer with 501.3B total and 23.4B active parameters. The authors propose depth-scaled residual connections and adapt expert balancing with a cosine-decayed bias update. Their scaling ladder uses data-limited subsampling to preserve source repetition rates, while split-shuffle packing disperses long-document chunks across batches (§§2.1.1, 2.1.3, 2.2.5).
- **Long-context midtraining.** The authors build a two-stage recipe around verifiable tasks and grounded source material. Stage 1 uses a 32K training context. Stage 2 trains at 512K and mixes organic long documents, synthetic tasks, and earlier data to balance long-context gains against regressions elsewhere (§§3, 3.5–3.6, Fig. 14).
- **Post-training and serving.** Separate agentic and alignment experts receive supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning, then merge through multi-teacher on-policy distillation. The agentic run used fully asynchronous rollouts and generated over 100M rollouts in four weeks on 10.5K GB300 GPUs. The report also describes integrating FP8/NVFP4 quantization and DFlash 2 speculative decoding for serving (Abstract, §§1, 4–4.1.2).
- **Safety evaluation.** The authors developed Iris Safety Evaluations to measure responses to harmful and harmless requests across conversational and agentic settings (§1).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Pretraining scaling tracked predictions | Across four orders of magnitude in training compute, Beam Base matched predicted validation loss and was Pareto-optimal among the open base models shown, using bits per byte on decontaminated in-house code and web sets. | §2.1.3, Fig. 3 |
| Web quality ranking improved compute efficiency | The in-house classifier achieved 1.35× ladder-average compute efficiency on general web validation relative to DCLM fastText, with the same English-web upsampling schedule. | §2.2.1, Fig. 6 |
| DeepSWE improved during RL | At requested effort `e=512`, pass@1 increased from 35% at 79% of the run to 41% at the final checkpoint. Harder software-engineering tasks entered training at 83%. The figure also reports better performance at comparable output lengths versus the supervised checkpoint. | §4.1.2, Fig. 16 |
| BrowseComp improved before search-specific RL | Held-out pass@1 went from 50.1 to 57.9 between 27% and 83% of the run, before search or browsing tasks entered RL. Measurement began after a search-backend change. Settings were temperature 1, top-p 0.99, effort 512, and a 256K output limit. | §4.1.2, Fig. 17 |
| Iris measured refusal and compliance | Beam refused or pivoted on 88.9% of harmful requests and complied with 81.5% of harmless requests, averaged across conversation turns and harm categories in the authors’ evaluation. | Abstract, §1 |

The evidence supports pretraining efficiency on the reported validation sets and capability gains across checkpoints in the observed RL run. Because the task mixture changed during RL, the checkpoint comparisons leave the effect of additional compute alone unresolved. The Abstract and §1 claim a 1M-token effective context and 3–4× lower inference compute than many models in Beam’s class. The supplied text documents 512K Stage 2 training and omits the detailed million-token results and matched inference comparisons.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ observations.** Long-context mixture choices often improved RULER while reducing other benchmark scores. Post-training duplicate retrieval can miss qualifying lexical matches, and its lexical overlap criterion can miss paraphrases, translations, and short samples embedded in longer documents (§§3.6, 4.1.4).
- **Analyst inference.** Changes in task difficulty and domain mix limit causal attribution for the RL gains. BrowseComp’s improvement is consistent with transfer across domains, but the supplied text gives no matched training control that isolates the source of the gain (§4.1.2, Figs. 16–17).
- **Analyst inference.** The aggregate Iris rates leave performance by harm category and turn unclear. The excerpt also lacks the detailed comparator settings needed to assess the authors’ safety ranking and inference-efficiency claims (Abstract, §1).

## Open research questions

1. **How much of the late DeepSWE gain comes from harder tasks?** Task difficulty and cumulative compute changed together (Fig. 16). Resume from the same checkpoint with matched compute and vary only the introduction of the harder task set.
2. **How well does long-context performance hold between 512K and 1M tokens?** The excerpt gives the Stage 2 training length and the effective-context claim, with no results broken down across that interval (§3, Abstract). Evaluate the same retrieval and reasoning task families across input lengths and report score changes alongside short-context retention.
3. **Does the Iris refusal–compliance balance hold across categories and turns?** The supplied results are aggregate (Abstract, §1). Run a stratified evaluation on a held-out prompt set and report both rates with uncertainty by category and turn.

## Confidence and missing information

**Medium.** The supplied source was truncated mid-§4.1.5 and resumes in the references before the appendices. It provides detailed evidence for parts of pretraining, midtraining, and agentic RL. Material gaps include the full final-model benchmark tables, matched serving and quantization comparisons, evaluation near the claimed context limit, merged-model ablations, detailed safety and third-party results, and release or access details for the training and evaluation assets.
