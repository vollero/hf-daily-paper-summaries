# HIVE-Bench: Evaluating Patch-Level Visual Representations for Egocentric Robot Manipulation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.30813) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30813)


## One-sentence takeaway

In HIVE-Bench, mean-pooling onboard visual tokens sharply reduced bimanual task success in the tested comparison, while inverse-dynamics error helped shortlist frozen encoders on RoboTwin but fell below random selection on RoboCasa (§§4.1, 5.4).

## Problem and motivation

Earlier encoder evaluations often use a pooled feature from a third-person view. Bimanual policies can attend to dense patches from moving, sometimes occluded onboard cameras, where local detail helps distinguish nearby grasp targets. The authors ask how pretrained encoders compare through that policy interface and whether inexpensive diagnostics can narrow the search before training and rolling out policies (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Benchmark.** HIVE-Bench compares 20 pretrained encoders from seven families on 24 simulated tasks across RoboCasa-GR1 and RoboTwin 2.0. A common DiT flow-matching action-head design consumes visual patch tokens, with frozen CLIP text features providing task conditioning in multi-task runs (§3, Figure 3).
- **Controlled comparisons.** The study evaluates single-task and multi-task training, frozen and fine-tuned encoders, and changes to demonstration count, checkpoint size, extraction layer, pooling, proprioception, and action-head queries (§4, Appendix C).
- **Diagnostic evaluation.** The authors compare token statistics, inverse-dynamics, state and object probes, and published vision scores against closed-loop success. They test whether associations hold across suites, encoder families, and adaptation, and evaluate retrospective candidate selection (§5, Appendix E).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Mean pooling sharply reduced success | Replacing each view’s patches with one mean token lowered frozen RoboCasa multi-task success from 32.28% to 9.94% for DINOv2 and from 31.22% to 6.44% for DINOv3. Success fell in 23 of 24 encoder–task pairs. | §4.1, Figures 4a and 9 |
| Fine-tuning improved every tested pair | Joint encoder and head training improved all 10 frozen-to-fine-tuned comparisons. Suite-mean gains ranged from 1.11 to 9.44 percentage points on RoboCasa single-task and from 2.22 to 26.06 points on RoboTwin multi-task. | §4.1, Figure 4b, Appendix C.2 |
| Frozen encoder rankings differed by suite | VGGT-Ω led the default frozen multi-task comparison on RoboCasa at 35.39% mean success. DepthVLM led RoboTwin at 53.67%. Across the 20 encoders, cross-suite rank correlation was Spearman ρ = 0.43. | §4.2, Table 3 |
| Task sharing preserved most success with fewer updates | Across 17 matched frozen RoboCasa encoders and equal demonstrations per task, mean success was 26.94% for separate policies and 23.62% for a shared policy. The shared policy retained 87.7% of mean success with 100,000 total optimizer steps versus 1.2 million. | §4.3, Figure 4c, Appendix C.1 |
| Demonstration scaling was more consistent than model scaling | At fixed updates, increasing RoboCasa multi-task demonstrations from 50 to 1,000 per task raised suite-mean success by 24.44–28.16 percentage points across the tested encoders. Larger checkpoints gave mixed returns across families. | §4.4, Figure 5 |
| Inverse dynamics helped on RoboTwin | Among frozen vision encoders, two-frame probe error correlated with RoboTwin multi-task success at Spearman ρ = −0.75 (95% bootstrap interval −0.90 to −0.40, adjusted q = 0.020). Across all eligible frozen encoders, the retrospective five-candidate screen retained a best success of 53.22%, versus 50.52% expected for random-five and 53.67% for the full cohort. The screen fell below random-five in both RoboCasa regimes. | §§5.1, 5.4, Appendix E.5, Table 20 |
| Published vision scores gave weak selection evidence | In exact-checkpoint, same-protocol comparisons, all 10 primary published vision metrics had permutation p > 0.05 against frozen multi-task success on both suites. | §5.2, Appendix E.6, Table 21 |
| A physical comparison preserved one simulated ordering | On three ARX A7 tasks, mean success was 49.67% for DINOv2, 41.00% for DINOv3, and 17.00% for CLIP, with 100 trials per task per encoder. | Appendix D, Table 15 |

The pooling and fine-tuning comparisons support those interventions for the tested encoders and policy setup. The diagnostic results support retrospective screening in specific settings. Frozen inverse-dynamics error failed to rank adapted RoboTwin success, and the physical experiment leaves broader encoder rankings and diagnostic transfer unresolved (§5.1, Appendix D).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** The main evaluation uses one action-head design and two simulation suites. Robot, action space, cameras, tasks, and data change together across suites, so their individual effects cannot be isolated. Most configurations use one training seed. The repeated-seed study covers four RoboCasa multi-task encoders (§§3.1, 6, Appendix C.7).
- **Author-stated.** The inverse-dynamics target includes current robot state, and adding a future frame also adds tokens and attention computation. The two-frame control therefore leaves motion sensitivity unresolved. Probes use only the head camera, while RoboTwin policies also see wrist views (Appendix E.2).
- **Analyst inference.** Encoder rankings also reflect preprocessing, token counts, cross-view context, and width-dependent head parameters. A common head design reduces architectural variation while leaving these interface differences in the comparison (§3.2, Appendices A.1, B).
- **Analyst inference.** The pooling result leaves learned compression untested. The task-sharing result measures optimizer steps under equal updates per policy, leaving the success trade-off at matched total compute open (Appendices C.1–C.2).

## Open research questions

1. **Would a motion-specific probe screen encoders across suites?** The current inverse-dynamics target contains a static component, and shortlist performance varies by suite. Compare capacity- and token-matched probes with state supplied explicitly and with future frames shuffled, then measure shortlist regret on a held-out suite (§§5.1, 5.4, Appendix E.2).
2. **How much spatial detail can a policy compress before success drops?** The pooling control tests one mean token per view for two encoders on RoboCasa. Compare learned pooling and spatial subsampling at matched token budgets across both suites and report success alongside per-step compute (Appendix C.2).
3. **Which encoder rankings persist across policy heads and hardware?** The main study uses one head design, and the physical comparison covers only three encoders. Rerun a broader encoder subset with a second action head and multiple training seeds on matched simulation and physical tasks (§6, Appendices C.7, D).
