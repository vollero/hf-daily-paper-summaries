# Beyond Attention Maps: Understanding and Improving Medical MLLMs via Visual Values

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.29361) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29361)


## One-sentence takeaway

On paired medical images where answer correctness differed, swapping decoder visual values changed predictions more than swapping attention weights, and VIVID used that effect to improve non-contrast visual question answering over the tested distillation baselines (§§3.3, 5.2).

## Problem and motivation

Attention maps show where a multimodal model reads, while the value vectors at those locations determine what information enters the attention readout. The authors ask which component better explains why the same model can answer differently from anatomically corresponding non-contrast (NC) and contrast-enhanced (CE) scans. They also ask how to transfer useful CE supervision to an NC model, given the cost and adverse-reaction risks the paper associates with contrast administration (§§1, 2.2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Paired medical VQA benchmark.** The authors construct 84,998 questions from 3,295 patients across liver CT, brain MRI, and breast MRI, with patient-disjoint splits. Paired views share annotation-derived questions and answers. TriALS contributes NC-only external tests (§3.1, Apps. A.1–A.4, Table 11).
- **Bidirectional decoder interventions.** The authors independently swap image-token attention weights or value vectors between views and measure changes in correct-answer probability and accuracy. They also test noisy attention weights and repeat the swaps on cases where the NC view has the correct answer (§3.3, App. B.2).
- **VIVID distillation.** Along student-generated answer prefixes, an auxiliary branch mixes paired CE values into attention-selected NC image positions. VIVID positively projects the CE teacher’s correction onto the resulting prediction shift in centered log-probability space and retains a weak direct teacher loss. Inference uses the NC student alone (§4, Fig. 4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Similar attention maps accompanied different answers | On NC-incorrect/CE-correct pairs, the authors report similar response-averaged foreground maps and layer-wise image-attention trends across liver CT, brain MRI, and breast MRI. | §3.2, Figs. 2–3 and 8–9 |
| Value swaps changed answers more than weight swaps | For Qwen3-VL on NC-incorrect/CE-correct liver CT pairs, all-layer CE-to-NC swaps raised accuracy by 37.55 percentage points (pp) for values versus 22.36 pp for weights. NC-to-CE swaps lowered it by 24.89 versus 8.02 pp. The larger value-swap effect also held on the reverse-correctness subset across all three datasets with Qwen3-VL-4B. | Table 1, App. B.2, Table 12 |
| VIVID led the tested distillation baselines | With NC-only inference, VIVID led OPSD, Med-OPD, and VA-OPD on all six test sets at both Qwen3-VL scales. At 4B, unweighted internal/external mean accuracies were 79.49%/72.05% versus 79.17%/71.05% for VA-OPD, the strongest competing distillation baseline by both means. The 2B model trailed the best off-the-shelf model on external liver CT. | Table 3 |
| Gains extended to other backbones and benchmarks | On liver CT, VIVID improved internal and external accuracy over SFT and OPSD for InternVL3-2B and MedGemma-4B. On four closed-ended zero-shot benchmarks, Qwen3-VL-4B VIVID averaged 68.72% accuracy versus 67.98% for OPSD and 67.80% for SFT, with a lower SLAKE score than both. | Tables 4–5 |
| The full method led its tested variants | Across both Qwen3-VL scales and both liver CT splits, full VIVID had higher accuracy than random patch selection, attention-weight mixing, and direct auxiliary-prediction distillation. | Table 6 |

The swaps support the authors’ conclusion that visual values carry more of the answer-relevant difference under the tested interventions. Attention still matters: perturbing its weights reduced the accuracy gains from CE value transfer on the primary subsets (Tables 2, 15 and 17). The training results establish gains on the reported benchmarks. Clinical equivalence between NC and CE imaging remains untested.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported scope.** The intervention analysis targets pairs where correctness changes between views, including a reverse-correctness analysis. VIVID uses paired training views, and the additional backbone comparisons and reported multi-seed study are confined to liver CT (§4, Table 4, App. B.2, Table 21).
- **Author-reported resource trade-off.** VIVID used more peak actor memory than Med-OPD and VA-OPD. Its observed step times were lower, but batch configurations and retained response counts differed across runs, and the memory figures exclude separately hosted teachers (Table 23).
- **Analyst inference.** The selected-slice, closed-ended protocol leaves full-volume and open-ended performance uncertain. Radiologist scoring of generated evidence correctness used questions every compared method answered correctly, so those scores leave evidence quality on model errors unresolved (App. A.3, §5.1, App. C.5).
- **Analyst inference.** Phase-specific liver CT window levels mean the swap study measures differences between the processed views. An experiment with matched windowing would help isolate the contribution of contrast acquisition (App. A.2, §3.3).

## Open research questions

1. **How much spatial misalignment can VIVID tolerate?** The method mixes values at corresponding token positions, and TriALS is used only for NC testing. Train with graded offsets between paired views and measure NC accuracy against the aligned-pair setup and OPSD (§4.1, App. A.2).
2. **Can middle-layer mixing preserve the gains at lower training cost?** Single-layer swap effects peak in middle layers, while VIVID mixes values across all decoder layers. Compare middle-layer-only and all-layer training with matched batches, reporting accuracy, peak memory, and step time (§4.1, Fig. 10, Table 23).
3. **Does VIVID improve evidence quality when its answers are wrong?** The correctness review samples shared correct answers, while evidence–answer consistency can reward support for an incorrect prediction. Have blinded radiologists rate factual grounding on a sample stratified by answer correctness (App. C.5).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the reported benchmark comparisons and intervention effects within their stated settings. The supplied text gives a project page but does not establish code, checkpoint, or processed-benchmark availability or licensing. It also omits the numerical values for the projection stabilizer and cutoff (§4.2) and does not establish how much of the intervention effect persists across the full, unselected paired population.
