# From Rewarding What Models Say to How They Think: Attention Process Reward Model for LLM Reinforcement Learning

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.30627) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30627)


## One-sentence takeaway

Adding a reward model that scores hidden states and attention maps improved four-benchmark math pass@8 by an average of 2.88 percentage points across five paired Qwen3 RL comparisons (Table 2).

## Problem and motivation

Final-answer rewards give sparse feedback, while textual process rewards typically require step-level judgments and observe only the written solution. The authors call the resulting blind spot *textual reward aliasing*. Under a rank condition, they show that distinct valid causal attention matrices can produce the same attention output, illustrating how external observations can miss differences in internal computation (§2, Appendix G.1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Internal process scorer.** APRM fuses final-layer hidden states with late-layer attention, pools tokens into learned latent concepts, and uses concept-level attention as both an edge bias and a message gate in a graph attention network. It outputs one score per rollout (§3.1, Figure 2).
- **Training with verifier labels and attention perturbations.** A ranking loss favors correct over incorrect rollouts for the same prompt. A counterfactual loss favors original attention over perturbed attention for correct rollouts while holding hidden states fixed (§3.2).
- **RL integration.** APRM adds a weighted, group-centered score to the base token-level advantage when a rollout group contains both correct and incorrect answers. It is periodically refreshed on current-policy rollouts to address distribution drift (§3.3, Algorithm 1).

The benchmark results establish that a set of eight samples more often contained a correct answer in the reported APRM comparisons. The authors suggest improved candidate diversity, which would require a direct diversity measure to verify. The shuffle and regression results establish sensitivity to attention structure and an association with judge-rated quality under the stated controls. Faithful recovery of internal reasoning and a causal role for the highlighted concepts remain untested.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ stated scope.** APRM uses fixed-weight additive reward integration, and the authors leave the choice of internal representation and reward architecture open (Appendix A). GPU constraints also shaped the reported training configurations (Appendix D.5).
- **Analyst inference.** All evaluated backbones are from the Qwen family, and the generalization study uses DAPO on one instruction-tuned backbone. GMPO and FAPO appear as baselines without paired APRM variants, limiting claims about transfer across models and optimizers (Tables 2 and 5).
- **Analyst inference.** GLM-5.1 sees the gold answer and correctness flag, so residual predictiveness is evidence about that judge’s ratings under the specified controls. The hidden-only ablation changes both attention access and concept modeling, and the architectural ablations measure ranking accuracy. Their effect on downstream RL gains remains unresolved (Appendices B and D.4).

## Open research questions

1. **Which components drive downstream pass@8 gains?** The architecture comparisons end at rollout ranking. Run matched RL trials with full APRM, APRM without GAT, and a hidden-only scorer, holding policy and rollout budgets fixed.
2. **How much does refreshing APRM help?** Figure 7 shows score-scale shifts after refreshes, while the reported results leave their contribution to accuracy unresolved. Compare frozen and periodically refreshed scorers across repeated runs with matched rollout budgets.
3. **Does APRM track independently assessed step validity?** The process-quality analysis uses one LLM judge with access to answer correctness. Compare APRM with blinded expert step annotations on correctness-matched rollouts.
