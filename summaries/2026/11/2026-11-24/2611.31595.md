# LUCID: Statistically Grounded Self-Correction for LLM Reasoning Evaluation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.31595) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31595)


## One-sentence takeaway

On PRM800K, one round of expert-informed prompt feedback reduced GPT-5-mini’s soundness-rate gap and improved unsound-step F1, while class-balanced performance changed little and a separate statistical correction improved trace ranking and low-budget estimation.

## Problem and motivation

Correct final answers can conceal unsound intermediate reasoning. Expert step labels are costly, and systematic errors from cheaper LLM graders can distort reasoning audits, trace rankings, and statistical estimates. The authors ask whether the same expert sample can diagnose and correct a grader’s bias at inference time while also supporting downstream statistical debiasing (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Expert-grounded re-grading.** LUCID measures signed rating bias overall and by step type, plus its correlation with trace position. It feeds a plain-text summary back to the grader without updating its weights. A problem-clustered pre-check screens for material overall bias, and a separate expert-labeled validation set determines whether to keep the revised ratings (§3.1, Fig. 1).
- **Step-level adaptation of existing estimators.** The paper applies Design-based Supervised Learning (DSL) to raw ratings and step features, holding out whole problems when fitting a boosted-tree model. Its out-of-fold probabilities score traces, and inverse-probability-weighted expert residuals correct logistic-regression estimates. Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI) serves as a comparator (§3.2, App. A).
- **Sound-step transition estimation.** The authors extend DSL to adjacent pairs labeled at both endpoints, estimating transitions between step types as ratios of adjusted counts. Regression uncertainty is clustered by problem (§§3.3–3.4, App. C).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| One pass reduced strictness and improved detection on PRM800K | On 1,000 held-out steps, the absolute predicted–expert soundness-rate gap fell from 7.8 to 0.9 percentage points. Unsound-step F1 rose from 0.146 to 0.192 (+32% relative), and sound steps flagged as unsound fell from 144 to 76 with unsound-step recall maintained. Content-blind rate adjustments reduced the gap but lowered unsound-step F1. | Fig. 1, Tables 1–2, 28 |
| Class-balanced performance changed little | On 84 sound and 84 unsound PRM800K steps, accuracy moved from 57.1% to 58.3% and unsound-step F1 from 0.333 to 0.327. | App. J.1 |
| A sampled diagnosis and simpler feedback retained gains | The canonical PRM800K result used a corpus-wide diagnostic profile. Separate tests with 1,000 labeled diagnostic steps retained gains, and a four-run ablation found similar accuracy gains for overall-gap and full-profile feedback. | §4, Table 31, App. G.1, G.5 |
| Transfer depended on the grader and setting | GPT-5-mini feedback improved GLM-5.2 accuracy on PRM800K with no reported unsound-step F1 gain. GPT-5-mini gained over 5 percentage points in ProcessBench step accuracy overall, with a slight decline on GSM8K. The deployment rule skipped balanced Mind2Web and rolled back ProofWriter. | §5.2, Tables 3, 32, 37–39 |
| DSL improved trace selection | With 1,000 expert labels, DSL probabilities improved final-answer success over raw GPT-5-mini ratings by 13.7 percentage points at K=1 and 19.5 at K=2 across 7,348 MATH problems. Performance matched or slightly exceeded ranking by mean expert step labels through K=5. | Fig. 3, Table 25 |
| DSL improved low-budget estimation | On PRM800K with 200 expert labels, logistic-coefficient RMSE against the all-expert fit fell from 6.09 for expert-only estimation to 0.84 for DSL, which also beat PPI. With 1,000 uniformly sampled, expert-labeled adjacent pairs, relative Frobenius error fell from 0.083 for raw ratings to 0.031 for DSL against the expert-soundness transition matrix. | Table 1, §5.3, Table 34, Fig. 11 |

The authors interpret the balanced-subset result as evidence that feedback changes grading strictness. The experiment leaves improved sound-versus-unsound discrimination unestablished. The ranking gains come from DSL probabilities and measure final-answer success, so they leave open whether prompt re-grading improves trace selection or whether the selected traces contain sounder reasoning (§§5.1, 7).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Coverage centers on GPT-5-mini and GLM-5.2, with most benchmark results based on single passes. The checks cannot distinguish conservatism from missing skill before re-grading. One dataset-wide profile leaves residual late-step bias and can go stale under distribution shift (§§5.2, 7, App. G.3).
- **Author-stated.** Both methods inherit systematic bias in the human reference. The model-assigned taxonomy lacks human inter-annotator validation, and model agreement is especially weak for verification and restatement. DSL’s regression advantage diminishes at larger expert budgets, where expert-only fits can briefly perform better (§§5.3, 7, App. G.6).
- **Analyst inference.** Both deployment checks use the overall rate gap, so offsetting subgroup errors could pass the initial screen or survive validation. Also, PRM800K’s binary target groups neutral and incorrect labels together, leaving performance on explicitly incorrect steps alone unclear (§§3.1, 4).

## Open research questions

1. **Can position-specific feedback reduce residual late-step bias?** The dataset-wide prompt left substantial late-step under-rating. Compare global and position-specific prompts on held-out problems at the same label budget, tracking both calibration and unsound-step F1 by position (App. G.3).
2. **Can a diagnostic distinguish strictness from lack of skill before re-grading?** The current method detects ineffective correction after the pass. Test whether class-specific recall on a small balanced diagnostic set predicts validation gains across graders and domains (§§5.2, 7).
3. **Does prompt re-grading improve best-of-K selection directly?** The reported ranking experiment used DSL scores. Re-grade all candidate traces on a held-out problem set and compare raw ratings, prompt-corrected ratings, and DSL probabilities under the same ranking and tie-breaking protocol (§§5.1, 7).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported PRM800K comparisons and the distinction between prompt re-grading and DSL. Generalization is less certain given the limited grader coverage and single-pass results on most benchmarks. The supplied text includes many table captions without full rows, leaving some per-model and per-benchmark effect sizes and uncertainty estimates unavailable. It references full prompts and benchmark scripts in supplementary material that was not supplied. Human taxonomy validation and direct best-of-K evaluation of prompt-corrected ratings were not performed.
