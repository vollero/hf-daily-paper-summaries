# A Glimpse of Training VLMs as Foundation Models for Robotic Manipulation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.29338) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29338)


## One-sentence takeaway

nanoAstra-9B fine-tunes a VLM to generate executable semantic actions and achieves 17.8% zero-shot success on randomized RoboTwin 2.0, ahead of the evaluated baselines, while finishing below its initial accuracy on all four reported vision-language measures (§§3.3, 3.5, Table 1, Fig. 7).

## Problem and motivation

The authors ask how to train a general-purpose VLM for manipulation across unfamiliar tasks and environments while retaining its broader vision-language skills. Limited disclosure of GPT-6 Astra’s training motivates an explicit recipe. The authors hypothesize that densely sampled control targets make it harder to learn reusable behaviors because the same motion can produce different targets depending on execution speed, sampling frequency, or local corrections (§1, Fig. 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Semantic action supervision.** A pipeline filters and aligns heterogeneous demonstrations, captions subtasks, and segments trajectories at behavioral decision points. It converts 5,310.5 retained hours into 14.9 million training samples pairing observations with local progress descriptions and parameterized arm and gripper commands (§2.1, Figs. 2, 4).
- **Capability-preserving supervised fine-tuning (CP-SFT).** The recipe fully fine-tunes Qwen3.5-9B on robot and general vision-language examples through its native text interface. A robot-specific prompt and structured targets guide action generation, and a modified number-token loss adds decimal-place weighting and explicit sign supervision (§§2.1–2.2, 3.1).
- **Streaming execution.** The controller executes semantic steps as they arrive and requests the next response while remaining steps from the current response are still running (§3.3, Fig. 6).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Highest reported randomized RoboTwin success rate | nanoAstra-9B reached **17.8%** over 50 tasks with 10 runs per task. It led both LingBot-VLA-2.0 and Qwen3.8-27B with thinking by **7.0 percentage points**. Evaluated WAMs reached at most **0.2%**. | §§3.2–3.3, Table 1 |
| Higher success on RoboDojo Open | **4.75% (19/400)** versus **0.25%** for untuned Qwen3.5-9B without thinking, over 8 tasks with 10 seeds and 5 runs per seed. The authors also report the highest progress score among evaluated models. | §3.4, Table 4 |
| Inference-to-motion ratio varied by GPU | On one RTX 4090, inference took **3.89 s** for **2.87 s** of motion, a ratio of **1.36**. On one H20, inference took **1.69 s** for **2.66 s** of motion, a ratio of **0.64**. | §3.3, Table 2 |
| Streaming reduced idle time | On one H20, idle share fell from **35.0%** with blocking execution to **6.9%** with streaming, with no reported success-rate loss. RoboTwin ran continuously under its official time limit, with 5 runs per task. Idle measurements excluded first turns and turns affected by a failed step. | §3.3, Table 3, Fig. 6 |
| Vision-language accuracy partially recovered during training | Initial → final accuracy was **58.6% → 53.1%** on MMMU, **47.3% → 41.0%** on ERQA, **82.2% → 81.6%** on CV-Bench-2D, and **92.6% → 87.6%** on CV-Bench-3D. | §3.5, Fig. 7 |

The results support improved benchmark performance for the combined training recipe and reduced waiting under the tested H20 streaming protocol. Absolute manipulation success remained low. The experiments leave the contribution of each training component and the policy’s reliability on physical robots unresolved.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported.** Vision-language capability retention was incomplete. Two RoboTwin failures illustrate different problems: straight-line commands opened a microwave door only partway, and drifting progress predictions caused the policy to revisit a completed subtask and attend to a distractor (§§3.5–3.6, Figs. 7–8).
- **Analyst inference.** The untuned-model comparison measures the effect of the full adaptation recipe. Without controlled ablations, the effects of semantic segmentation, progress captions, prompt conditioning, vision-language co-training, and numerical loss remain unclear (§§2.1–2.2, 3.3–3.5).
- **Analyst inference.** The streaming result uses a continuously running simulator, and the idle metric omits startup and failure-affected turns. Its scope is the measured portion of that protocol (§3.3, Table 3).

## Open research questions

1. **How much do semantic steps and progress captions each contribute?** The reported model uses both (§§2.1, 3.3). Train matched variants that vary action granularity and the presence of progress captions, then compare zero-shot success under the same protocol.
2. **Which CP-SFT components reduce capability loss?** The checkpoint curves describe the combined recipe (§3.5). Vary robot-prompt conditioning and general vision-language co-training independently while holding robot supervision fixed, then measure both retention and manipulation success.
3. **Can object-centric actions and explicit subtask tracking address the observed failures?** The authors propose both based on illustrative cases (§3.6). Test each change independently on held-out articulated-object tasks and multistage tasks with distractors, recording completion and subtask regressions.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The prose provides the headline metrics and their protocols. The supplied text omits full table rows, per-task outcomes, uncertainty estimates, the exact blocking and streaming success rates, and the numerical RoboDojo progress scores. It also leaves component ablations, physical-robot validation, and the release status of code, weights, and the processed corpus unestablished.
