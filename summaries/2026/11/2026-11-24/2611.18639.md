# SynthDCB: World Distillation for Scalable Device Control Benchmarking

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.18639) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18639)


## One-sentence takeaway

SynthDCB turns device knowledge into reusable simulations and replay-validated tasks across Home, Car, and Phone, where the strongest of seven tested models achieved 68.2% Pass@1 on 2,560 conversational tasks (§§3–4).

## Problem and motivation

As devices gain capabilities, a benchmark must update the executable behaviors, tool interfaces, tasks, and success criteria together. The authors argue that generating more instructions over a fixed simulator leaves its executable coverage unchanged. They also want to evaluate whether agents can discover what a configured world supports, distinguish device settings from environmental outcomes, and complete feasible parts of a request when other parts fail (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **World distillation and simulator.** Typed capability specifications link discoverable tools, Python behavior, and task generation. Deterministic templates and LLM-generated implementations and tests pass through validation and bounded repair. Reusable components are composed into worlds with shared environmental state and simulated time (§§3.1–3.2, Fig. 2).
- **Execution-grounded benchmark.** Builders derive requirements and private scoring targets from available capabilities, then replay reference plans to validate the tasks. The benchmark spans five task families and includes memory, infeasibility, and partially feasible requests (§3.3, Table 11).
- **Agent and construction studies.** The authors compare models under a common ReAct protocol, audit failed attempts, and measure construction cost and capability coverage under different levels of source documentation (§§4–5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| GPT-5.6 Luna led overall | 68.2% Pass@1 across seven models on 2,560 conversational test tasks using the same ReAct harness. Luna led Home and Car, while DeepSeek led Phone. | §§4.1–4.4, Table 4 |
| Query performance varied sharply by subtype | GPT variants scored 82.1–94.9% Pass@1 on top-k queries and below 13% on counting and enumeration in the conversational test. The authors’ audit primarily attributes the latter failures to answer submission. | §§4.4–4.6, Fig. 3 |
| Partial feasibility was harder for GLM | GLM led on the 170 infeasible test tasks with 77.6% Pass@1 and achieved 28.7% on the 101 partial tasks, which also required completing independent feasible requests. | §§4.1, 4.4, Table 4 |
| Omitting instruction details reduced success | On paired validation tasks for the GPT-5.6 models, Implicit versus Template reduced average Pass@1 by 27.1 percentage points. Average space-property accuracy fell from 75.6% to 27.3%. | §4.5, Table 15 |
| World distillation used fewer construction tokens | When building one RobotVacuum class with GPT-5.6 Sol across three documentation levels, world distillation used 0.9–66.0% fewer tokens than Codex low and 46.2–92.5% fewer than Codex xhigh. It achieved higher complete coverage than Codex low at comparable token budgets on the two richer inputs. | §5, Appendices C and D.3 |
| Three-domain construction cost was measured | The combined build used 19.16M tokens, took 113.87 minutes with 16 workers per domain, and cost an estimated $120.09 before cache discounts. | §5, Appendix C |

The results distinguish state control, answer delivery, feasibility reporting, and timing under the benchmark’s scoring rules. Pass@1 combines those requirements, so a failure can reflect an incorrect state, a malformed submission, or another unmet check. The construction comparison supports a token-efficiency claim for the tested RobotVacuum setup. Broader efficiency across device classes and readiness for physical deployment remain unestablished.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** The environmental dynamics are behavioral abstractions without physical calibration. The authors limit their claims to simulated tool use and call for independent validation before physical deployment (Appendix A.3, Ethics Statement).
- **Author-reported scope.** The controlled construction comparison uses one device excluded from the final benchmark. Coverage was limited with sparse documentation, and the generated artifacts retained gaps such as non-triggering schedules (§5, Appendices C and D.3).
- **Analyst inference.** Validation and test are disjoint by base task and may share world instances, leaving generalization to held-out worlds or device classes unmeasured by this split (§4.1, Appendix B.1).
- **Analyst inference.** Rankings and failure shares may depend on the shared tool protocol, including typed answer submission. The multi-label audit describes observed failures and cannot isolate their causes (§§4.2, 4.6, Appendix D.4).

## Open research questions

1. **How well do agents generalize to unseen worlds and device classes?** The splits can share worlds. Evaluate matched task families with separate world-disjoint and class-disjoint splits.
2. **How much of the query failure rate comes from answer formatting?** The audit identifies submission errors under one protocol. Compare typed submission with schema-constrained decoding while holding tasks, observations, and answer semantics fixed.
3. **Do construction savings persist across devices and domains?** The controlled comparison covers one RobotVacuum class. Repeat it across Home, Car, and Phone at matched documentation levels using independently authored behavioral checks and reporting both coverage and token cost.
