# Motion Mamba 2.0: Autoregressive Diffusion Mamba for Long-Horizon Humanoid Control

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.29897) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29897)


## One-sentence takeaway

Motion Mamba 2.0 uses attention-readable recurrent memory to improve terminal spatial control in retargeted HiPHI rollouts up to 39.52 s, while its optimized execution delivers a 6.138× median paired speedup over the reference implementation on an RTX 4090 (Tables 1, 4, and 7).

## Problem and motivation

Interactive animation and humanoid control need a continuous stream of motion references that respond to newly arriving text instructions and spatial goals. The authors identify two gaps in windowed diffusion generation. Bounded attention caches evict older motion features, while expanding caches increase storage and computation. Repeated denoising also adds execution overhead, even for short windows (§I).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Persistent content memory (PCM).** Each denoising step predicts the root trajectory first and uses it to condition body articulation. The architecture adopts ARDY’s explicit-root and latent-body representation and combines local attention with resumable Mamba-3 recurrence. PCM turns recurrent state summaries, changes between accepted states, and active text features into gated slots that local attention can read (§§IV-A–C).
2. **History updates after acceptance.** All denoising steps read the same history snapshot. A clean pass updates the cache, recurrent states, and PCM slots together after each window is accepted. With a fixed window and conditioning budget, stored context and computation per window stay bounded as the rollout grows (§IV-C, Appendix B-B).
3. **State-preserving inference.** The implementation skips unused state materialization, fuses selected operations, and uses separate root and body CUDA Graphs, each capturing all three guidance branches (§IV-D).
4. **G1 evaluation.** The authors assemble a common G1-format corpus from native and retargeted trajectories, compare independently trained G1 adaptations under a matched update budget, and test the generator with the existing SONIC tracker in simulation and on a physical Unitree G1 (§§III, V-A, VI).

The paired, matched-budget comparisons support improved terminal spatial control for the evaluated G1 adaptations. Semantic and distributional results are more mixed. For example, MotionStreamer led single-window HumanML3D R@3, and CMDM led single-window HiPHI L2-FID (§V-B, Tables 1 and 3). R@3 and L2-FID support within-benchmark comparisons because evaluators and candidate or reference sets differ. Cmd. measures coarse root-motion agreement, and L2-FID summarizes individual windows, leaving full-instruction fulfillment and whole-rollout coherence open. The GPU benchmark measures steady-state generation, while the robot trials measure the combined generator and tracker (§V-D, §VI, Appendix C-D).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Object manipulation is outside the current scope, and the HiPHI object-interaction subset is excluded. PCM has fixed capacity and carries compressed context, leaving exact recall of evicted events unverified (§§I, III-A, IV-C, VII).
- **Protocol limits acknowledged by the authors.** Extended benchmark rollouts are confined to retargeted HiPHI, whose held-out initializations span three actors. Instruction-phase composition and terminal deadlines change across horizons, so cross-horizon scores cannot isolate the effect of duration. Training uses clean ground-truth prefixes shorter than the local cache, while inference commits generated histories (Appendices B-A and C-A–D).
- **Analyst inference.** The ablations support PCM under the tested setup. They leave the separate effect of the clean-history commit rule and the trade-off between PCM capacity and cache width unresolved (§V-C). Hardware trials use the same generator and tracker throughout, limiting attribution of success or failure to either component (§VI).

## Open research questions

1. **How much of PCM’s gain comes from retaining information after cache eviction?** The terminal goals remain available throughout the benchmark rollout, and training histories fit inside the cache. A follow-up could cue a future action once, require it after eviction, and compare Native and PCM across matched cache widths (Appendices C-A–B).
2. **Does training on generated histories reduce drift at longer horizons?** Training uses ground-truth prefixes, and benchmark instruction schedules change with rollout length. A follow-up could compare clean-prefix and generated-prefix training under matched budgets, then vary rollout length while holding instruction schedules and target timing fixed (Appendices B-A and C-A).
3. **Which component drives physical execution failures?** The robot trials evaluate one integrated system. A paired experiment could compare generated and prerecorded references on the same SONIC tracker while logging reference error, tracking error, and interventions (§VI, Appendix D).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the reported method and headline comparisons. The supplied text gives protocols and key numbers in prose, but omits the complete table bodies. Material gaps include the full baseline rankings and score margins, the real-motion calibration values in Table 12, the complete handoff and tail-latency statistics in Table 9, and the base diffusion step count stored in the G1 configuration. The abstract links code, while checkpoint and retargeted-corpus release status remain unclear from the supplied text.
