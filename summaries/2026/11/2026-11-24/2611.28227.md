# Feedback Shortcut: Speculative Decoding for Latent Feedback Transformers

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.28227) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28227)


## One-sentence takeaway

With one pipelined correction, Feedback Shortcut reported 2.36× and 2.75× higher batch-one decoding throughput than exact decoding at 135M and 780M parameters, respectively, while staying within \(10^{-4}\) bits per byte on Paloma (§4.2, Table 1).

## Problem and motivation

Latent feedback feeds each token position’s top-layer state into the next position, creating a dependency that blocks parallel draft verification. Approximate states restore parallelism, but training the model around those approximations can worsen its exact-decoding quality, and errors from using them during decoding can persist in the cache. The authors seek to reduce both sources of error without adding separate verification calls (§1, Fig. 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **State alignment.** A lightweight projection maps chained multi-token prediction (MTP) head states to the first-pass hidden states used as feedback during training. The alignment loss updates only the projection, preserving the base model’s exact-decoding quality (§2, Figs. 2–3).
- **Pipelined correction.** Each verification call checks new drafts while recomputing pending positions using their predecessors’ states from the previous call. The correction depth \(D\) controls how much refinement is required before committing a prediction (§3, Fig. 4, Appendix A Algorithm 1).
- **Gap decomposition.** The authors separate changes in exact-decoding quality caused by training from approximation error introduced during speculative decoding, then use that distinction to compare four feedback stand-ins (§1 Eq. 4, §2, §4.3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Near-ceiling quality at batch size 1 | With state alignment and \(D=1\), both model sizes stayed within \(10^{-4}\) BPB of exact latent-feedback decoding, averaged over seven Paloma subsets. | §§4.1–4.2, Table 1, Appendix A |
| Throughput and training cost | At 135M and 780M, \(D=1\) delivered 2.36× and 2.75× steady-state throughput at batch size 1 on first-turn Spec-Bench and SPEED-Bench prompts. The matched Transformer with MTP delivered 2.49× and 2.93×. Each speedup is relative to its own exact decoding in the same engine. Total training FLOPs were 1.13× and 1.19× the matched Transformer’s, with about 2% attributed to state alignment. | §§4.1–4.2, Table 1, Appendix A |
| Stand-in trade-off | Zeros and raw MTP states preserved exact-decoding quality but incurred several times state alignment’s Paloma one-call error. In-place substitution had the lowest one-call error but worsened its exact-decoding BPB. | §4.3, Table 2 |
| Quality–throughput trade-off at large batch sizes | At batch size 256, \(D=1\) delivered about 1.7× exact-decoding throughput. In the high-batch regime, \(D=0\) ran about as fast as standard Transformer speculation and retained 80–85% of the latent-feedback quality advantage. Batched timing excludes prefill and CPU bookkeeping between calls. | §4.3, Figs. 5–6, Appendix A |
| Small distribution shifts in free generation | Across 35 samples of 1,024 tokens at temperature 1, the authors report state alignment’s KL divergence from exact decoding below \(6\times10^{-4}\) nats per token with \(D=1\). A second correction reduced it about twentyfold. | §4.3, Appendix B Table 4 |
| Downstream gold-answer scoring | From \(D=1\) onward, state alignment and in-place substitution each served within \(10^{-4}\) BPB of their own exact-decoding quality on Olmo 3 Base Easy gold answers. The authors also report a downstream advantage over the matched Transformer. | §4.2, Appendices D–E, Tables 7–8 |

The evidence supports a quality–throughput trade-off for the two tested model sizes. Paloma and downstream BPB score fixed text, so they provide limited evidence about generated-answer correctness. The free-generation tests quantify distribution shifts and greedy departures, leaving exact output equivalence unestablished. The headline throughput ratios apply to steady-state decoding in the reported engine.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** The shortcut remains approximate. The authors leave lower-error stand-ins that preserve exact-decoding quality and larger-scale experiments to future work (§6).
- **Author-reported.** Correction adds work per call as batching makes verification compute-bound. Temperature-1 sampling also reduced speedups in the single-request tests (§4.3, Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference.** The fixed three-head draft chain and single-GPU engine limit conclusions about other draft lengths, hardware, and serving stacks. Gold-answer BPB also leaves downstream task success under free generation open (§4.1, Appendices A and E).

## Open research questions

1. **Can a better projection make correction unnecessary?** State alignment still incurs measurable error at \(D=0\). Compare first-pass targets with exact-feedback targets at matched projection size and training FLOPs, then measure Paloma BPB and free-generation KL (§§2, 4.3).
2. **How does the trade-off change with model scale and draft length?** The study tests two scales with three draft heads. Sweep draft length and correction depth at larger scales, measuring throughput, committed tokens per call, and BPB across batch sizes (§4.1, §6).
3. **Do the downstream BPB gains carry over to answer correctness?** The downstream protocol scores gold answers. Generate answers under exact decoding and Feedback Shortcut on the same tasks, then compare task-level correctness and disagreement rates (Appendices A and E).
