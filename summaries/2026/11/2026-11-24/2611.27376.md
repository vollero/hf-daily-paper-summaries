# Composable Soft-Prompt Distillation of RL Experts: Toward Continual Post-Training

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.27376) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27376)


## One-sentence takeaway

Independently distilled soft prompts recovered at least 90% of the RL teacher’s gain in 22 of 23 single-expert settings and, when concatenated on one frozen backbone, scored 67.83% across four domains versus 67.79% for multi-teacher on-policy distillation (§§3.2–3.3, Tables 1–2).

## Problem and motivation

RL post-training often produces separate domain experts that need to be consolidated as new capabilities arrive. The authors identify a gap between methods that repeatedly consolidate experts into shared weights, risking interference and degradation outside the target domains, and methods that keep experts separate but require routing or request-specific adapter computation. They ask whether each new expert can be integrated using only its own supervision while retaining the ability to combine it with prior experts (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Independent expert distillation.** CSPD freezes the shared pre-RL model and fits one 256-vector input prompt per expert through length-normalized response likelihood on verifier-filtered teacher responses (§2.1).
- **Composition by concatenation.** At inference, selected prompts are concatenated in the system message without joint training. To prepare prompts for preceding context, half of training examples place a 128–256-token prefix drawn from other same-domain examples before the soft prompt. The prefix is omitted at inference (§2.2, Appendix A).
- **Empirical characterization.** The authors test individual expert recovery, addition and removal of independently trained prompts, held-out generalization, and request-specific serving. They also probe how one math prompt shifts next-token predictions toward its teacher (§§3–4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Single prompts recover most teacher gains | Across 23 math, code, and agent settings, the best evaluated prompted checkpoints recovered at least 90% of the teacher’s score gain over the pre-RL student in 22 settings and matched or exceeded the teacher in 15. | §3.2, Table 1, Appendix B.3 |
| Four-prompt composition reaches MOPD’s mean | On frozen M2RL-SFT, the mean avg@K score across AIME24, LiveCodeBench v5, GPQA-Diamond, and IFBench was 67.83% for concatenation, 67.79% for MOPD, 65.38% for the same prompts used individually, and 50.41% for the unprompted student. The respective per-benchmark sample counts were 64, 8, 16, and 8. | §3.3, Table 2 |
| Sequential addition improves the mean, with domain interference | Prepending prompts in instruction-following, science, code, then math arrival order improved the four-domain mean at every step. Adding code lowered IFBench from 66.71% to 64.58%, and adding math brought it to 68.42%. | §3.4, Fig. 3 |
| Prefix augmentation helps in the tested ablation | With AdamW, prefix augmentation raised the four-domain composition mean from 64.50% to 66.60% at the final checkpoint under the same avg@K protocol. | Appendix C, Table 5 |
| Composition leads on held-out mean | The four-prompt composition averaged 56.6% versus 53.5% for MOPD across six equally weighted held-out benchmarks and led the compared methods on four. On TruthfulQA, the unprompted student scored 64.6% versus 64.2% for CSPD. | §§1, 3.5, Table 3, Appendix E |
| Request-specific soft prompts have low measured serving overhead | On GPT-OSS-20B served with SGLang on one B200, with 20,480 text input tokens, 512 generated tokens, and one distinct synthetic adapter per request, soft-prompt throughput stayed within 1.5% of the backbone across seven concurrency levels. At 32 concurrent requests, throughput was 715.4 output tokens/s versus 721.3 for the backbone and 298.5 for unmerged rank-4 LoRA. P95 time to first token was 17.26 s versus 16.89 s and 39.21 s, respectively. | §3.6, Fig. 5 |
| One math prompt shifts token predictions toward its teacher | On 200 held-out GPT-OSS-Swallow math teacher traces, the prompt closed 86.8% of the next-token KL gap, compared with at most 2.1% for the tested written instructions. In a separate 60-trace masking analysis, response-only attention retained 80% of the prompt’s KL improvement and input-only attention retained 33%. | §4, Fig. 6, Appendix G.3–G.4 |

The evidence supports single-expert transfer across diverse tested pairs and shows that the four tested modules can be added without retraining earlier modules. The near-equal in-domain means are observed scores, with no statistical equivalence test reported. The held-out mean measures transfer on the selected benchmarks, and the attention analysis measures distributional alignment for one prompt. Broad capability preservation and a causal link between checking behavior and task accuracy remain unestablished.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Composition uses one backbone, four domains, and one seed. Prompts can interfere, add context and KV-cache cost, and require re-distillation for a new backbone (§5, Appendix F).
- **Author-stated.** Single-expert checkpoints and Task Arithmetic coefficients were selected on evaluation benchmarks. Optimizer and learning-rate screening used IFBench. Recovery ratios are sensitive to small teacher gains, and single-expert protocols vary by pair (Appendices A, B.3, D).
- **Analyst inference.** Nemotron’s prompted student receives teacher-provided bug localization while the baselines localize end to end, so that result does not isolate end-to-end transfer. The supervision ablation also varies per-question response caps, leaving the effect of correctness partly confounded with question coverage (Appendices B.1–B.2).
- **Analyst inference.** The serving comparison covers one synthetic workload and one unmerged LoRA configuration. The mechanism analysis uses one prompt without confidence intervals, and XSTest covers refusal behavior on the tested requests (§3.6, Appendices F and G.4, Impact Statement).

## Open research questions

1. **When does adding experts stop improving the mean?** The study uses one four-expert library and a shared arrival sequence for the prepend and append comparisons. Test larger libraries across backbones, seeds, and randomized arrival sequences, tracking both mean scores and the largest per-domain regressions (§5, Appendix C).
2. **Can request-level prompt selection reduce interference and serving cost?** Selection is manual, and removal tests reveal both helpful and harmful cross-domain effects. Compare a learned selector with full concatenation and single-prompt selection on held-out requests, measuring task scores, latency, and KV-cache use (§3.4, §5).
3. **Does the observed attention pathway explain cross-domain transfer?** The masking analysis covers one math prompt in isolation. Apply prompt-specific attention masks to composed systems and measure both next-token KL and task accuracy, including the effect of the math prompt on science questions (§3.4, Appendix G.4).
