# Self-WSP: Can Language Models Improve Themselves Through Weight-Space Programming?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.28833) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28833)


## One-sentence takeaway

With an outer loop that checks edits against reference answers, Qwen3.5-0.8B proposed changes to its own weights that substantially improved selected models on two math benchmarks, while typical continued runs stalled and the updated editor showed no clear advantage over a frozen editor in the reported shuffled-interface comparison (§§3, 5–6).

## Problem and motivation

Prior weight-space programming showed that a stronger language model could directly edit a smaller model’s weights. The authors ask whether a general-purpose model can edit its own weights and use the updated weights to propose better future edits. This distinction matters for recursive self-improvement because better task performance alone leaves the model’s editing ability untested (§§1, 8).

## Approach and contributions

- **A shared-weight editor and solver.** Qwen3.5-0.8B reads sampled values and summary statistics from its own tensors and proposes grammar-constrained edits without gradients. An outer loop tests each valid proposal on two disjoint practice batches with reference answers. Accepted weights serve both roles. Separate validation questions select checkpoints and runs before final testing (§2, Fig. 1, Appendices A–B).
- **Tests of what drives improvement.** The study compares shuffled and layer-ordered interfaces, evaluates random search and a frozen editor under the shuffled interface, and follows selected and predesignated runs through continued editing. It also documents how few weights some successful edits change and checks whether the editor’s explanations match its actions (§§3–6, Appendix D).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Selected models gained substantial test accuracy | GSM8K rose from 11.8% unedited to 61.0% with ordered editing from the original model and 62.2% after continued editing. MATH500 rose from 7.0% to 24.7% and 32.2%, respectively. The authors report that 22 of 24 selected checkpoints significantly beat the unedited model under a paired bootstrap with Bonferroni correction. | §3, Table 1, Appendix B |
| A small edit could produce a large gain | An ordered-interface checkpoint reached 24.2% MATH500 test accuracy versus 7.0% unedited, changing only 16 values in the first linear-attention layer’s `A_log`. | §3, Appendix D Table 9 |
| Random search was competitive under the shuffled interface | With matched valid-edit budgets, the reported GSM8K test scores were 32.8% for random search and 31.9% for Self-WSP. On MATH500, matched-budget random search found no improvement, while the Self-WSP checkpoint in that comparison scored 17.3%. The frozen editor showed no clear average disadvantage on shuffled validation runs. | §5, Fig. 6, Appendix C |
| The interface strongly influenced edit targets | Layer 0 received 62% of valid edits under the ordered interface and 2.9% under the shuffled interface. The explanation for one high-scoring GSM8K proposal also contradicted the operation it performed. | §4, Figs. 3–4 |
| Predesignated runs gained less than selected runs | Ordered runs started from the original model and designated in advance gained an average of 24.7 percentage points on GSM8K and 7.7 on MATH500 at their final round. In the eight-round MATH continuation setting, validation accuracy was unchanged on average across all 12 runs. | §4, §6, Fig. 8, Appendix B |

The held-out tests establish that some model-proposed edits improve this checkpoint’s math benchmark scores under supervised selection. The authors report that almost every newly correct answer was on a question the original model had failed to finish, which supports their interpretation that reduced repetition accounts for much of the gain (§7). Broader reasoning gains and reliable improvement in the model’s editing ability remain unestablished.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** The study covers one small model and two math benchmarks, and the baseline’s dominant failure mode is repetitive thinking. Acceptance depends on labeled practice questions. The ordered interface was designed after the shuffled results, and the study includes neither ordered-interface random search nor a reversed-catalog test. GPT-6 comparisons are indicative because tools, feedback, and budgets differ (Limitations).
- **Analyst inference.** The interfaces change catalog presentation, tensor selection, practice batch size, and acceptance rules together, so the design cannot isolate which change drives the ordered interface’s accuracy gains. Continued runs also start from selected checkpoints and switch interfaces, which limits what their best scores reveal about the effect of additional editing rounds alone (§§2, 4, 6).

## Open research questions

1. **How much does catalog position drive the layer-0 preference?** The interfaces vary several factors together. Reverse or permute the layer order while holding all other settings fixed, then compare first-proposal targets and final test accuracy (§4, Limitations).
2. **Does Self-WSP outperform random search under the ordered interface?** The strongest results use that interface, while the reported matched-budget random comparison uses the shuffled protocol. Compare both editors under the ordered protocol with matched valid-edit counts and report both selected and predesignated runs (§5, Limitations).
3. **Do the edits improve reasoning when repetition is rare?** The study evaluates a repetition-prone model, and most newly correct answers were previously unfinished. Repeat the experiment on a model that usually finishes, and report both answer accuracy and completion rates (§7, Limitations).
4. **Do accepted edits make the editor better at proposing useful changes?** The frozen-editor comparison is confined to the shuffled interface. Compare frozen and updated editors at the same solver checkpoints under the ordered protocol, using identical feedback and proposal budgets (§§5–6).
