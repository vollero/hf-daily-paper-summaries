# Fast-JEPA: Advancing the Success-Latency Frontier for Latent World Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.25927) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25927)


## One-sentence takeaway

Fast-JEPA uses intermediate latent predictions to build a plan and scores complete alternatives by their predicted endpoints, achieving 96.50% mean success at 10.88 ms mean planning latency across four visual-control tasks, with 2.41× faster planning than INTACT (§5.1, Table 1).

## Problem and motivation

Latent world-model controllers compare candidate action sequences online. Recursive scoring predicts intermediate states for every candidate in every search round, consuming time that matters when the environment changes during planning or when planning and execution share a deadline. The authors identify a gap in how planners allocate prediction compute. Intermediate outcomes can guide action choices while a plan is being built, whereas a terminal visual-goal objective needs only the endpoint once a candidate is complete (§§1, 3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Fast-JEPA planner.** A learned action generator builds a goal-directed proposal chunk by chunk. One shared world model predicts intermediate outcomes from the current observed latent and the chosen action prefix. Proposal-centered cross-entropy search then scores complete candidates in batches using squared latent distance to the goal. The original proposal remains eligible for selection. The contribution is the use of a shared, observation-anchored predictor for both sequential proposal construction and direct terminal scoring (§§3.2–3.5, Fig. 2).
- **DynaGoal benchmark.** Shift changes targets or geometry, Deadline budgets planning and execution jointly, and Delay controls the interval between observation and plan execution. All three reuse models trained on the original tasks without fine-tuning (§4, Appendices B.2 and E).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Higher mean success and lower planning latency than INTACT | Across PushT, Reacher, Cube, and TwoRoom, Fast-JEPA reached 96.50% at 10.88 ms versus 95.58% at 26.19 ms for INTACT, a 2.41× planning speedup. The comparison used 100 paired episodes per task and checkpoint, three training seeds, and warmed batch-one timing on an RTX PRO 6000. Fast-LeWM + SC led Fast-JEPA on PushT alone, 92.00% versus 90.00%. | §5.1, Table 1, Fig. 1, Appendix B.1 |
| Higher success under changing conditions and deadlines | Across the four tasks without fine-tuning, Fast-JEPA reached 73.83% mean Shift success versus INTACT’s 56.25%, and 87.67% mean Deadline success versus 75.33%. Fast-JEPA averaged 13.42 ms and 13.75 ms planning latency, respectively. | §5.2, Tables 2–3, Appendix B.2 |
| Delay sensitivity varied by task | At imposed total response delays of 0, 300, 500, and 800 ms, Fast-JEPA led evaluated alternatives at every tested delay on PushT, Reacher, and TwoRoom. Its Reacher and Cube success fell sharply as delay increased. The protocol used 100 episodes per task at each delay. | §5.2, Fig. 4, Appendix E |
| The proposal mattered more than refinement in the matched ablation | Across the four original tasks, removing the proposal at the same search budget reduced mean success from 96.33% to 68.17%. Proposal-only execution reached 93.83% at 5.53 ms, compared with 96.33% at 10.78 ms for full Fast-JEPA in this ablation. | §5.3, Table 4, Appendix C |
| Direct scoring reduced candidate evaluation time | In the matched-budget four-task comparison, direct versus recursive scoring yielded 96.33% versus 96.08% success, 10.78 versus 20.54 ms complete planning latency, and 2.32 versus 11.16 ms scoring time. | §5.5, Table 5, Appendix C |
| Low latency persisted across the tested search grid | Across 32 configurations per task using seed-0 checkpoints and fixed cases, Fast-JEPA took approximately 9.7–16.8 ms versus 71.8–197.2 ms for INTACT Guarded A. Their success ranges overlapped on PushT, Reacher, and Cube. | §5.4, Fig. 5, Appendix D.1 |

The results support an improved observed success–latency trade-off among the tested configurations. The matched scoring comparison ties much of the measured latency reduction to candidate evaluation under the terminal objective. The evidence leaves statistical separation of the close success rates, hard per-call latency guarantees, and performance on other hardware or physical robots unresolved.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** DynaGoal’s out-of-distribution scope covers changed control conditions and execution constraints. Visual-domain and episode disjointness were outside that scope (Appendix B.2).
- **Author-stated.** The search-budget envelope selects and reports configurations on the same fixed cases using one training seed. Mean-latency budgets provide no per-call deadline guarantee (Appendix D.1).
- **Author-stated.** Retaining the proposal bounds the selected plan’s predicted terminal cost by the proposal’s cost. Realized success can still worsen (§3.4, Appendix A).
- **Analyst inference.** The proposal ablation changes initialization, sampling spread, and proposal retention together, so it cannot identify which change drives the success gap. The reported scoring comparison also leaves open how direct terminal prediction fares when intermediate trajectory constraints determine success (§5.5, Appendix C).

## Open research questions

1. **Which parts of the proposal mechanism drive the gain?** The broad-search comparison changes the center, initial spread, and retention together. Vary each independently with fixed checkpoints, cases, and candidate budgets (Appendix C).
2. **How much intermediate prediction is needed for trajectory constraints?** The current scoring objective uses terminal latent distance. Compare endpoint-only, sparse intermediate, and recursive scoring on matched tasks that require both goal reaching and waypoint or obstacle compliance (§§3.1, 3.4).
3. **Does the latency advantage hold under deployment deadlines?** Local timing uses one GPU and the search envelopes use mean latency. Measure tail latency, deadline misses, and task success across devices and under concurrent workloads (§5, Appendix D.1).
