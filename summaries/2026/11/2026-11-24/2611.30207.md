# What Makes Good Data for Training Coding Agents? A Systematic Study on SWE-Gym

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.30207) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30207)


## One-sentence takeaway

Training Qwen3.5-9B with GRPO on audited SWE-Gym tasks improved held-out pass@1 by 2.4 percentage points on SWE-bench Verified-397 and 2.7 on SWE-bench Pro v2 versus original tasks at equal size and matched difficulty, while filtering out never-solved tasks yielded no average gain over the original set (§§5.1, 5.4).

## Problem and motivation

Hidden tests serve as reward labels for coding agents. Checking that the reference fix passes and the original code fails leaves two errors undetected: tests can reject valid alternatives or accept incorrect patches. The authors identify a gap in execution-backed measurement of both errors in real training data and in controlled comparisons of their effects on RL (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Assertion-level audit.** Two LLM families inspect individual fail-to-pass assertions. A false-negative verdict requires an executed alternative fix that the test rejects and at least one family considers valid. A false-positive verdict requires both families to judge a passing patch incorrect and an executed counterexample to show a regression (§§3, 4.1–4.2).
- **Controlled training comparison.** The authors trace flawed tasks into GRPO groups with varying rewards, then compare original data, audited data, audited data with added regression tests, and original data filtered by base-model pass rate (§§5.1–5.5).
- **Reusable release.** The authors report releasing SWE-Gym-Verified with 1,087 tasks, per-task verdicts and execution evidence, four training sets, held-out attempt outcomes, and the audit pipeline (§4.3, Appendix A).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| False negatives concentrate among never-solved tasks | 1,091 of 2,001 tasks whose assertions were judged received a false-negative verdict. In Orig, 74% of tasks with zero passes in eight base-model samples were classified as false-negative-flawed, versus 20% of tasks with one to seven passes. | §5.2, Fig. 1c, Appendix C |
| A pass-rate filter retains flawed tasks that contribute to training | Sometimes-solved false-negative-flawed tasks accounted for 19.5% of gradient-carrying groups in Orig and 19.9% in Orig+Filter. All-zero reward groups fell from 20.7% to 8.3% with filtering. | §5.3, Fig. 3, Appendix F |
| Audited data improved held-out scores | Clean exceeded Orig by 2.4 pp on Verified-397 and 2.7 pp on Pro v2 in mean pass@1 over three seeds, using dev-selected checkpoints and difficulty-matched sets of 512 tasks each. Orig+Filter scored 1.1 pp above Orig on Verified-397 and 2.1 pp below it on Pro. | §§5.1, 5.4, Table 2, Fig. 4 |
| Original tests accept demonstrably incorrect patches | Of 1,854 distinct passing patches sampled from the base model on Clean tasks, 26.1% met the regression-based false-positive rule and 37.3% were undetermined. | §5.2, Fig. 1c, Appendices B–C |
| Partial hardening lowered rewards with no detectable held-out change | New tests on 143 of 512 Clean+FP tasks caught 25.3% of false positives in a sampled set of passing Clean training patches. Reward on hardened tasks fell by about 13 pp versus Clean, close to the drop from regrading Clean’s patches. Mean held-out pass@1 differences over three seeds were −0.1 pp on Verified-397 and +1.1 pp on Pro v2. | §5.5, Fig. 5, Table 2, Appendix F |

The results support a benefit for the combined audit and curation procedure in this GRPO setup. The comparison leaves the effects of statement repairs, task replacement, and retaining difficult tasks with attainable rewards unresolved. The pass-rate filter’s average result was sensitive to one seed. The hardening experiment leaves policy equivalence and differences in semantic correctness unresolved because the held-out tests can themselves accept incorrect patches (§§5.4–5.5, Appendix F).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope.** The study uses one base model, scaffold, RL recipe, and training source, with three seeds per set and 80 training steps. Difficulty depends on the base model and its sampling budget (§6).
- **Author-stated audit limits.** Execution demonstrates test behavior, while LLMs still judge whether an alternative fix satisfies the issue. Two authors independently reviewed a sample of 105 items. The systematic false-negative audit focuses on fail-to-pass assertions, and confirmed false positives cover regressions, leaving incomplete fixes undercounted (§§3–4, 6, Appendix C).
- **Author-stated evaluation assumption.** Both held-out benchmarks can contain reward-label errors. The authors assume these affect all training sets alike (§6).
- **Analyst inference.** Matching on base-model pass rates only approximates matching on underlying difficulty. Clean is measured using final statements and Orig using original statements, and the audit shows that test alignment itself affects the measured pass rate (§§5.1–5.2, Appendix D).

## Open research questions

- **When does repairing a task beat replacing it?** Clean combines repairs and replacements. Compare equal-size, difficulty-matched sets that differ only in whether repairable tasks are kept with revised statements or replaced (§5.4, §6).
- **Does broader false-positive coverage improve patch correctness?** The added tests covered only part of Clean, and held-out pass@1 uses the original benchmark tests. Vary training-test coverage and independently review held-out passing patches for incomplete fixes and regressions (§5.5, Appendix F).
- **Can a cheaper audit recover the same training gains?** Single-family ratings often agreed with the final audit verdict, but the paper did not train on data curated that way. Compare single-family screening and the full execution-backed audit under matched training conditions and record audit cost alongside held-out pass@1 (§6, Appendix C).
- **Do the gains transfer to other training setups?** The study used one model, scaffold, recipe, and task source. Repeat the original-versus-audited comparison on a second model and task collection while matching data size and measured difficulty (§§6–7).
