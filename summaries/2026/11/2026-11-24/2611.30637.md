# SWEEP-Bench: Do Embodied Policies Act on the Speed They See?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.30637) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30637)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across 80 simulated conveyor tasks at 13 speeds, six of eight robot policies performed best at intermediate speeds, visual history substantially improved three models, and explicit belt-speed input improved all four models tested with it.

## Problem and motivation

A robot manipulating a moving object must adjust where and when it makes contact. The authors identify a gap in existing benchmarks: broad task suites seldom report success at enough speeds to reveal timing failures, while dense speed sweeps typically cover few tasks. SWEEP-Bench tests whether policies adapt their actions to visible motion under paired scenes and uses scripted experts to check task feasibility at representative speeds (§§1–3, Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Benchmark and protocol.** SWEEP-Bench pairs randomized scenes across 13 constant speeds from 0.10 to 0.40 m/s for 80 tasks in 16 skill categories. Every task has a static counterpart and an expert feasibility check at four speeds. A separate protocol tests within-episode acceleration on 40 tasks (§3, Appendix A).
- **Policy comparison.** The authors fine-tune six vision-language-action models and two world-action models with either the current frame or two additional past frames. They assess speed-wise success, retest four models trained on narrower speed bands, and hand-label timing failures (§§4.1–4.2).
- **Speed estimation and conditioning.** The authors relate history gains to linear speed probes, compare policies given ground-truth or optical-flow-estimated speed, and test selected configurations on a physical conveyor (§§4.2–4.3, Appendices E–G).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Six models peaked at intermediate speeds | Across 80 tasks and 13 speeds, peak scores for both input variants of these six models ranged from +3.2 to +22.9 percentage points per 0.1 m/s, with all 95% task-bootstrap intervals above zero. | §4.2, Fig. 3, Table S8 |
| Timing errors tracked speed, and best performance shifted with training bands | Across the six peaked past-frame models, 48% of labeled episodes at 0.10 m/s failed from acting early and 69% at 0.40 m/s failed from acting late. On the 20-task subset, all 12 band-trained policies achieved their highest success within their training band. | §4.2, Figs. 4–5, Appendix D.7 |
| History gains varied by model and tracked speed decodability | Across 80 tasks and 13 speeds, adding two past frames raised mean success by 26.4 points for Fast-WAM, 9.6 for Cosmos Policy, and 39.5 for DynamicVLA. The other five changed by −5.6 to +0.9 points. On held-out tasks with the arm stationary, the three gainers had action-module probe R² of 0.86–0.99, while the others reached at most 0.58 across stages. | §4.2, Table 2, Fig. 6, Appendix E |
| Explicit speed improved all four tested models | On 20 tasks at seven speeds, ground-truth speed added 34, 20, 12, and 15 success points over past frames alone for π₀.₅, Fast-WAM, VLA-JEPA, and DynamicVLA, respectively. At four adapter-test speeds, estimated speed recovered 98%, 98%, 105%, and 83% of the corresponding gains. | §4.2, Fig. 7, Appendix F |
| Response to acceleration varied by model | On 40 simulation tasks, acceleration-trained Fast-WAM gained 14.4 success points at +0.05 m/s² relative to its own zero-acceleration baseline. After the authors’ adjustment for local belt speed, the gain was 7.9 points (95% CI 3.3–12.7). The adjusted change was negative for π₀.₅ and indistinguishable from zero for DynamicVLA and VLA-JEPA. | §4.2, Fig. 8, Appendix D.6 |
| Explicit speed helped on hardware | Across three tasks at four speeds from 0.05 to 0.20 m/s, past-frame π₀.₅ scored 31 without speed, 80 with the set speed, and 66 with an estimate (both speed comparisons p < 0.001). These scores award partial credit for contact. The overall history-only gains for π₀.₅ and Fast-WAM were statistically inconclusive. | §4.3, Fig. 10, Appendix G |

The authors interpret the timing labels and band-training results as evidence that many policies default to timing suited to the demonstration speeds. The probe results establish an association between speed decodability and history gains. Causal use of the probed features and the independent effect of multi-frame pretraining remain unresolved. The explicit-input comparisons establish a benefit for the tested speed-conditioned policies in their respective settings.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Most configurations had one fine-tuning run. The extra π₀.₅ seeds cover a limited subset, and the main intervals capture task variation. Architecture and training data differ across models, limiting attribution to multi-frame pretraining. Pausing simulation during inference removes the effect of latency (§5, Appendices B–C).
- **Author-stated.** The adapter was evaluated with one simulation belt texture. Its simulation runs also re-plan when the first estimate arrives, while the ground-truth and no-speed runs keep the original schedule. Removing early re-planning reduced π₀.₅’s recovery of the ground-truth speed gain from 98% to about 77% on the four-speed subset (Appendix F).
- **Analyst inference.** The static comparison is a learnability check because initial poses, some success conditions, and training step counts differ. The hardware experiment supports a speed-input benefit for π₀.₅ on one setup, with broader transfer across hardware, motion settings, and tasks still uncertain (Appendices A.5 and D.3, §4.3).

## Open research questions

1. **Does multi-frame pretraining cause the history gains?** The models differ in architecture and training data. Pretrain matched versions of one architecture on the same data with single-frame or multi-frame inputs, then fine-tune and evaluate across several seeds (§5, Appendix B).
2. **How much of the estimated-speed benefit comes from the estimate itself?** The adapter and its controls use different initial re-planning schedules. Match the schedules across ground-truth, estimated-speed, and no-speed conditions, then repeat with unseen belt textures (Appendix F).
3. **Can policies adapt to changing speed using explicit motion inputs?** The acceleration study compares training regimes, while speed conditioning is tested at constant speeds. Compare instantaneous speed and speed-plus-acceleration inputs on paired accelerating episodes with held-out motion profiles (§4.2, Appendix D.6).
