# Context-Grounded Skill Internalization for Vision-Language Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.30075) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30075)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across three benchmarks and two Qwen3.5 model sizes, masking detector-selected sentences during full-parameter fine-tuning reduced the rate of skill-free test trajectories with unsupported references by 72.5–94.6% relative to simply dropping the skill, with no statistically significant difference in task success (§5.2, Table 2, Appendix E).

## Problem and motivation

A common way to internalize an agent skill is to collect successful demonstrations with the skill in context, then fine-tune on those demonstrations with the skill removed. The training targets can still cite the removed guidance, teaching the deployed agent to attribute its actions to a source it cannot see. The authors call this **context misalignment (CoMi)** and argue that it makes agent behavior harder to audit. They identify a gap in measuring this failure in both demonstrations and deployed agents (§§1–3, Figure 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Define and measure CoMi.** Paired audits of the same demonstration with and without the skill identify references that lose support specifically because of removal. At deployment, the audit checks for any unsupported reference in the actual skill-free context (§3.2, Appendix B).
- **Detect affected sentences.** A frozen backbone scores each sentence under both contexts. A logistic classifier combines token likelihood gaps with pooled skill-free hidden states and is calibrated separately for each benchmark and model (§§4.1–4.3, Figure 2).
- **Correct supervision before fine-tuning.** CGSI (Mask) zeros the loss on selected sentence tokens while retaining the text in teacher-forced prefixes. CGSI (Rewrite) uses a VLM to make checked edits and rebuilds later prefixes from the edited text. Both variants train without the skill (§§4.4–4.6, Appendix C).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Skill removal makes some demonstration references unsupported | 18.3–75.4% of successful teacher trajectories contained at least one reference that lost support across ALFWorld, WebShop, and ARC-AGI-1 with Qwen3.5-4B and 9B. After full-parameter skill-dropped SFT, 12.2–46.0% of skill-free test trajectories contained an unsupported reference. | §§3.4, 5.2, Tables 1–2 |
| Targeted masking lowers deployment CoMi | Against full-parameter skill-dropped SFT, CGSI (Mask) reduced trajectory-level CoMi by 72.5–94.6% across all six benchmark–model settings. On WebShop-4B, CoMi fell from 46.0% to 3.4% and task success went from 39.2% to 41.6%. The authors found no significant success difference in any of the six settings. | §5.2, Table 2, Appendix E |
| A similar amount of random masking failed to reproduce the gain | On full-parameter WebShop-4B, random reasoning-sentence masking covered 1.07% of target tokens and left 49.4% deployment CoMi. Detector-guided masking covered 1.21% and left 3.4%. | §5.2, Appendix D, Table S7 |
| Detector-guided correction cuts API spending | For 2,281 ARC-9B demonstrations, estimated API processing costs were $1,378 for full VLM audit plus rewriting, $165 for CGSI (Rewrite), and $0 for CGSI (Mask), excluding detector development. The one-time detector labeling cost was $464. | §5.5, Table 5, Appendix G |
| Training-time comparisons favor CGSI on ALFWorld-4B | CGSI (Mask) reached 85.04% success in about 1.25 training hours, compared with 81.02% for Skill0 in 19.1 hours and 88.32% for Skill-SD in 9.26 hours. Training times exclude demonstration generation and data processing. | §1, §5.6, Table 4, Appendix G |
| Task-reward RL leaves substantial CoMi after skill-dropped SFT | After 60 skill-free GRPO updates in the ALFWorld and WebShop experiments, skill-dropped SFT models retained 69–86% of their pre-RL CoMi, ending at 8.4–35.2%. Models initialized with corrected SFT supervision ended at 0.4–5.2%. | §5.7, Figure 3, Appendix E |

The shared-demonstration comparisons and the WebShop random-mask control support targeted masking as the explanation for the measured CoMi reduction. The success tests resolve differences only at the precision of these test sets. CoMi measures explicit source attribution, so lower rates establish fewer unsupported references under the audit. Causal faithfulness of the reasoning and compliance with instructions that conflict with an internalized skill remain open (§§2, 3.2, 5.2, Appendices A and E).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ limitations.** Compute allowed one training run per configuration, leaving run-to-run variation unmeasured. The same VLM annotated training data and audited deployment outputs, creating a risk of shared auditor bias. Human checks of sampled positives and a second-model review of selected negatives address part of that risk (Limitations, Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference.** The independent negative re-audit covered full-parameter skill-dropped SFT and CGSI (Mask) on ALFWorld-4B and WebShop-4B, so miss rates elsewhere remain less directly validated. One rollout per test task also leaves within-task rollout variability unmeasured (§5.1, Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference.** The online training comparisons use only ALFWorld-4B, and the Skill-SD implementation uses fixed skills in place of its original dynamic skill bank. The reported timing comparison therefore applies to these implementations and excludes upstream processing (§5.6, Table 4, Appendices E and G).

## Open research questions

1. **Can a detector transfer across skills, benchmarks, or backbones?** The paper calibrates each benchmark–model pair separately and reports reuse on a larger pool within WebShop. A next experiment could freeze a source detector and compare zero-shot transfer, threshold-only recalibration, and full refitting on a held-out skill or benchmark (§4.3, Appendix E).
2. **Does reducing CoMi improve compliance when visible instructions conflict with the internalized skill?** The paper motivates this risk but measures task success and unsupported references in skill-free contexts. A next experiment could vary whether a visible instruction agrees with or overrides the learned procedure and score action compliance alongside CoMi (§2, §5.1).
3. **How stable are the success and CoMi differences across training runs?** The reported intervals reflect test-task sampling. A next experiment could repeat skill-dropped SFT and CGSI (Mask) across independent training seeds and evaluate multiple rollouts per task (Limitations, Appendix E).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the reported direction and magnitude of the main comparisons within the evaluated settings. The supplied text lacks the full numerical rows for several captioned tables, including the per-setting detector results and calibration thresholds in Tables 3 and S4 and the paired interval bounds in Appendix E. Independent-run variance and public availability of code, checkpoints, detector weights, or trajectories are also unestablished.
