# GameAnything: From Any Inspirations to Playable Game Worlds

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.29238) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29238)


## One-sentence takeaway

GameAnything pairs coding agents with gameplay-based feedback to adapt multimodal references into existing games, and its benchmark finds that implementing playable interactions and precise edits remains difficult even when agents capture the requested content (§§4, 6.2).

## Problem and motivation

Game creators often start with footage, images, or stories. Turning that material into a playable extension requires identifying what can transfer, expressing it through the host game’s mechanics, and preserving existing functionality. The authors argue that prior benchmarks emphasize explicit specifications and predefined tests, leaving cross-world adaptation and the discoverability of new content during ordinary play underexamined (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **GameAnything platform.** A coding agent builds or revises a mod, and a separate critic checks gameplay evidence against the requirements. A persistent record tracks verified progress, unresolved issues, and regressions. The platform retrieves relevant lessons from prior tasks within a fixed context budget and supports user feedback anchored to gameplay frames (§4).
- **GameAnything-Bench.** Construction tasks ask agents to adapt external references into an existing game. Editing tasks start from validated checkpoints and score both the requested change and preservation of unrelated content. The evaluator combines runtime probes, action replay, exploratory playtesting, and visual rubrics (§5).

The results support comparisons among the tested configurations on these tasks and a visual-fidelity benefit from references in the matched Sonnet test. The refinement comparison bundles additional development time, critic feedback, and retrieved experience, so the contribution of each remains unresolved. Human agreement covers the scored Construction dimensions other than Host Fidelity, and the reported error shows that individual judgments can still differ (§§7.1, 16.1).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported.** Part of the early Construction refinement used shorter rounds without experience memory. Native comparisons use each model’s default harness and settings, so the rankings describe complete configurations. Repeated development runs also showed enough variability for the authors to caution against overinterpreting small gaps (§§6.1, 7.1, 12.1, 15.3).
- **Author-reported.** Full evaluation adds both exploratory playtesting and adjudication, so their individual effects are unresolved. The cross-family judge comparison rescored saved evidence and therefore measures scoring robustness on that evidence (§§16.2, 16.4).
- **Analyst inference.** Curating reference–game pairs for feasibility and starting Editing from validated checkpoints drawn mainly from Claude Fable 5.2 may limit transfer to poorly matched references, arbitrary player mods, or edits starting from broken projects (§§5.1, 10.3). The modality rankings also mix different task and host-game distributions, so they cannot isolate the effect of input format (§7.2, Table A7).

## Open research questions

1. **How much do critic feedback and experience memory each contribute?** The reported refinement runs bundle both with extra development time. A matched-budget factorial experiment could toggle feedback and memory independently on the same tasks and starting submissions (§§6.1, 7.1).
2. **Do the apparent modality strengths hold when the creative goal and host game are fixed?** The reported modality comparisons use different task groups. A paired experiment could present equivalent text, image, video, and hybrid references for the same adaptation and score them against a shared rubric (§7.2, Table A7).
3. **How well does the evaluator detect regressions that surface later in play?** The reported playtest-budget comparison covers the tested submissions, and the human agreement study omits Host Fidelity. A follow-up could use mods with known late-triggering regressions and compare detection by agents and human players across playtest budgets (§§16.1, 16.3).
