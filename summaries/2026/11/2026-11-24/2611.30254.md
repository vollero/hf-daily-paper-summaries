# Innata-0: A Native Embodied World System with Model-Agent Co-Design

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.30254) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30254)


## One-sentence takeaway

Innata-0 trains a multimodal robot policy on the same task context its agent maintains at runtime, and the supplied results report that online recovery adds successes on some RoboCasa365 composite tasks.

## Problem and motivation

Long-horizon manipulation requires a robot to track progress from partial observations, detect when an action has failed, and recover while the physical scene continues to change. The authors argue that standalone policies struggle to sustain this loop and that loosely coupling a planner to a controller can leave the planner’s representation of progress misaligned with the controller’s training and capabilities (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Shared training and execution interface.** The policy conditions on the full instruction, active subtask, and completed-subtask history. Training annotations supply these fields, and the agent updates them from verified physical outcomes at runtime (§§1, 3.1.4, 4.1).
- **Multimodal world-action model.** A three-tower Mixture-of-Transformers combines vision–language understanding, video prediction, and joint action–tactile generation. Training progresses from embodied video and action-conditioned prediction to action learning, robot-state conditioning, and tactile supervision (§§3.1, 3.3).
- **Verification, recovery, and experience reuse.** The agent maintains hierarchical memory, reviews observed state transitions, and invokes bounded tools when it verifies a deviation. Validated experience informs versioned verification and recovery skills, while selected corrective and post-recovery segments enter subsequent policy training (§§4.1–4.3, 3.3.3).
- **Shared data preparation.** The pipeline annotates heterogeneous sources once, then prepares task-specific supervision. It defines Hand32 and Ego128 for hand and egocentric motion and aligns heterogeneous tactile measurements to regional contact proxies (§§2.1–2.6, Tables 10.4 and 10.7).

The authors report competitive results on the compared benchmarks and task-dependent additions to RoboCasa365 success from online recovery. The available evidence cannot isolate the contributions of structured conditioning, tactile supervision, and rollout reuse, or establish how well recovery transfers to physical robots. The throughput result applies to the historical DROID configuration tested.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope.** Deployment recovery works from the live state with a fixed skill version. Failure replay and skill revision occur during experience accumulation (§4.3). Historical infrastructure measurements omit tactile tokens and span different workloads. The authors report that grouped attention passed operator checks but missed update-level numerical acceptance (§§12.1, 12.5).
- **Analyst inference.** Recovery gains vary by task, and the missing episode counts and uncertainty estimates prevent judging how stable those gains are across configurations (§13.2). Bench2Dex appears both as an action-learning source and as an evaluation asset. The supplied text leaves the train/evaluation split relationship unclear (§5, Table 10.6).

## Open research questions

1. **How much does training–runtime alignment improve long-horizon success?** The supplied results leave its effect unresolved. Compare policies trained with and without active-subtask and completed-history conditioning under the same runtime planner and rollout budget.
2. **Do evolved skills transfer to unfamiliar layouts and failure modes?** The paper describes held-out validation, but the excerpt omits generation-by-generation results. Compare frozen initial and evolved skill versions on fresh configurations, measuring false interventions, recovery success, and final task success.
3. **When does tactile input improve closed-loop control?** The excerpt names tactile evaluations but omits their results. Compare matched policies with and without tactile inputs on contact-sensitive tasks under varied visibility and sensor coverage.

## Confidence and missing information

**Medium.** The supplied text describes the architecture and protocols in detail, but the source was truncated in the middle of Table 5 and coverage is incomplete. Missing material includes most evaluation tables, baseline scores and episode counts, detailed RoboDojo-Sim and tactile results, component ablations, physical-robot evaluation evidence, and availability details for checkpoints, code, and curated data.
