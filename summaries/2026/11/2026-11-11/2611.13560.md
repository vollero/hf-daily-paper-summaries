# Are Dense Rewards Worth Their Tokens in GRPO?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-11.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.13560) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13560)


## One-sentence takeaway
In the tested mathematical-reasoning settings, accounting for generated tokens and GPU time reverses Monte-Carlo dense rewards’ apparent advantage over GRPO, while prompt selection delivers small, replicated gains that largely disappear with longer training or baseline retuning (§§3–4).

## Problem and motivation
Outcome-only GRPO assigns the same credit to every token in a completion, motivating dense rewards that distinguish useful intermediate steps from mistakes. However, comparing methods per update ignores the continuations and computation required to estimate those rewards. Gradient alignment tests also assess local update directions rather than sustained training performance (§§1–2).

The authors ask whether these interventions improve accuracy when their full training costs are counted, and whether cheaper prompt selection offers a better use of compute.

## Approach and contributions
- **Matched-compute evaluation protocol:** Count all generated tokens, including discarded continuations and samples, and separately measure training GPU-hours. Use paired seeds, preregistered contrasts, equivalence tests, and controls for prompt-stream traversal, learning rate, and verifier behavior (§2.3).
- **Controlled empirical case studies:** Compare Monte-Carlo rewards, learned critics, sample-reuse adaptations, and existing prompt-selection rules. The contribution is their evaluation and interpretation; the authors explicitly do not claim a new prompt-selection rule (§§3–5).
- **Fixed-batch characterization:** Establish exactly when token-level credit produces an update equivalent to reweighting whole completions, accounting for the score map’s kernel. This describes raw gradients at a fixed policy and batch, not eventual training gains (Appendix I, Theorem 1).
- **Reported research artifacts:** The authors report releasing the trainer, configurations, preregistrations, analysis code, and run records; these assets were not independently inspected (Reproducibility and Responsible Reporting).

## Evidence and main results

Accuracy differences below are percentage points against outcome-only GRPO; brackets denote reported 95% intervals.

Retuned outcome-only baselines finish within **0.5 points in mean accuracy** of the default-rate filter on both replicated models. | §4.3; Figures 5–6 |

These results support compute accounting as a material evaluation choice and reject substantial benefits for the tested dense-reward recipes. They do **not** establish that every dense reward loses: the cheaper equal-time Monte-Carlo comparison remains inconclusive, and the baseline-form critic has a small matched-token gain that disappears at matched time (§3.3). Prompt-selection gains are conditional on model, pool, horizon, and baseline tuning.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Matched-compute studies focus on numeric-answer mathematics and models up to 4B parameters; dense rewards were not tested on the Llama models. No prompt-selection gain was demonstrated outside Qwen (Appendix B.1).
- **Recipe dependence:** Reuse variants adapt published methods rather than faithfully reproduce them. Other segmentations, estimators, reward models, and tuned recipes could change the cost trade-off (§5; Appendix B.1).
- **Truncation and optimization:** Many initial completions hit the short generation limit. Learning rates and schedules were not exhaustively tuned per configuration; larger limits reduce the Monte-Carlo deficit (Appendices A–B).
- **Statistical qualifications:** Some studies have few seeds; multiplicity correction applies within studies. Preregistrations were internal files whose timing rests on filesystem records. Several equal-time findings are exploratory (Appendices B.1, C.1).
- **Verifier and transfer risks:** Default-verifier formatting drift complicates some strict-verifier transfer comparisons; contamination was not tested (Appendix B.1).
- **Analyst inference:** The evidence supports a conditional warning about these implementations, rather than a general rejection of process supervision, especially for larger models, other tasks, or inference-time search.

## Open research questions
1. **Can faithfully implemented dense-reward methods win at equal time?** Published recipes were adapted here. Next experiment: reproduce their native segmentation, reuse, and regularization while counting all training costs (Appendix B.1).
2. **Does the prompt-difficulty rule generalize beyond Qwen?** Its predicted Llama-1B gain failed. Next experiment: preregister matched-time tests across additional families, using pilot training reward to select difficulty settings (Appendix B.1).
3. **Does prompt selection retain value after symmetric tuning?** Existing retuning nearly closes the gap, but schedules were not tuned for both methods. Next experiment: allocate equal tuning budgets and compare accuracy-versus-time curves (§4.3; Appendix B.1).
4. **Can gradient diagnostics predict sustained gains?** Initial-policy efficiency extrapolation is systematically optimistic. Next experiment: measure diagnostics across checkpoints and test predictions on independent models and training runs (§3.3).
