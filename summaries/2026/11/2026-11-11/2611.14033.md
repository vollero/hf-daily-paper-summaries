# Few-Step Streaming Video Generation via Condition Shifting

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-11.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.14033) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14033)


## One-sentence takeaway
APEX adapts condition shifting to streaming video, removing DMD’s separate fake estimator to reduce measured training costs and improve instruction following, while retaining a frozen teacher and showing motion and audiovisual trade-offs (§§3–4).

## Problem and motivation
Few-step streaming generation must repeatedly produce new video blocks while preserving appearance, history, and responsiveness to changing instructions. Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD) uses an auxiliary fake-score network that adds training memory and computation and must track the changing generator. The authors investigate whether the generator can also learn this estimate within S2-Avatar’s causal replay framework (§§1–2.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Shared generator and fake estimator:** Original projected text selects generation; negated projected text selects fake-velocity estimation using the same parameters. Flow matching trains the estimator on detached, re-noised generated sequences (§3.1).
- **Integration with causal replay:** APEX applies this estimator to Self-Replay Forcing outputs. Differences between fake and frozen-teacher clean-latent predictions produce a normalized, detached generator target; perceptual regularization is retained (§§3.2–3.3).
- **Streaming evaluation and cost measurements:** The paper evaluates long-video quality, instruction response, sampling budgets, and training costs (§4).

The contribution is the streaming adaptation and integration: condition shifting and Self-Replay Forcing are explicitly inherited from earlier work (§§1, 3; Appendix A.2–A.3).

## Evidence and main results

Unless indicated otherwise, quality comparisons use 180-second outputs, three backbone evaluations per block, and the same one-step 2× spatial Refiner; StreamAV-Bench resolution is 1344×768 (§4.1).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Instruction fulfillment and aesthetics improve | Across 320 StreamAV-Bench cases, APEX versus S2-Avatar: pooled VIF **3.891 vs 3.834**, AIF **2.610 vs 2.570**, VA **0.641898 vs 0.632606**; each improves on both tracks. Higher is better. | Table 2 |
| Benchmark quality gains are mixed | On the same 320 cases, pooled VQ is **3.256 vs 3.267**, AQ **2.725 vs 2.788**, and AVSync error **1.340521 vs 1.318854 seconds**. VQ/AQ are higher-is-better; AVSync is lower-is-better. AVAlign and subject consistency also decrease. | Table 2 |
| Human preference is strongest for instruction following | In 70 paired I2V cases, instruction following has **22 wins / 47 ties / 1 loss**, giving **+30.0%** net preference. Consistency and video quality each yield **+4.3%**; motion quality **−8.6%**. | §4.2; Fig. 4; Appendix D.2 |
| Synchronization and usability do not improve | Main human study: synchronization has **69 ties and 1 loss**; both models have **62/70 usable sequences (88.6%)**, requiring instruction and synchronization MOS above 2. | §4.2; Table 3 |
| Training costs decrease | Separate matched-setting measurement against DMD: peak allocated memory **102.7→98.2 GiB/GPU (−4.4%)**; mean iteration time **35.9→32.6 seconds (−9.2%)**. | §4.3; Fig. 5 |
| Two-step instruction gains retain other trade-offs | APEX at two backbone NFE versus S2-Avatar at three, with the common Refiner, obtains **+27.1%** instruction preference in a separate 70-pair round; consistency/video quality are each **−2.9%**, synchronization/motion each **−7.1%**. | §4.3; Fig. 6; Table 4 |

These author-reported results support instruction-following gains and lower training costs in the tested setup, rather than uniform quality improvement. Reduced backbone NFE does not establish proportional end-to-end acceleration: Refiner and cache costs remain separately accounted for (Appendix D.3). Statistical significance is not reported.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Motion quality and audiovisual synchronization remain trade-offs (§5). The theory does not establish long-horizon stability or an exact KL gradient for the joint video trajectory (Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference—confounded ablation:** Shared versus separate optimizer states also changes inference NFE, preventing isolation of optimizer-state effects (Table 4; Appendix D.2).
- **Analyst inference—comparison limits:** Additional human rounds compare variants against a common reference, not directly against one another. Several preference margins reflect few net wins, and uncertainty intervals are unavailable (Appendix D.2).
- **Analyst inference—generalization:** Evidence comes from one inherited backbone/pipeline and 180-second evaluations. Training excludes large or complex camera motion, limiting support for that setting (§4.1; Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference—baseline comparability:** Other models’ benchmark scores are imported from prior reports, whereas APEX and S2-Avatar are measured here (Table 1; Appendix D.1).

## Open research questions
- **Can optimizer-state sharing improve quality independently of sampling budget?** The current comparison changes both. Test shared and separate states at matched training settings and identical inference NFE.
- **Can instruction gains coexist with stronger motion and synchronization?** Both remain weaknesses. Compare fake-update schedules at fixed NFE, evaluating all human dimensions and benchmark synchronization.
- **Does quality remain stable over longer streams and camera motion?** Neither longer-duration stability nor complex-camera generalization is established. Evaluate increasing durations and camera-motion strata with time-resolved consistency and instruction scores.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly specifies the mechanism, evaluation settings, and principal outcomes, but leaves material reproducibility and uncertainty gaps:

- Model size, hardware configuration, training duration, batch size, learning rates, and update ratios.
- Exact scoring-time schedule and full perceptual-regularization configuration.
- Training-data quantities and access; code/checkpoint availability.
- Rater count, assignment, blinding, agreement, statistical uncertainty, and repeated-seed results.
- End-to-end inference latency and performance beyond the evaluated duration.
