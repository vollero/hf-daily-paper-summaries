# LEASH: Stay Safe in Agentic Robotics

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-11.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.14115) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14115)


## One-sentence takeaway
LEASH combines a shared safety policy with visual checks before tasks, actions, and irreversible operations, reducing reported harm across evaluated robot planners while leaving substantial dependence on planner reliability (§§4–6).

## Problem and motivation
Foundation-model robot controllers can execute semantically dangerous actions even along collision-free trajectories—for example, carrying liquid over electronics. The authors argue that existing safeguards and benchmarks emphasize harmful instructions or text outputs rather than hazards throughout physical execution (§§1–2).

Their identified gap is closed-loop evaluation and protection spanning unsafe goals, unsafe plans, unexpected events, scene-based injection, and forgotten safety information (§3.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **RoboRisk benchmark:** 92 simulation tasks across 19 task families and 13 physical-robot tasks covering five risk categories. Outcomes distinguish Harm, Safe Success, and Safe Incomplete (§3; §A.1).
- **LEASH safety harness:** A black-box planner and three visual safety gates share a policy. The task gate can terminate a task; action and commit gates block candidates and return control to the planner. Commit monitoring currently covers gripper opening and pouring. Jev is the default reviewer; no planner retraining is required (§4).
- **Empirical evaluation:** Comparisons across six foundation models, with different subsets evaluated in simulation and physically, plus two robot APIs in adapted simulation. The authors also document excessive caution around people in physical scenes, termed the Human-Presence Gap (§5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Default LEASH improves reported safety and success | Authors report lower HR and higher SSR for every evaluated planner/environment pair using Jev; full table cells are absent from supplied text. | §5.2; Table 2 caption |
| Strong simulation result for GPT-6 Astra | Baseline → LEASH: HR **11.23% → 0.00%**, SSR **78.99% → 80.80%**; 92 tasks, three seeds each, 276 runs per configuration. | §§5.1–5.2 |
| No observed physical harm for four planners | With LEASH, GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini 4 Argon, and Claude Opus 5.5 each achieve **0% HR and 84.62% SSR**, over 13 tasks with one run each. | §5.2 |
| Weak planners retain substantial harm | LEASH HR is **12.68%** for GPT-6 Luna in simulation and **38.46%** for Grok 4.7 physically; authors describe unstable motion and policy failures. | §5.2 |
| API outcomes show different safety–completion failures | Adapted simulation subset, 39 runs per API: Dream has no harm and **28.2% SSR**; RoboAPI has **59.0% HR and 0.0% SSR**. These setups are not directly comparable to the main evaluation. | §§5.1–5.2; Table 2 caption |
| Measured overhead is small in the tested setting | Across GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol on RoboRisk-S, mean time rises **245.21 → 249.28 s (1.66%)**. Estimated gate cost is **$0.00522/run**, **0.66%** of approximately **$0.791/run** total. | §5.6; §C.3 |
| Human-adjacent tasks remain incomplete | Four stronger physical planners achieve no safe successes on P1 or P2, involving cleaver transport near a hand and knife handover. | §5.7; §C.4 |

HR includes both actual damage and predefined potential harm; SSR includes appropriate refusal or termination in relevant categories, so it is not simply manipulation completion (§§3.1–3.3, B.2). The evidence supports improvements for the combined harness in these settings, but does not establish universal safety, causal contributions of individual components, or reliable physical failure rates from single task repetitions.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors:** Safety depends on reviewer accuracy and planner compliance. Unsafe approvals and unstable motion remain possible; long skills contain hazards outside the two monitored commit operations (§6).
- **Authors:** No full component ablation was run because of cost; logged gate decisions substitute for intervention-based comparisons (§5.5).
- **Analyst inference:** Because baseline planners receive no policy, the comparison cannot isolate the benefit of gates from policy prompting (§B.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Action/commit gate analysis labels executed approvals but not denied candidates. Its reported accuracy therefore cannot characterize false rejections or overall candidate classification (§C.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Physical evidence is narrow: one arm setup, safe props, and one run per task. Simulation includes related object-substitution tasks, grasp assistance, and selected restarts from pre-grasped states (§§5.1, A.1–A.3, B.2).
- **Analyst inference:** The Human-Presence Gap is observational. Built-in safety restrictions are the authors’ suspected explanation, not an established mechanism (§C.4).

## Open research questions
1. **How much benefit comes from each component?** No full ablation separates policy and gates (§5.5). Next experiment: compare policy-only, gates-only, full LEASH, and individual gate removals under matched conditions.
2. **Can monitoring protect long skills against broader hazards?** Commit checks cover only opening and pouring (§6). Next experiment: introduce mid-skill hazards outside those operations and compare expanded monitoring with current LEASH.
3. **Can physical collaboration improve without increasing harm?** Human-adjacent tasks remain incomplete (§5.7). Next experiment: repeat matched scenes while varying human presence, perception quality, and handover strategy.
4. **How reliable are safety gains across deployments?** Physical repetitions and platforms are limited (§5.1). Next experiment: repeat tasks across arms, camera conditions, and unseen task families with uncertainty estimates.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The method and selected results are explicit, but supplied tables largely contain captions without their data cells.

Material gaps include complete per-model/category results, detailed task-table entries, outcome-labeling agreement, safety-metric uncertainty estimates, and confirmed code/data release availability. Reviewer timing also differs between accounts (§4.2 versus §D), and aggregate configuration counts in §5.1 are not fully enumerated in the supplied prose.
