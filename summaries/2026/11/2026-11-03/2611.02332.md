# LEANGUARD: Lean-Verified Guardrails for Tool-Using Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-03.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.02332) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02332)


## One-sentence takeaway
LeanGuard verifies tool-call admission against formal temporal policies and blocks policy-denied execution, but its protection depends on trusted evidence and specification coverage, while its proved repairs remain evaluated offline.

## Problem and motivation
Tool-using agents can issue valid tool calls that violate authorization, state, consent, or ordering requirements. These failures matter when calls change external state, especially when prompt injection steers the agent.

The authors identify a gap between runtime policy enforcement and a decision procedure proved equivalent to an independent temporal semantics, alongside mechanisms that help agents recover after rejection (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Verified admission:** A Lean 4 runtime intercepts calls and checks reviewed policies against backend snapshots and execution history. Policies combine permits, requirements, forbids, and scoped past-time temporal operators; missing or malformed evidence causes rejection. The evaluator is proved equivalent to its LeanLTL interpretation (§§2.2–2.4).
- **Feedback-guided recovery:** Denials return failed rules and a natural-language hint. The rule record comes from the policy; the hint itself is unverified (§2.5).
- **Proof-guided repair:** Lean searches a finite, ordered candidate set for argument edits satisfying an explicit intent-preservation condition and returns a kernel-checked admissibility proof. This search runs offline in the experiments (§2.6; Appendix H).
- **Failure analysis:** The paper categorizes violations as invalid invocation, state incompatibility, temporal incompatibility, authorization failure, and policy prohibition (§3, RQ1).

The authors position verified temporal admission and proved call repairs as their distinguishing contributions; the supplied text does not independently establish priority over all prior systems.

These results support enforcement of the encoded policy, rather than complete compliance with natural-language requirements. LeanGuard’s zero formal-violation rate follows from its admission design and is confirmed by replay. The DTap results do not establish comparable protection when benign writes are authorized, and offline repair results do not establish improved task completion from delivering repairs.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated boundaries:** Proofs do not validate policy translation, adapter correctness, complete interception, or conversion of dialogue into consent. The audit found incorrectly generated or bound confirmation evidence (Limitations; Appendices A and G).
- **Restricted protection:** Response-text harms remain outside tool enforcement. Unmodeled effects are blocked, potentially preventing legitimate operations (Limitations; Appendix G).
- **Evaluation scope:** Experiments use one model family, English tasks, simulated users, and only seven DTap domains. Two τ²-bench rules exceed the benchmark requirements (Limitations).
- **Sampling and scoring:** Main τ²-bench comparisons use one sampled episode per task and arm. DTap excludes unscored episodes, with different exclusion counts across arms (Appendix E).
- **Analyst inference:** Unequal DTap exclusions may affect aggregate comparisons; the supplied text does not quantify that sensitivity.
- **Analyst inference:** Repair admissibility establishes compliance with the supplied `EditOK` condition, not independent validation of actual user intent. Failed finite search also does not prove that no repair exists (§2.6).

## Open research questions
1. **Do runtime repairs improve completion?** Repairs were never delivered during live episodes. Compare feedback alone against feedback plus revalidated repairs, measuring completion, latency, and unintended edits.
2. **Does protection persist with authorized writes?** Read-only DTap blocks many benign tasks. Repeat evaluation with scoped write grants, independent payload review, and trusted confirmations.
3. **How robust is consent handling?** Consent generation lies outside verification and produced audited errors. Test a trusted confirmation interface against mismatched arguments, stale state, and ambiguous dialogue.
4. **How well does the approach generalize?** Evidence is limited to Qwen, English, simulated users, and selected domains. Evaluate another model family, multilingual interactions, and additional supported applications.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly describes the method and qualifications, but many tables contain captions without their numerical bodies.

Material unavailable information includes full per-model results and denominators, statistical test details, end-to-end admission overhead, repair candidate construction and intent-condition details, and inspected implementation artifacts. These gaps limit independent assessment of reproducibility, comparison fairness, and deployment cost.
