# When Morphs Look Better: Face Image Quality as a Training-Free Signal for Morphing Attack Detection

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-03.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.00074) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00074)


## One-sentence takeaway
Pretrained face-quality scores can detect morphs without morph-specific training, but fixed-direction evaluation reveals attack-dependent reversals and severe threshold-transfer failures that limit their reliability.

## Problem and motivation
Face morphs can let multiple people pass recognition checks against one identity document. Supervised morphing attack detection (MAD) can degrade under unfamiliar attack generators or image sources, motivating signals that require no morph-specific training (§I).

Face image quality assessment (FIQA) offers such a signal, but the authors identify two gaps: limited evaluation of recent FIQA methods and diffusion-generated morphs, and prior protocols that inverted score directions using attack labels, potentially hiding failures (§§I–II).

## Approach and contributions
- **Expanded evaluation:** Apply 17 released FIQA methods directly to single-image MAD across 20 attack sets from FRLL and FEI, including five diffusion-based sets. Existing models retain their original weights (§§III–IV).
- **Fixed-direction protocol:** Normalize each score’s direction from its original meaning before evaluation; classify low-utility images as attacks. Set the operational threshold using bona fide images alone, targeting BSCER ≈20% (§III).
- **Direction-aware analysis:** Report signed separation \(S=\operatorname{sign}(\mu_{\mathrm{BF}}-\mu_{\mathrm{A}})\sqrt{\mathrm{FDR}}\), alongside detection errors, to expose morphs receiving higher predicted utility. Analyze attack dependence, threshold transfer, and FIQA–MAD ranking agreement (§§III–V).

The contribution is an evaluation protocol and empirical findings, rather than a newly trained FIQA model.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strongest average FRLL result | Across eight FRLL attacks, DifFIQA(R) achieves mean D-EER **11.42%**, 95% CI **[9.0%, 14.0%]**, and mean MACER **10.75%** at BSCER ≈20%; CLIB-FIQA follows at **16.99% / 20.03%** D-EER/MACER. | §V-A; Table III referenced |
| Diffusion attacks frequently reverse quality ordering | Higher mean quality for morphs occurs in **11/17**, **10/17**, and **8/17** methods on FRLL DCMorph, MorDIFF, and Arc2Morph, respectively; **10/17** reverse on each FEI Arc2Morph and StableMorph set. | §V |
| Strong separation can accompany detection failure | On FRLL DCMorph, FROQ has **S = −2.29** and **94.90% D-EER**. Attack-informed direction reversal would yield **5.10%**, but violates the protocol. | §§V, V-A |
| Method rankings depend on the attack | GraFIQs(L) achieves **9.20%** and **5.66% D-EER** on FRLL DCMorph and Arc2Morph, versus **37.70–61.20%** on FRLL landmark/GAN attacks. | §V-A |
| FEI average performance is substantially weaker | ViT-FIQA(T) has the lowest FEI mean D-EER, **39.75%**, 95% CI **[37.3%, 42.6%]**, and lowest mean MACER, **58.96%**, at BSCER =20%. Its D-EER interval overlaps seven other methods’ intervals. | §V-B; Appendix C |
| Thresholds can fail across datasets | DifFIQA(R) and CLIB-FIQA thresholds calibrated at BSCER ≈20% on FRLL yield **100% BSCER** on FEI. FEI-calibrated thresholds yield **0% BSCER** on FRLL, with mean MACER **90.9%** and **98.1%**, respectively. | §V-B |
| FIQA ranking is only a fragile predictor of MAD ranking | Across 14 methods, Spearman correlations are **ρ=0.54, p=0.047** on FRLL and **ρ=0.55, p=0.043** on FEI; removing one method makes the correlation nonsignificant in **8/14** and **9/14** leave-one-out cases. | §V-A; Appendix F |

These results establish useful discrimination in some evaluated settings and show why unsigned separation or attack-informed score inversion can mislead. They do not establish deployment readiness: operational thresholds are calibrated and evaluated on the same bona fide images, and the best method is identified retrospectively using attack labels (§§III, V-A). Cross-dataset average errors also combine changes in bona fide data and attack composition (§V-B).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Bona fide cohorts contain only 204 FRLL and 200 FEI images. The benchmark’s BSCER ≈20% operating point is not intended for deployment; in-dataset MACER is explicitly described as optimistic (§§III, V-B).
- **Author-stated:** Capture setup, resolution, subjects, and potentially generator settings differ across datasets; the experiments do not isolate the causes of score shifts (§§IV, V-B).
- **Reproducibility constraints:** Two FEI generators are described only as LADIMO-/StyleDemorpher-inspired, without documented morph-production details. MorDIFF and FRLL Arc2Morph use author-code reproductions (§IV).
- **Comparison constraint:** SelfMAD++ receives upsampled aligned inputs rather than its native input setting, so the authors exclude it from direct FIQA comparisons (§IV).
- **Analyst inference:** Excluding images where SelfMAD++ fails face detection conditions all methods’ results on that detector’s successful cases; the effect of these exclusions is not quantified (appendix preamble).
- **Analyst inference:** Controlled frontal-image evaluation does not establish robustness to the deployment variations discussed in the motivation (§§I, IV).

## Open research questions
1. **Does bona fide-only calibration generalize within a target domain?** Current operational results reuse calibration images. Next experiment: split target-domain identities into calibration and held-out evaluation cohorts, then measure achieved BSCER and MACER (§§III, VI).
2. **What determines response direction?** Artifact absence alone does not explain diffusion reversals, since MIPGAN attacks reverse less frequently. Next experiment: compare generators on matched identity pairs while controlling preprocessing and generation settings (§V).
3. **Can a FIQA method be selected without attack labels?** The reported winner requires retrospective attack evaluation. Next experiment: predefine a selection rule using bona fide-only statistics and test it on unseen attacks (§V-A).
4. **Does quality rejection help or harm a trained MAD pipeline?** This interaction is proposed but not evaluated. Next experiment: compare a trained detector with and without FIQA rejection, reporting both retained-sample performance and attack retention (§VI).
