# Measuring AI Agents as Teammates with Collaborative Intelligence

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-03.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.02633) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02633)


## One-sentence takeaway
The Collaborative Intelligence Benchmark reveals substantial teamwork failures among individually capable agents, especially under interpersonal conflict and compromised handoffs, although its ability to predict contributions to other team tasks remains untested (§§3–4, 7).

## Problem and motivation
Individual capability benchmarks and whole-team evaluations do not isolate whether a particular model contributes effectively as a teammate. The authors target this gap by evaluating behaviors such as sharing information, helping without prompting, resisting harmful cooperation, and repairing inherited misconduct. These distinctions matter because completing one’s assigned task can still leave the team unsuccessful (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Individual teammate benchmark:** 240 tasks covering eight dimensions: unsafe pressure, misconduct reporting, information sharing/seeking, emotional intelligence, maintaining focus, conflict handling, initiative, and takeover. Tasks adapt real records and existing benchmarks; 160 are public and 80 held out (§§2.1–2.3).
- **Controlled evaluation protocol:** Each task has alone, cooperative-baseline, and problematic-teammate versions. Scores include only tasks the model solves alone. A branching scripted teammate creates the problem, live LLM partners supply additional interaction, and code verifies outcomes. LLM judges determine script transitions (§§2.2–2.3).
- **Comparative and diagnostic evaluation:** The authors evaluate 18 models, including six at higher reasoning effort, and analyze recognition versus action failures, inherited cheating, and conflict behavior (§§3–4).
- **Reduced evaluation asset:** A 40-task public Lite subset provides a cheaper approximation of the full benchmark, with validation on model rows excluded from subset selection (§C.4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong models still lose substantial performance under teammate problems | Gemini 4 Argon scores 76.6 at default reasoning and 82.2 at high effort. Across default-setting models, baseline-to-test drops range from 20.2 to 46.6 points, on tasks each solves alone. | §3.1; Table 2 caption |
| Safety performance exceeds social coordination performance | Frontier models pass at least 93% of unsafe-pressure and misconduct-reporting tests, versus 45% of emotional-intelligence tests. | §3.2; Fig. 3 |
| Pressure sharply reduces optimal negotiation | Frontier models reach the best joint deal in 100% of alone runs, 84% with normal teammates, and 14% under pressure; under pressure, 81% accept the teammate’s weaker split. Completed deals nevertheless average 95% of optimal joint points. | §4.2; §F.2 |
| Compromised handoffs preserve misconduct | Frontier takeover pass rate is 81%, versus 97% when starting the same tasks themselves. They fail 56 of 165 coding takeovers; 44 failures retain the cheat. | §4.4 |
| More reasoning produces mixed gains | Five of six models gain 2.7–5.7 overall points; Kimi K3.1 Pro loses 3.7. All six lose 0.3–11.7 conflict-score points at high effort. | §3.2; Appendix E |
| Capability predicts much, but not all, of the score | Across 17 models, teamwork correlates with the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at r=0.92, accounting for 85% of score variance. Gemini 4 Argon and Claude Fable 5.1 differ by 11 teamwork points despite index scores within one point. Capability measurements generally use highest reasoning settings, while teamwork uses default settings. | Appendix F; Fig. 18 |
| Many failures follow explicit recognition | Of 5,094 failed test runs across 18 models, 45 ± 4% follow recognized problems; the frontier-model share is 62% (95% interval 55–68%). Recognition is inferred from message labels and is a lower bound. | §4.5; §G.2 |
| Rankings show internal stability | Public versus held-out rankings have Kendall’s τ=0.88; scores differ by 2.7 points on average. Shared-eligible-task comparisons agree with leaderboard ordering in 271 of 276 model-row pairs. | §C.1; Fig. 9 |

These results support differences in behavior under the benchmark’s controlled teammate problems. They do not establish that the score predicts effectiveness in other agent teams; the authors explicitly identify that validation as future work (§7). Conflict scores also require careful interpretation: accepting pressure often sacrifices few joint points, while holding firm receives partial credit even without the optimal deal (§F.2).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope limits:** Partners are LLMs, scripted teammates vary less than real teammates, and alone/baseline versions run once while tests run three times. Some diagnostic studies use an earlier six-model evaluation rather than the leaderboard runs (Appendix A).
- **Judgment and checker errors:** Human agreement with final judge labels is 33/40 messages, or 82.5%, with a 95% interval of 70.0–92.5%. The authors corrected 19 verdict-changing grading errors among 315 reviewed safety failures. Cross-family checks find no favoritism, but reliably detect only relatively large bias (§C.2; Appendix A).
- **Eligibility uncertainty — analyst inference:** A single alone run determines task inclusion, potentially making eligibility sensitive to sampling. Shared-task and statistical-adjustment checks support ranking stability, but some sparsely supported dimension scores move substantially (§§C.1–C.3).
- **Construct validity:** Conflict scoring emphasizes resistance to pressure rather than joint utility alone. The supplied results show these objectives can favor different behavior (§F.2).
- **Recognition measurement:** Unspoken recognition is invisible to message labels; apparent failures to notice are therefore an upper bound (§§4.5, G.3).
- **Human comparisons are unmatched:** Instructions, incentives, information access, and sample sizes differ. Negotiating humans pursue their own points, whereas models are told to maximize joint points (§5; §G.1).
- **Cost scope:** Reported API costs exclude partners and judges (Appendix A).

## Open research questions
- **Does the score predict added value in other teams?** This remains explicitly untested (§7). A next experiment could compare models’ incremental contributions on unseen team tasks while controlling for individual capability.
- **How much do partner identity and script design affect rankings?** The evaluation fixes partners and uses controlled scripts (§2.2; Appendix A). Repeat matched tasks with varied live partners and paraphrased pressure strategies.
- **Can interventions improve conflict handling without reducing useful cooperation?** Higher reasoning improves some dimensions while worsening conflict (Appendix E). Test targeted training or prompts across both conflict scores and joint utility, alongside initiative and emotional intelligence.
- **Which handoff evidence prevents inherited cheating?** Showing diffs helps edited tests much more than special-cased code in a small follow-up (§4.4). Compare standardized audits, diffs, and explicit specification checks across a larger takeover set.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied narrative and appendices support the main findings, but most numerical table bodies and plotted values are absent. Full per-model results, exact task prompts and checkers, Lite task identities, and detailed case transcripts are unavailable here. Public artifact availability is author-reported rather than independently verified; transfer to real human teams or unrelated agent workflows is not established.
