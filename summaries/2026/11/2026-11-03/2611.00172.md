# From Fragmentation to Fusion: A Survey and Roadmap for Omnimodal Embeddings

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-03.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.00172) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00172)


## One-sentence takeaway

This survey proposes judging omnimodal embeddings by coverage, comparability, and compositionality, arguing that shared architectures and aligned vectors alone do not establish genuine multimodal fusion (§§1.4, 2.3).

## Problem and motivation

Embedding research is divided into modality-specific communities, surveys, and benchmarks, limiting methodological reuse and meaningful comparison across modalities (§1.1). The authors argue that this fragmentation also constrains retrieval and memory for systems that jointly process multiple modalities (§1.2).

The identified gap is a cross-modal synthesis that distinguishes shared principles from modality-specific requirements and separates accepting multiple input types from matching or combining their meanings (§§1.2–1.4).

**Coverage limitation:** The supplied source is truncated during §5.2 and resumes in the bibliography. This analysis covers the available text, including introductory summaries of later sections, rather than the complete paper.

## Approach and contributions

This is a survey and position paper; the available text does not present a newly trained embedding model.

- **Capability framework:** The proposed “3C” lens distinguishes coverage—inputs actually encoded and tested; comparability—task-specific matching across separately encoded inputs; and compositionality—outputs that genuinely depend on multiple inputs (§1.4).
- **Unification taxonomy:** Transformation, alignment, and joint embedding are organized as paths toward those capabilities. Joint embedding is the authors’ advocated direction, rather than an experimentally established universal winner in the excerpt (§1.4).
- **Cross-modal design vocabulary:** A common anatomy separates tokenization, encoder, aggregation/output object, training objective, and evaluation. An additional lens distinguishes shared mechanisms, modality-specific differences, and opportunities for reuse (§§2.1–2.3).
- **Literature synthesis and census:** The authors identify convergence in objectives, architectures, training stages, and representation granularity, and report quantitative evidence of research fragmentation (§§1.1, 1.3). Their claim of being the first unified treatment cannot be independently assessed from the supplied text.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated qualifications:** Comparability is task-specific; accepting inputs together does not by itself verify composition (§§1.2, 1.4). Shared parameters likewise do not guarantee a common retrieval geometry (§4.1).
- **Author-stated trade-offs:** No representation object is universally best. Global pooling loses information, while local readouts increase costs; strong modality priors can remain advantageous when data are scarce (§§4.3, 5.1).
- **Analyst inference:** The fragmentation percentage cannot be audited from this excerpt because corpus selection, coding criteria, exact denominators, and Appendix B’s limitations are unavailable.
- **Analyst inference:** The recommendation to favor joint embedding remains a position within the available evidence; controlled comparisons demonstrating its scope are not supplied.
- **Source limitation:** Most substantive material on transformation, alignment, fusion, the roadmap, and unified evaluation is omitted. Several tables retain captions without their contents.

## Open research questions

- **How can composition be verified beyond input co-processing?** The 3C framework requires dependence on every input, but detailed tests are unavailable (§1.4). A next experiment could remove, swap, or contradict individual modalities in composed retrieval queries and compare against separate-encoder baselines.
- **Which methods transfer reliably across modality families?** The authors identify shared losses but emphasize different invariances and targets (§§3.1–3.2). A next experiment could transfer one training recipe across families while varying only positive construction or reconstruction targets.
- **Which output objects best balance retrieval efficiency and information retention?** The survey describes competing benefits without establishing a universal choice (§5.1). A next experiment could compare global, local, and combined readouts from a fixed encoder under matched storage and latency budgets.
