# CrossSWE: Benchmarking Cross-Repository Joint Development for Coding Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-03.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.01484) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01484)


## One-sentence takeaway
CrossSWE introduces 171 tasks requiring coordinated repository changes, with the strongest evaluated model reaching 58.48% Pass@1 under a shared coding-agent scaffold (Table 1).

## Problem and motivation
Software changes can require synchronized updates to interfaces, dependencies, and consumers across repositories. The authors argue that existing benchmarks largely constrain executable solutions to one repository, even when agents consult other repositories, leaving coordinated implementation insufficiently evaluated (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Benchmark:** CrossSWE contains task statements, prepared execution environments, behavioral tests, and repository-specific developer patches for 171 real-world tasks spanning 165 repositories, 20 technical domains, and 13 programming languages (§§3.1–3.3).
- **Joint-necessity verification:** For each task, the authors test every combination of base and gold-patched repositories. Target tests must fail for every partial combination and pass only when all gold patches are applied (§3.2).
- **Empirical characterization:** Five models share mini-SWE-agent v2.4.6; additional experiments compare joint versus single-side modification and manually categorize unsuccessful trajectories (§§4–5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Broad coverage, concentrated repository count | 171 tasks: 165 involve two repositories, five involve three, and one involves five; 165 distinct repositories overall. | §1; Limitations |
| Larger aggregate reference patches | More than ten modified files: CrossSWE 56.1%, SWE-bench Pro 6%, ProMax 30%. More than 200 modified lines: 66.7%, 13%, and 32%, respectively. | §3.3; Fig. 3 |
| Highest observed success remains limited | Overall Pass@1: GPT-6.1 Sol 58.48%; DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 52.63%; Claude Sonnet 5.5 50.29%; Qwen3.8-Flash-Next 45.03%; GLM-5.3 Flash 43.86%. Shared scaffold, high reasoning effort, maximum 300 steps and $10 per run. | §4; Table 1 |
| Language-setting difference for GPT | GPT-6.1 Sol achieves 60.34% Pass@1 on single-language tasks and 54.55% on multi-language tasks. | Table 1 |
| Single-side variants yield higher success | On 330 variants derived from 165 two-repository tasks, GPT reaches 64.55% and DeepSeek 61.81% Pass@1. Table 2 compares these with joint scores of 58.48% and 52.63%, respectively. | §5.2; Table 2 |
| Shorter GPT trajectories accompany competitive reported cost | GPT averages 32.9 steps and $0.3496 per instance; DeepSeek averages 152.7 steps and $0.2881 under the same evaluation limits. | Table 1 |
| Interface consistency is a prominent failure category | Cross-repository inconsistency accounts for 44.2% of GPT’s unsuccessful trajectories, 41.2% of Claude’s, 29.2% of DeepSeek’s, and 40.7% of GLM’s in manual classification. | §5.4; Fig. 7 |

The results establish difficulty for these model–scaffold combinations and support the authors’ claim that coordinated changes pose challenges. They do not isolate coordination from patch size or task complexity. The 44.2% figure concerns GPT’s failures, not all failures pooled across models. The single-side comparison also requires caution: its variants come from the two-repository subset, whereas the displayed joint scores reproduce the overall benchmark scores.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Larger repository networks are sparsely represented; agents receive the relevant repositories; findings use one scaffold and fixed resource limits. Transfer to other frameworks requires direct validation (Limitations).
- **Analyst inference:** Exhaustive verification establishes necessity among combinations of the supplied gold patches. It does not prove that every possible alternative solution must modify every repository (§3.2).
- **Analyst inference:** The single-side experiment incompletely specifies how task statements, counterpart repository states, and tests are adapted, limiting interpretation of its coordination effect (§5.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Manual failure labels lack reported annotator counts, agreement, and adjudication details. Numerical Qwen category shares are unavailable in the supplied text (§5.4).
- **Analyst inference:** The text supplies no uncertainty estimates, scaffold ablations, or matched controls for modification size; reported differences therefore have limited causal interpretation (§§4–5).

## Open research questions
- **How much difficulty comes specifically from coordination?** Patch scope also differs, and the single-side comparison has unclear matching. Next experiment: evaluate paired variants on the same repository subset with explicit counterpart states and matched modification scope.
- **Can explicit interface checks reduce failures?** Inconsistency is prominent, but no targeted intervention is tested. Next experiment: add interface-contract checks under matched budgets and measure Pass@1 and failure-category changes.
- **Does performance generalize to repository discovery and larger dependency networks?** Relevant repositories are supplied and larger networks are rare. Next experiment: add distractor repositories and more tasks requiring changes across larger networks.
- **How scaffold-dependent are the rankings?** Only one shell-based scaffold is evaluated. Next experiment: repeat the same tasks across multiple scaffolds with comparable resource budgets.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** Benchmark construction and headline results are explicit, but causal interpretation is limited. Missing information includes a concrete dataset/download URL, complete executable specifications, detailed single-side variant construction, failure-labeling protocol, repeated-run uncertainty, and scaffold/budget ablations. Appendix A.1 contains no substantive availability details, although Appendix B states that the dataset is hosted on Hugging Face.
