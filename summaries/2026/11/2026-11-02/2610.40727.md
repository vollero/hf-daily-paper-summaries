# VoxLock: Target-Speaker Extraction for Voice Agents Without an Enrollment Step

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.40727) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40727)


## One-sentence takeaway
VoxLock adapts a public target-speaker extractor to use speech sampled from the call itself, substantially reducing recognition errors on tested recordings, but assuming the caller speaks early and dominates the initial audio (§§3–5).

## Problem and motivation
Voice agents can transcribe and respond to nearby people rather than their intended caller. Conventional noise suppression preserves speech, while target-speaker extraction typically requires a separate enrollment recording. The author identifies a second gap: research often measures signal quality on simulated mixtures rather than downstream transcription on real recordings (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Automatic in-call conditioning:** Uses a short voice sample selected from the recording or accumulated during live speech. This removes separate enrollment, but still requires automatic identification of the intended caller (§3.2).
- **Targeted fine-tuning:** Adapts the existing causal REAL-TSE BSRNN through simulated calls, real headset crosstalk, longer speech sequences, and varied speaker distances. The backbone and conditioning architecture are inherited; the contribution is their adaptation and training recipe (§§3.1–3.3).
- **Recognition-centered evaluation:** Compares transcription accuracy, caller-word loss, and background-word leakage across commercial and open systems, with an additional frozen AMI evaluation set (§§4–5).
- **Deployable release:** Reports releasing model weights, ONNX graphs, a Python runtime, and per-file scores, including a quantized CPU implementation (§§6, 8).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Large improvement on Krisp recordings | On 265 recordings, whole-file VoxLock reports **4.95% WER**, versus **23.21% unprocessed**; **9 background words** enter transcripts across **85.3 minutes** of background-only speech. | Abstract; §5, Table 1 discussion |
| Competitive commercial comparison | Author reports no significant WER difference versus Krisp Default or Balanced, and significantly lower WER than the tested non-Krisp commercial and open systems. Krisp Default admits **130 background words** over the same **85.3 minutes**. | §5, Table 1 discussion |
| Improvement on frozen AMI windows | On **400 windows, 16 people, 11 meetings**, WER is **10.63%**, versus **42.95% unprocessed** and **14.75% REAL-TSE**. Background leakage is **1.17**, **98.19**, and **12.18 words/minute**, respectively. | §4; Table 3 |
| Live operation has an accuracy cost | Live WER is **5.15% versus 4.95%** whole-file on Krisp; on frozen AMI it is **11.97% versus 10.63%**, with difference interval **[−0.27, +2.41] percentage points**. | §6; Table 3 |
| Filtering can hurt relatively clean speech | In the Krisp phone-call scenario, WER rises from **3.82% unprocessed to 5.36%**. On **200 caller-only AMI windows**, it rises from **7.18% to 7.49%**; difference interval **[−0.32, +1.33] points**, not significant. | §5 |
| Wrong conditioning materially degrades extraction | On **399 frozen AMI windows**, a different participant’s sample produces **23.23% WER** and **16.97% missed words**, versus **10.63%** and **8.26%** for the default correct-sample setting on 400 windows. | Table 3; §5 |
| CPU feasibility excludes a substantial startup stall | The 8-bit model reports **5.11% Krisp WER** and uses **77% of one CPU thread** with **40 ms chunks** on the specified Xeon system. Algorithmic latency is **24 ms**, additional to chunk duration; live activation stalls output for **2–5 seconds** on one thread. | §6 |

These results support improved transcription and reduced background leakage in the evaluated settings. They do not establish universal speaker selection, statistical equivalence to commercial filters, or uninterrupted low-latency activation. The strongest broad commercial comparisons use development-exposed data; the frozen AMI set compares only VoxLock variants, REAL-TSE, and unprocessed audio (§§4–6).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated selection constraint:** A background speaker dominating the opening can cause VoxLock to retain the wrong person. Every AMI evaluation window begins with the caller, favoring the sampling rule (§§4, 7).
- **Author-stated evaluation limits:** Krisp and the initial AMI set informed development. Frozen AMI covers only successfully downloaded meetings and lacks most comparison systems (§§4–5).
- **Statistical limits:** Intervals bootstrap people despite dependencies within meetings and are uncorrected for multiple comparisons; the author warns AMI intervals may be too narrow (§4).
- **Training attribution remains unresolved:** Stages were developed sequentially from observed failures in one training run. The author explicitly says this is not a controlled ablation and cannot isolate the benefit of real crosstalk (§5).
- **Scope and deployment limits:** English training, one recognizer family, single-caller operation, clean-speech degradation, and the activation stall restrict generalization (§§5–7).
- **Reproducibility concern:** Re-extracting the reported checkpoint changed error counts on some Krisp files; the supplied text does not explain why (§5, Table 4 caption).

## Open research questions
1. **Can the intended caller be selected reliably when another person speaks first?** Current sampling assumes early caller dominance. A next experiment could vary speaker onset order and relative loudness while measuring target-selection failures and WER (§§3.2, 7).
2. **Do gains transfer across recognizers and languages?** Evaluation uses one recognizer family and English-trained extraction. A next experiment could hold audio fixed while testing multiple recognizers and multilingual caller/background combinations (§§4, 7).
3. **Which training changes cause the gains?** Later stages combine changes to examples, duration, distance, and learning rate. Matched-budget, repeated-seed ablations could isolate their effects on leakage, continuous speech, and distant callers (§§3.3, 5).
4. **Can live activation avoid the startup stall without losing accuracy?** The runtime warms its recurrent state by replaying preceding audio. A next experiment could compare incremental warm-up strategies using activation delay, backlog, WER, and leakage (§6).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly supports the main accuracy findings and deployment caveats, but the bodies of Tables 1, 2, and 4 are absent despite their captions and narrative summaries. Consequently, complete baseline scores, comparison intervals, and stage results are unavailable. Also missing are repeated training runs, cross-recognizer or multilingual results, unconstrained caller-onset evaluation, and an explanation for checkpoint re-extraction discrepancies.
