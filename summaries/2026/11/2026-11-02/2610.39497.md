# Does Scaling Toward AGI Depend on What System-1 Lets System-2 Execute?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.39497) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39497)


## One-sentence takeaway

This Perspective formalizes cheap utility prediction before costly execution and derives conditional efficiency gains, while leaving its proposed connection to AGI scaling empirically unestablished in the supplied, truncated text.

## Problem and motivation

The authors argue that scaling models, reasoning, and agents increasingly spends resources on interactions that contribute little useful signal. Their proposed gap is an architectural one: how to estimate an interaction’s value **before** paying to execute it, while tracking an executor whose capabilities keep changing (§1).

Their central hypothesis is that utility-relevant interaction density, \(\rho_u\), helps explain resource efficiency beyond total compute; its decline during learning is presented as a prediction, not an established finding (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Conditional-interaction framework:** System-1 predicts candidate utility; a threshold gates System-2 execution; observed outcomes update the predictor. Utilities remain application-specific rather than interchangeable (§§2.1, 3.1).
- **Resource-accounting framework:** Interaction Information Efficiency (IIE) measures executed utility per screening-plus-execution cost, paired with yield to expose gates that save resources by suppressing too much useful signal (§2.2).
- **Formal guarantees:** The authors derive ideal screening gains, selection-regret bounds, conditional memory savings, and a qualitative co-scaling result under fixed predictor-error curves (§§3.2, 4.1).
- **Scaling hypothesis:** A perfect gate horizontally shifts a learning curve under restrictive assumptions; extending this into an explanatory scaling law remains a hypothesis (Appendix A.11).

This is a framework and theoretical Perspective, rather than a single implemented algorithm. The authors explicitly identify the active-inference correspondence as standard and the interpretive connections as separate from their contributions (§§3.1, 4, 4.2).

## Evidence and main results

All results below are theoretical, rather than measured benchmark improvements.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Ideal IIE gain is \(1/\rho_u\) | Relative to unconditional execution, assuming perfect free prediction, equal execution costs, and zero utility for excluded candidates. Unequal costs change the ratio. | §2.2, Proposition 7, Eq. 12 |
| Perfect screening costs a fraction \(\rho_u+r\) of exhaustive evaluation | Same relevant subset; equal execution cost; \(r=c_{\text{screen}}/c_{\text{exec}}\). Audits and predictor refitting add overhead. | §4.1, Proposition 14, Eq. 24 |
| Top-B selection regret is at most \(2TB\varepsilon_\phi\), and zero below the margin | Deterministic predicted top-B versus oracle top-B; uniform prediction error \(\varepsilon_\phi\); exact selection when \(2\varepsilon_\phi<\mathrm{gap}_{\min}\). | §4.1, Theorem 18; Appendix A.6 |
| Optimal predictor size weakly increases as execution becomes costlier | Comparative statics holding relevance density and predictor-error curves fixed, with the stated cost monotonicity assumption. | §3.2, Proposition 13; Appendix A.10 |
| One-probe halting bounds cost and accuracy loss | Versus \(C\) increments per query: compute fraction at most \(\rho_u^{\mathrm{tt}}+(1+c_{\text{pred}})/C+\delta_{\mathrm{FP}}\); accuracy loss at most \(\delta_{\mathrm{FN}}\), provided relevance covers all queries benefiting from continuation. | Appendix A.8, Proposition 22 |
| Ideal gating horizontally rescales the loss curve | \(\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CI}}(\mathcal C)=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{base}}(\mathcal C/\rho_u)\), assuming constant density, inert dead interactions, preserved relevant-interaction order/distribution, and perfect free screening. | Appendix A.11, Proposition 24 |

These results establish how savings depend on predictor quality, costs, and utility definitions. They do not establish that suitable cheap predictors generally exist, that the coupled learning loop reliably improves itself, or that interaction density explains observed AGI scaling. The supplied text references earlier sampling studies but does not provide their experimental details.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Gating can fail under distribution shift, utility misspecification, costly prediction, catastrophic false negatives, or unmodeled exploratory value (§2.4).
- **Author-stated:** Suppression censors the predictor’s own supervision; audits of rejected candidates are needed to expose blind spots (§2.4; Fig. 6).
- **Author-stated:** General coupled-loop convergence remains open; fixed-error co-scaling does not cover an executor that changes the prediction problem (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Author-stated:** Memory savings depend on synchronous reactive evaluation; streamed reactive execution also has pool-independent memory (§4.1).
- **Author-stated:** Agentic guarantees require separability; jointly useful actions can invalidate individual gating (Appendix A.8).
- **Analyst inference:** Utility-specific gains cannot alone demonstrate general capability gains, because the paper explicitly distinguishes information gain, gradient signal, adaptation risk, and accuracy improvement.
- **Coverage limitation:** A substantial middle portion was omitted. Missing experiments or ablations cannot be treated as absent from the complete paper.

## Open research questions

1. **Does \(\rho_u\) explain scaling beyond compute?** The paper presents this as a hypothesis. Next experiment: measure declared utility density throughout training and compare predictive fits using total versus informative compute.
2. **How much auditing prevents persistent blind spots?** Selective supervision is acknowledged, but the visible text establishes no operational audit rate. Next experiment: vary audit budgets under controlled distribution shifts and measure missed utility, yield, and total cost.
3. **How should predictor capacity track an improving executor?** Proposition 13 fixes error curves. Next experiment: jointly vary predictor and executor sizes while measuring how utility distributions and predictor errors change.
4. **Can gating preserve delayed and complementary benefits?** One-step utilities and separability limit the guarantees. Next experiment: compare individual gates with batch-aware or longer-horizon selection on tasks requiring jointly useful actions.
