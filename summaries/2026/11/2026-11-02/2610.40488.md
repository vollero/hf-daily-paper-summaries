# rTaste: Do Research Agents Have Taste? Evaluating Early Stage Research Judgment

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.40488) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40488)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across seven NLP seed papers, agents proposed many experiments judged worthwhile by model evaluators, but their rankings did not demonstrably outperform proposal order, and their forecasts did not measurably outperform fresh instances of the same models (§§4.1–4.5).

## Problem and motivation
The paper evaluates early research judgment: choosing which inexpensive experiments deserve investment before outcomes are known. The authors identify a gap between benchmarks that measure execution on assigned problems, evaluations of unexecuted ideas, and forecasting studies: these do not jointly test agents’ self-generated proposals, predictions, and priorities against outcomes from every proposal (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Evaluation protocol:** Six models, each acting alone and as a team, use recent papers and their code to register five minimum viable experiments (MVEs), forecast threshold events, and rank research priorities. Separate executors attempt every proposal, allowing evaluation of low-ranked experiments too (§§3.1–3.4).
- **Comparison and assessment framework:** Fresh model instances forecast and rank the same registrations; model auditors assess implementation and research value; novelty judges and subsequent reviewers assess prior work, obviousness, and relation to the seed paper (§§3.3–3.4).
- **Reusable assets and findings:** The authors report releasing the harness and all 415 registrations, forecasts, and audited outcomes. The study separates proposal quality from forecasting and selection ability, and compares single researchers with a particular team architecture (§§1, 4–7).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Many proposals meet the authors’ quality criteria, fewer meet the stronger originality criterion | Of 415 MVEs, 194 were judged sound, valuable, and new; 73 of those were also beyond the seed paper and non-obvious. Labels come from models. | Abstract; §4.2 |
| Most experiments remain close to their seed | Among delivered MVEs, 66% build on the paper, 24% check claims, and 5% open a direction merely suggested by it; 44% of all MVEs match generated obvious-follow-up lists. | §4.2; Table 2 cited |
| Agent context provides no measurable forecasting advantage over the same-model judge | Agent minus same-model-judge Brier score: −0.006, 95% CI [−0.021, 0.009]; lower is better. | §4.4; Table 3 cited |
| Forecasts outperform the stated base rate | Agent Brier score: 0.192; mean probabilities of six judges: 0.179; base rate using event rates from the other six seed papers: 0.240. | §4.4 |
| Rankings beat random selection but do not clearly improve on proposal order | Agent first choice matches auditors’ most valued MVE in 38% of conditions versus 20% for random choice; advantage over the first-written MVE: 3 percentage points, 95% CI [−8, 15]. First choices are no more often sound, valuable, and new than random picks. | §4.5 |
| Teams show uncertain quality gains at higher average cost | Team minus single-researcher quality: 0.71 additional sound, valuable, new MVEs per five, 95% CI [−0.10, 1.52]. Average condition cost, including proposal, implementation, and rerun: $23.93 versus $15.31. | §§4.6–4.7 |
| Passing a registered threshold is an imperfect proxy for informative evidence | Reviewers found informative evidence in 50% of sound MVEs whose event failed, and inconclusive evidence in 20% whose event occurred. | §4.3 |
| Label reliability is imperfect | Novelty judges’ agreement: κ = 0.27. In a planted-defect check on one seed paper, auditor majority flagged 8/10 altered copies and none of 10 originals. | Appendices B.1–B.2 |

The evidence supports a distinction between producing useful experiments and selecting among them. It does not establish equivalence between agents and simpler selection methods: the reported confidence intervals leave uncertainty. Forecast scores concern agent-defined threshold events, while ranking success concerns model auditors’ retrospective preferences; neither directly measures human research judgment or long-term scientific impact.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** All labels are model-generated, with no human baseline. Two auditors also serve as evaluated models and executors; auditors see outcomes when assessing value (§6).
- **Author-stated:** Rankings have no allocation consequences because every experiment is attempted. Complete registrations may transmit much of the information needed for forecasting, and each condition runs only once (§6).
- **Analyst inference:** Generalization is restricted by seven recent NLP papers selected for feasible short experiments using small open models. Larger-scale or other-domain research judgment remains untested (§3.1; Appendix A.3).
- **Analyst inference:** “New” and “non-obvious” measure different properties, and weak novelty-judge agreement makes originality estimates sensitive to evaluation choices (§§4.1–4.2; Appendix B.2).
- **Analyst inference:** The team comparison applies to the specified architecture. It does not isolate coordination benefits under equal expenditure; critic and lead lack tools, and average team costs are higher (§§3.2, 4.7).
- **Reporting constraint:** The supplied text contains table captions but omits table bodies, limiting inspection of detailed model-level comparisons and seed-specific results.

## Open research questions
1. **Do consequential rankings improve budget allocation?** Rankings here do not determine execution. A next experiment could give agents fixed budgets and permit adaptive investment after initial results, alongside a human baseline (§§5–6).
2. **Do humans agree with the quality and originality labels?** All current labels come from models, with imperfect agreement. A blinded expert assessment of a stratified MVE sample could test agreement and whether model rankings persist (§6; Appendix B).
3. **Does proposal order influence selection independently of content?** Agents frequently choose their first-written proposal, but this does not establish causation. Randomizing presentation order across repeated fresh-judge evaluations could separate content preference from order effects (§4.5).
4. **Would teams help under matched costs or different coordination designs?** The tested team has higher average cost without a statistically clear overall quality gain. Compare repeated single-agent and team runs under equal spending, varying tool access for the lead (§§3.2, 4.6–4.7).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The narrative supports the principal findings and explicitly describes major validity limits. Missing from the supplied text are the table bodies, complete seed-paper listing, detailed per-model results and costs, numerical auditor-agreement breakdowns, and individual MVE records. Human validation and repeated-condition variability were not measured; the reported asset release was not independently verified.
