# AuditFL: A Contract-Backed Clearing Layer for Open Federated Learning

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.41278) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41278)


## One-sentence takeaway
AuditFL makes federated-learning admission and settlement publicly auditable through smart contracts, while retaining off-chain aggregation and demonstrating predictable overhead on local-chain MNIST experiments.

## Problem and motivation
Conventional federated learning leaves admission, update collection, aggregation, and rewards under a trusted coordinator, enabling opaque omission or accounting decisions. The authors identify a gap in blockchain-assisted FL: explicit round-clearing rules and consensus assumptions without putting model tensors or ML execution on chain (§§I–II).

## Approach and contributions
- **Clearing-layer architecture:** Store content identifiers and economic state on chain; keep training, scoring, aggregation, and model artifacts off chain under a designated coordinator (§§III–IV).
- **Round state machine:** Specify funded tasks, deposit-backed update tickets, publication, finalization, refunds, reward entitlements, and claims. Contracts validate transaction relationships and settlement arithmetic (§IV).
- **Accountability argument:** Combine confirmed ledger history, replay rules, deposits, and explicit accounting. Auditing aggregation requires retrievable artifacts; auditing scores additionally requires public validation data or a committed evaluation procedure (§§IV-C, V–VI).
- **Implementation and measurement:** Provide deterministic state-machine and Solidity/Anvil backends, analytical overhead models, and clean-run and failure-stress evaluations (§§VII–VIII).

The distinctive contribution is auditable round clearing; the paper does not introduce a new learning algorithm or cryptographic proof of aggregation correctness (§II, §VI).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Clean-run learning matches Plain FL | Across tested client counts, identical accuracy with matched splits and learning rules; final held-out accuracy is 89.65% at one client and 89.21% at 200 clients, after 12 rounds. | §VIII-B |
| Transaction and artifact models match measurements | Clean Anvil runs, 12 rounds: receipts increase from 51 at one client to 5,026 at 200; artifacts increase from 25 to 2,413. Receipt counts include deployment. | §§VIII-A–B, equations (7)–(8) |
| Client calls dominate gas | At 200 clients and 12 rounds: 829.7M total gas; publication uses 408.7M and reservation 314.3M, approximately 87.1% combined. | §VIII-B, Fig. 3 |
| Identifier payloads are much smaller than artifacts | Same 200-client setting: 144.9 MiB artifact bytes versus 0.163 MiB committed identifier strings, approximately 887×. This is not a comparison against total on-chain storage. | §VIII-B, Fig. 4 |
| Injected failures preserve round completion | State-machine tests: 50 clients, 12 rounds, three seeds, failure rates from 0% to 50%; inclusion falls approximately linearly, with approximately 25 included updates per round at 50% failures. | §VIII-C, Fig. 5 |
| Observed accuracy degradation is small | In those stress tests, final accuracy differences against corresponding Plain FL runs remain within “a few tenths” of a percentage point; exact values are absent from the supplied text. | §VIII-C, Fig. 6 |

These author-reported results support predictable local execution costs and learning equivalence when the learning inputs are unchanged. They do not establish public-network performance, poisoning resistance, truthful scoring, or incentive effectiveness against strategic participants.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Aggregation remains with the coordinator, which can selectively include updates or use weak scoring. Contracts enforce accounting against submitted scores, not their truthfulness (§§IV-C, VI).
- **Author-stated:** Privacy, poisoning defense, and Sybil-proof reward fairness are not guaranteed (§§III-B, VI).
- **Author-stated:** Evaluation uses a local Anvil chain and local artifact store; public-network delay and replicated storage remain future work (§IX).
- **Author-stated:** Failure experiments use only three seeds and cannot establish which fault mode is systematically more harmful (§VIII-C).
- **Analyst inference:** Clean-run accuracy equality primarily verifies coordination transparency because both systems use the same included updates and learning rule; it does not demonstrate improved learning (§VIII-B).
- **Analyst inference:** MNIST-only experiments and exclusion-based faults provide limited evidence about larger workloads or strategic adversaries. Table I offers feature positioning, rather than measured performance comparisons with other blockchain-FL systems (§II, §VIII).

## Open research questions
1. **How does public-ledger operation affect round completion?** Local Anvil experiments omit network delay and reorganization behavior. A next experiment could measure confirmation latency and retry behavior on a public network (§§VII, IX).
2. **Can auditing reliably expose strategic coordinator behavior?** Scoring replay depends on accessible evaluation evidence. Inject selective omission and manipulated scores, then measure detection using public versus private validation procedures (§§IV-C, VI).
3. **How does replicated storage change availability and overhead?** The prototype uses local storage and tests unavailability through injection. Evaluate replicated artifact storage under retrieval delays and outages (§§V-B, VIII-C, IX).
4. **Do deposits deter strategic participation?** The paper argues that spam becomes costly but does not empirically establish incentive effectiveness. Vary deposits and fees under reservation spam and deliberate withholding, measuring attacker cost and honest-client inclusion (§VI).
