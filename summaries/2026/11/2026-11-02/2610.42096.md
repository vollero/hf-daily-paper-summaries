# CausalWAM: Learning the Causes of Actions in World Action Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.42096) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.42096)


## One-sentence takeaway
CWAM combines conditional identifiability results for sparse action mechanisms with a practical routing module that raises RoboCasa365 macro success from 0.24 to 0.43, although the deployed architecture does not enforce the theory’s full separation guarantee.

## Problem and motivation
World action models share representations between scene prediction and action generation, but prediction needs scene details that need not influence demonstrated actions. Fitting demonstrations can therefore leave action-relevant factors entangled with nuisance factors (§§1–2).

The authors seek to recover the action-relevant latent block and its dependencies on action coordinates from observation–action demonstrations, without requiring interventions, temporal dependence, or regime labels. Their gap is partial identification specifically through actions, rather than recovery of every scene factor (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Conditional identification theory.** Under invertible observation maps, pathwise coupling, derivative-span and support-inclusion assumptions, fitting demonstrations gives a lower bound on estimated action dependencies. Adding a support budget no larger than the true mechanism identifies the dependency pattern up to permutation and the relevant block up to an invertible transformation (§3, Proposition 1 and Theorems 1–2).
- **Practical CWAM router.** A gated residual attention branch routes clean visual and task tokens to the Cosmos 3 Nano action head. A penalty concentrates attention on selected tokens while preserving the existing joint video–action rectified-flow objective. The dense action pathway remains active (§4; Appendix H).
- **Empirical evaluation.** Synthetic probes assess relevant-factor recovery and excess nuisance predictability; robot experiments compare closed-loop success against matched Cosmos 3 and test routed versus random token subsets (§5).

The evidence supports improved task success for the combined router-and-penalty modification and synthetic behavior consistent with partial separation. It does not establish causal identification inside Cosmos 3: its true factors are unknown, its dense pathway remains available, and token attention is a surrogate for coordinate-level functional sparsity. Linear probes also do not prove invertibility or absence of nonlinear nuisance information.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Identification is at block level, not individual-component level, limiting precise latent interventions (§6).
- **Theory scope:** Guarantees require pathwise coupling, invertible observation maps, positive full-support state density, derivative-span conditions, support inclusion, and the true-support budget. Observational distribution matching alone is insufficient (§§2–3; Appendix B.1).
- **Analyst inference—synthetic theory mismatch:** The generator uses noisy observations and a linear action mechanism with regime scaling. Its derivative rows are collinear for a fixed action coordinate, resembling mechanisms excluded by the span discussion. These experiments therefore illustrate empirical separation rather than directly verify all theorem hypotheses (Appendices B.1 and C).
- **Analyst inference—selection assistance:** Synthetic top-coordinate selection uses the known number of relevant factors, leaving unknown-size discovery untested (Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference—attribution and scope:** The robot baseline removes both router and penalty, so their separate effects are unresolved. Token sufficiency is tested only on the strongest tasks, which limits generalization of that finding (§§5.2–5.3).
- **Evidence availability:** Several tables contain captions without numerical bodies, preventing assessment of effect sizes, variability, and individual-task outcomes.

## Open research questions
1. **Does sparsity drive the robot gains independently of the added router?** The comparison changes both together. Train matched router variants with and without the penalty, using equal optimization budgets (§5.2).
2. **Can the action pathway be made exclusively sparse across tasks?** Default evaluation retains dense access, and C-only testing covers selected strong tasks. Train a model without dense action access and evaluate it across the full benchmark (§§4, 5.3).
3. **How robust is separation when theorem assumptions fail?** The synthetic setup differs from the noiseless invertible formulation. Compare generators satisfying the span condition against controlled violations, with nonlinear nuisance probes (§2; Appendices B–C).
4. **Can relevant-block size be learned?** Synthetic selection assumes its true size. Sweep unknown support sizes and evaluate selection accuracy alongside action performance (Appendix C).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The method, assumptions, and RoboCasa365 aggregate result are explicit, but quantitative assessment is incomplete.

Missing material includes numerical bodies of Tables 1–2 and 4–6, exact LIBERO and Franka success rates, robot training-run variability, separate router/penalty ablations, detailed real-robot and LIBERO training protocols, and confirmed code or checkpoint availability.
