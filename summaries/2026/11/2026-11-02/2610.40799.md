# CaPtain: A Pipeline for Post-Training Code-as-Policy Robot Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.40799) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40799)


## One-sentence takeaway
CaPtain post-trains a 4B vision-language model to control robots through low-level Python APIs, raising held-out LIBERO host success from 55.4% after supervised training to 73.8% after reinforcement learning, while retaining substantial embodiment and termination limitations (§4.1, §5, Appendix I).

## Problem and motivation
Code-as-policy agents generate programs that call robot perception and motion APIs. The authors identify a gap between capable systems relying on frontier models or frozen manipulation skills and small open models that cannot reliably use low-level robot interfaces (§1).

Training these agents introduces difficult credit assignment: many requests observe or verify, while manipulation outcomes emerge later. The proposed opportunity is to use privileged simulator state during training to identify consequential physical actions without exposing that state to the deployed policy (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Integrated post-training pipeline:** Synthetic trajectories teach Qwen3.5-4B the execution protocol through supervised fine-tuning (SFT); multi-turn reinforcement learning (RL) then improves manipulation and recovery. The authors claim release of training code, data, and weights (§3, Contributions).
- **CaPtain-Harness:** A persistent Python environment exposes 22 perception, measurement, planning, state, and guarded-motion capabilities, plus `finish()`. Structured execution receipts complement camera observations; the policy chooses grasp poses and jaw commands without a VLA or grasp/place macros (§3.1).
- **Physical-state credit assignment:** An online-trained ensemble critic predicts success from privileged simulator features. Differences evaluated at a fixed request index provide per-request credit, combined with generalized advantage estimation and group-relative outcomes. The critic is removed at deployment (§3.3).
- **Transfer evaluation:** The paper tests perturbation robustness, unseen benchmarks, and hardware deployment following simulation-only embodiment adaptation (§4).

The methodological contribution combines established SFT, streaming RL, and asymmetric actor–critic ideas within a low-level code-writing robot agent; the paper does not introduce those underlying ideas individually (§2–3).

## Evidence and main results

All results below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| RL improves manipulation and completion | On 240 held-out LIBERO episodes, SFT→RL host success rises 55.4%→73.8%; strict success, requiring `finish()`, rises 52.1%→67.5%. | §4.1; Table 1 |
| Gains survive perturbations | Host-success gains over SFT: +19.0 percentage points on LIBERO-PRO short-horizon suites, +19.4 across all suites, +17.6 on LIBERO-Plus, and +18.8/+21.2 on LIBERO-MAX Base/Dynamic. Evaluations use 600/800, 700, and 80 cases per MAX setting, respectively. | §4 benchmarks; §4.1 |
| Several published frontier-agent scores are exceeded | LIBERO-PRO short-horizon host success is 78.5%, versus published K1 77.2%, WAA 75.6%, and ASPIRE 71.7%; protocols differ. | §4.2; Appendix K |
| Transfer is uneven | Without target-domain training: 83.3% success over four SimplerEnv WidowX tasks with 24 episodes each; 22.6% over 5,000 RoboTwin Hard episodes; 0.8% over RoboDojo’s 42 tasks with 15 layouts each. | §4.3; Appendix K |
| CaP-Bench performance depends on horizon | Cube Lift/Stack score improves 55.0→75.0 under the standard step horizon; extending execution yields 97.5% mean success over 20 trials per task. This does not cover the whole suite. | §4.3 |
| Critic analyses support physical credit concentration | On earlier 9B-policy offline rollouts, successful grasps have median predicted-success change +0.058, versus −0.0002 for perception-only requests; outcome-only versus critic-based gradient-noise scale is 9.0k versus 6.7k episodes. | §4.5 |
| Hardware feasibility is demonstrated | Two described XR1 tasks succeed after adaptation using simulated XR1 episodes only; no aggregate hardware success rate is supplied. | §4.4; Figures 4–5 |
| Policy inference is relatively inexpensive but slow | LIBERO averages 417 seconds wall time per episode on a GPU shared by eight episodes; estimated policy compute costs $0.03–0.06 per episode at assumed GPU prices, excluding perception services. | §4.2 |

The paired SFT-versus-RL evaluations support improvement from the complete RL stage. They do not isolate the simulator critic’s causal contribution from reward shaping, training randomization, or other RL choices. Published-system rankings are qualified by different budgets, observations, success definitions, and training data (Appendices K and Q). Hardware examples establish feasibility rather than deployment reliability.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope:** RL uses only the original LIBERO tasks and a single-arm embodiment. Simultaneous bimanual coordination and unfamiliar-arm reach remain major transfer barriers (§5).
- **Authors’ stated training dependency:** The critic requires exact simulator state. Its credit signal becomes less correlated with privileged credit under noisy pose estimates or policy perception (§5).
- **Authors’ stated interface restriction:** Guarded position primitives cannot express sustained force-controlled pressing; CaP-Bench Wipe cannot execute (§4.3, §5).
- **Observed termination instability:** Later RL checkpoints continue verifying solved scenes instead of finishing. The authors select an earlier checkpoint because strict success deteriorates despite stable host success (Appendix I).
- **Analyst inference—component attribution:** Offline critic diagnostics support the intended mechanism, but the supplied text lacks a matched end-to-end training ablation isolating its contribution to final success.
- **Analyst inference—comparison and deployment limits:** Benchmark-specific adapters and heterogeneous published protocols complicate comparisons. The policy also depends on perception and geometry services, so policy size and quoted policy-compute cost do not describe the full deployed system (§3.1, §4.2; Appendices K–L).

## Open research questions
1. **How much final performance comes specifically from the physical critic?** The paper provides diagnostics rather than a controlled final-policy comparison. Train matched critic-based and outcome-only RL runs with identical rewards, budgets, and seeds.
2. **Can termination remain reliable throughout RL?** Late training causes over-verification (Appendix I). Compare the current host-success critic against a success-and-finished critic, tracking strict success and post-success requests.
3. **Can broader embodiment training close transfer gaps?** Single-arm LIBERO training leaves coordination and reach failures (§5). Train with multiple arm configurations and coordinated dual-arm actions, then evaluate on held-out embodiments.
4. **Can estimated state replace privileged physics?** The reported noise sensitivity leaves hardware RL unresolved (§5). Test an external state estimator in simulation before controlled hardware updates, measuring credit fidelity and learning stability.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the main method and reported outcomes, but leaves material gaps:

- Many referenced tables provide captions without their contents, including detailed data composition and adapter changes.
- Full confidence intervals, training-seed variability, and a controlled critic ablation are unavailable.
- Hardware trial counts, aggregate reliability, and detailed adaptation settings are not established.
- Total perception-inclusive deployment cost and synthetic-data generation cost are unavailable.
- Claimed code, data, and weight releases cannot be verified from the supplied text alone.
