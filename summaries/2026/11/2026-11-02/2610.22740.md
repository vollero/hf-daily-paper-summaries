# R^2Orch: Recursive Self-Improvement for Agent Orchestration via Self-Play within a Skill-Internalization Loop

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.22740) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22740)


## One-sentence takeaway
R²Orch couples learned task selection with randomized skill-withdrawal tests, reporting the strongest results among evaluated baselines on twelve benchmarks with no skills supplied at deployment, although its theoretical guarantees have narrower scope than its practical training procedure.

## Problem and motivation
The authors address training an orchestrator that builds agent graphs for frozen Worker models while gradually internalizing procedural skills. They identify three gaps: reliance on human-selected curricula, loss of group-relative learning signals when rollouts all succeed or fail, and withdrawal decisions based on success rates that confound task difficulty with a skill’s benefit (§1).

The intended outcome is an orchestrator that retains performance after training-time skill prompts are removed (§3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Coupled training loops:** A Proposer selects tasks; a Director learns graph-editing actions through group-relative reinforcement learning. An outer loop revises, adds, and withdraws skills using evidence from the same rollouts (§4.1).
- **Recursive Learnability:** Task selection favors mixed success and failure outcomes when a candidate skill is hidden, targeting the learning frontier after withdrawal. The practical reward adds shrinkage, a discounted assisted-practice branch, a collapse penalty, and compute weighting (§4.2; Appendices A.3, B).
- **Randomized Skill Withdrawal:** Each task uses five rollouts, with the candidate skill hidden from three and visible to two. Correctness contrasts populate a Bayesian-bootstrap ledger; posterior decision rules govern withdrawal and admission without additional evaluation rollouts (§4.3; Table 4).
- **Supporting analysis:** The paper derives local progress relationships, conditional unbiasedness of randomized contrasts, and an accounting identity separating training gains from scaffold-change costs. These are conditional results rather than a general guarantee of recursive improvement (Appendix A).

The experiments support the combined method and its component choices within this protocol. Test sets contain 128 items each, except AIME 2026 with 30; five-run standard deviations describe run variability, not uncertainty across all possible test items (Table 1; Appendix D). The results do not establish unrestricted autonomous improvement or globally safe skill removal. Figure 5f evaluates original seed cards together, so it is corroborating dataset-level evidence rather than an isolated held-out test of each withdrawn card.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated dependence on manual initialization:** Each dataset starts with customized skills; automatic initialization for a new domain remains unresolved (Limitations).
- **Explicit theoretical boundary:** With stochastic Workers, outcome diversity can reflect execution noise rather than graph-controllable progress. The local learnability guarantee assumes graph-determined outcomes; restricted policies can access less of that diversity (Appendix A.2).
- **Explicit withdrawal boundary:** Ledgers estimate effects on adaptively selected test tasks. Policy drift, changing contexts, and mismatch with the evaluation distribution can weaken transfer of posterior bounds to deployment (Appendices A.5, A.7).
- **Practical update differs from ideal theory:** Distinct-pair sampling changes the Proposer gradient and can change stationary points relative to the ideal Gibbs curriculum. Equilibrium tracking also requires strong stability and timescale assumptions (Appendices A.6, A.8).
- **Analyst inference:** Small benchmark subsets and simulated interactive environments limit conclusions about broader task populations and real deployment. Detailed sensitivity studies for ledger windows, withdrawal tolerance, and rollout allocation are not reported in the supplied text.

## Open research questions
1. **Can seed skills be generated automatically?** Manual initialization remains necessary. Compare automatically generated, human-written, and absent seed skills under matched training budgets on a new domain.
2. **Can curricula distinguish useful diversity from Worker noise?** Appendix A.2 shows the two can differ. Repeat executions of identical graphs to estimate execution variance, then compare outcome-based and controllable-variance rewards.
3. **How reliable is withdrawal under distribution shift?** Ledger guarantees concern the tested scope. Evaluate removals on a fixed independent task panel and shifted domains, measuring harm and restoration frequency.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text includes extensive methods, ablations, and theoretical qualifications, but all empirical evidence is author-reported.

Material information absent from the text includes exact training-pool sizes and item identities, complete prompts and seed-skill contents, detailed per-baseline compute accounting, hyperparameter sensitivity results, and independently verified release artifacts.
