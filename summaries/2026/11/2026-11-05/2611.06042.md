# UniRestorer++: Scalable Universal Image Restoration with Multi-Granularity Degradation Modeling

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.06042) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06042)


## One-sentence takeaway
UniRestorer++ scales universal image restoration through hierarchical, spatially routed experts, reporting stronger out-of-distribution scaling gains than flat MoE routing over the evaluated model sizes (§IV-D).

## Problem and motivation
Universal restoration must handle diverse and combined image corruptions with one model. The authors identify two obstacles: shared dense backbones can suffer conflicting restoration objectives, while increasingly specialized experts become sensitive to degradation-estimation errors. They argue that useful scaling requires both specialization and robust routing, particularly for unfamiliar and spatially varying degradations (§I).

## Approach and contributions
- **Hierarchical capacity expansion:** Recursively cluster pooled degradation representations with K-means, then attach group-specific LoRA experts to a shared Restormer backbone. Adding hierarchy levels introduces finer specialists while retaining broader ancestor experts (§§III-C–D).
- **Adaptive granularity:** A degradation router selects a hierarchy path; a granularity router selects a level using learned uncertainty cues and restoration features. Each location uses the base block plus at most one expert residual (§III-D). The introduction explicitly identifies multi-granularity routing as inherited from the preliminary UniRestorer work; its extension into a scaling framework is central here (§I).
- **Spatial expert selection:** Train dense degradation representations using text-aligned, pixel-wise contrastive supervision on CutMix inputs, enabling different regions and network blocks to select different experts (§§III-C–E).
- **Benchmark and scaling study:** Introduce CDD-50, covering 50 synthetic settings with up to five degradation operations, and evaluate hierarchical scaling, real-world generalization, and few-shot adaptation (§§IV-A, IV-D).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Improved standard restoration accuracy | RGB PSNR: seven-task average **32.24 vs 31.81 dB**, CDD-11 **30.31 vs 30.02 dB**, and CDD-50 in-/out-of-distribution **26.54/23.46 vs 26.04/23.09 dB**, all against UniRestorer. | Tables II–IV |
| Standard-size real-world zero-shot gains | After single-degradation training, FoundIR RGB PSNR is **24.05 vs 23.80 dB** for UniRestorer; UniRestorer++ has the highest PSNR among listed methods on every real-world benchmark. | Table V |
| Hierarchical scaling improves accuracy | From **154M to 2.9B parameters**, average RGB PSNR rises **26.54→27.05 dB** on in-distribution CDD-50, **23.46→23.93 dB** out of distribution, and **26.22→28.17 dB** on FoundIR. These models train on the complete CDD-50 degradation space. | §IV-D; Table VII |
| Stronger fitted scaling under distribution shift | Hierarchy versus vanilla MoE fitted gains per tenfold parameter increase: **0.386 vs 0.133 dB** on out-of-distribution CDD-50; **1.555 vs 0.808 dB** on FoundIR. In-distribution slopes are described as similar. | §IV-D; Eq. 6 |
| Dense representations and spatial routing help | Seven-task average RGB PSNR: global-routing baseline **31.81 dB**, dense representations averaged before routing **32.03 dB**, region-aware routing **32.24 dB**. | Table XI; §V-C |
| Multi-granularity routing improves the ablation setting | On a degradation-space subset, configuration **{1,4,8}** achieves **24.46/19.45 dB** in-/out-of-distribution PSNR; single-granularity eight-group routing achieves **24.22/18.35 dB**. | Table X |
| Few-shot transfer is strong but uneven | Authors report five-shot performance exceeding compared specialists on **14 of 17 test sets**. On SIDD, five-shot RGB PSNR remains **39.26±0.13 dB**, below NAFNet’s **40.30 dB**; five-shot experiments repeat five times. | §IV-D; Tables VIII–IX |
| Routing adds measured inference overhead | For **512×512 RGB**, one A6000, averaged over **100 runs**: UniRestorer++ versus UniRestorer uses **176 vs 171M parameters**, **1255 vs 1156 GFLOPs**, and **0.515 vs 0.484 seconds**. | Table VI; §IV-C |

These results support improved fidelity on the listed benchmarks and empirical scaling within the tested range. They do not establish an unrestricted scaling law or universal superiority across metrics: for example, CDD-50 fifth-order out-of-distribution SSIM is **0.708**, below UniRestorer’s **0.713** (Table IV). Standard-size and scaled-model results also use different training settings and should not be conflated.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** The hierarchy and expert pool are constructed offline and remain fixed during inference (§VI).
- **Scaling scope:** The authors explicitly qualify the log-linear relationship as empirical. Error floors and differences between average image-wise PSNR and PSNR of expected MSE prevent a direct general scaling-law interpretation (§III-B).
- **Analyst inference—comparison fairness:** Table II mixes models trained on different task counts; Table VII uses order-specific baselines while hierarchical variants train across all orders. These comparisons do not isolate architecture alone.
- **Analyst inference—generalization scope:** Synthetic out-of-distribution evaluation changes degradation parameter ranges on Urban100-derived images. Real-world benchmarks broaden the evidence, but performance on arbitrary unseen corruption families remains unestablished (§IV-A).
- **Analyst inference—cost and reproducibility:** Training the largest model reportedly takes approximately 23 days using the stated 64-V100 setup. Approximately unchanged inference cost across scaling is asserted, but a per-size latency and memory table is absent (§§IV-B–C).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Table II describes weighted benchmark averages, whereas Table XI describes a mean over seven tasks while repeating the same averages. Several scaling passages also reference Fig. 3 although the scaling caption appears under Fig. 2.

## Open research questions
- **Does scaling persist beyond the tested range?** The fits cover a bounded parameter range. Train larger variants with matched data and optimization budgets, then test held-out size predictions and error-floor models (§§III-B, IV-D).
- **Does coarse fallback handle entirely new corruption families?** Synthetic shifts primarily concern parameter ranges. Hold out whole degradation families during training and compare hierarchical routing, finest-only routing, and oracle expert selection (§§IV-A, V-B).
- **Is spatial routing equally useful at large scale?** Its quantitative ablation uses the seven-task setting. Repeat global-versus-region routing comparisons on composite and real-world degradations at matched parameter counts (§V-C).
- **Can the hierarchy adapt without forgetting?** Fixed experts are the stated limitation. Add experts for newly collected failure cases and measure both new-domain gains and regression on held-out original tasks (§VI).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the method and reported benchmark results clearly, but leaves material details unavailable: exact synthetic parameter ranges, complete optimizer and training schedules, few-shot update budgets, uncertainty calibration, scaling-fit uncertainty, matched total training costs, per-size inference memory/latency, and confirmed availability of code, models, and benchmark files.
