# Human-to-Robot Transfer via Bridging Representation and Action Space in VLAs

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.04391) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04391)


## One-sentence takeaway
H2RB combines geometry-grounded visual conditioning with shared action latents and separate human/robot decoders to improve transfer from unpaired human demonstrations, although full-task completion remains substantially below its headline progress scores (Tables 1–2, 11).

## Problem and motivation
Adapting robot-pretrained vision-language-action policies requires costly robot demonstrations. Human demonstrations are easier to collect, but differ in appearance and motion dynamics. The authors argue that a shared physical action space alone leaves both perceptual mismatch and embodiment-dependent action realization unresolved (§1).

The setting uses human demonstrations of target behaviors alongside robot demonstrations of source tasks; it does not eliminate robot data or robot pretraining (§3.1; Appendix B.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Geometry-grounded conditioning:** A residual adapter learns to predict current wrist pose and wrist-local fingertip positions from VLM features. Each observation supplies its own geometry supervision, avoiding paired trajectories or synthesized robot images (§2.3).
- **Shared latents with separate decoding:** Reconstructed human and robot motions enter a common wrist-hand representation. A variational temporal tokenizer shares its encoder but uses separate human and robot decoders, with centroid, variance, and covariance regularization (§2.2; Appendix A.2).
- **Pretrained-policy adaptation:** A flow-matching action expert predicts frozen tokenizer latents. Deployment uses the human decoder, whose outputs are already robot-compatible, followed by inverse kinematics (§§2.2–2.3; Appendix A.3).
- **Extensions:** Additional heterogeneous training and a separately evaluated instruction-renewal pathway improve downstream scores (§§3.3–3.4). These are distinct experimental additions, not a demonstrated combined configuration.

## Evidence and main results

OpenArm scores below use a **0–100 weighted task-progress metric**, unless explicitly identified as full-task success.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Better object transfer | Across 90 Box/Ball/Towel rollouts, H2RB scores **51.8**, versus **8.0** for vanilla co-training and **27.2** for Ego-Pi shared action. | Table 1; Appendix B.3 |
| Placement-shift improvement | Across 30 OOD placement rollouts, H2RB scores **45.3**, versus **6.0** for vanilla co-training and **21.3** for Ego-Pi shared action. | Tables 1, 12 |
| Target-skill transfer | Across 15 Open and 15 Close trials, H2RB averages **62.7**, versus **0.0** for both shared-action baselines; human-only adaptation scores **29.0**. | Table 2; Appendix B.3 |
| Full completion is lower | Object full-task success is **12.2%** for H2RB versus **2.2%** for Ego-Pi; skill success is **30.0%** versus **0.0%**, over 90 and 30 trials respectively. | Table 11 |
| Components contribute incrementally | Geometry-grounded physical actions score **34.7/0.0** on object/skill transfer; adding the tokenizer yields **43.3/49.7**; latent regularization yields **51.8/62.7**, while normalized latent gap falls **3.81→1.53**. | Table 3(a); Table 12 |
| Heterogeneous training helps | With the same downstream evaluation protocol, object/skill scores rise **51.8→57.2 / 62.7→81.7**; full success rises **12.2%→21.1% / 30.0%→56.7%**. | Tables 11, 15–16 |
| Geometry helps another robot | RB-Y1 binary success over 50 parcel trials is **64%** after robot-only HT, **72%** after human-robot HT, and **78%** with geometry-grounded human-robot HT; downstream adaptation is robot-only without the tokenizer. | §3.3; Figure 5; Appendix D.4 |
| Additional instruction conditioning helps | Separately from HT, instruction renewal raises the OpenArm skill progress score **62.7→83.3**. | §3.4; Figure 7 |

These results support useful transfer in the tested settings and incremental benefits from the proposed components. They do not establish broad manipulation reliability: progress scores include partial execution, and each condition uses one trained policy (Appendices B.2, B.4). The OpenArm HT comparison measures additional heterogeneous training rather than isolating geometry grounding; the RB-Y1 human-robot comparison isolates geometry more directly (Appendices D.3–D.4).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated alignment limit:** Centroid and dispersion regularization do not guarantee correct sample-level human–robot correspondence (Appendix A.2).
- **Analyst inference—limited generalization evidence:** Controlled transfer covers three target objects and opening/closing one appliance, using same-environment data and approximately matched viewpoints. OOD evaluation changes object placement rather than environment or camera domain (Appendices B.1–B.3).
- **Analyst inference—uncertain variability:** One trained policy per condition and no reported uncertainty intervals leave training-seed variability and statistical robustness unresolved (Appendix B.4).
- **Analyst inference—unequal total training cost:** Policy fine-tuning budgets are matched, but H2RB additionally trains a tokenizer outside that budget. Compute-normalized superiority is therefore not established (Appendix A.3).
- **Reporting inconsistency:** §3.2 describes in-domain skill performance improving “from 0.0 to 81.0,” whereas Table 2 assigns zero to target skills and reports several nonzero in-domain baseline scores. Table 2 is the clearer comparison.
- **Reproducibility boundary:** Detailed recipes are supplied, but code, checkpoints, data availability, and quantitative reconstruction-error sensitivity are not established.

## Open research questions
1. **Does transfer survive larger visual shifts?** Data share an environment and similar viewpoints. Next experiment: hold tasks fixed while changing camera pose, background, and lighting.
2. **Does distribution alignment preserve task distinctions?** The authors explicitly disclaim sample-level correspondence guarantees. Next experiment: vary human/robot task-mixture imbalance and measure per-task confusion alongside latent gap.
3. **How robust are the gains across training runs and compute budgets?** Each condition uses one policy, and tokenizer training adds cost. Next experiment: repeat matched-total-compute comparisons across seeds.
4. **What prevents full completion after successful reaching or grasping?** Table 11 shows substantial attrition across progress thresholds. Next experiment: annotate contact, grasp, and execution failures and test targeted corrections.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the method and reported outcomes, but broader reliability remains uncertain. Material missing information includes repeated-seed results, uncertainty estimates, artifact-release status, reconstruction-quality sensitivity, exact physical placement bounds, and performance under substantial viewpoint or environment shifts.
