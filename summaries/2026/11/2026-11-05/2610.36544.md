# Monitor Accuracy Isn&#x27;t the Answer: A Decision-Value Framework for Evaluating AI Agent Restart Monitors

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.36544) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36544)


## One-sentence takeaway
A failure monitor’s value depends on whether its prescribed recovery outperforms continuation within the remaining budget, and this paper’s trace audit finds insufficient evidence to certify any individual restart preference under its specified simultaneous rule (§§4, 6.3).

## Problem and motivation
Failure classification does not measure whether restarting helps: an alarm can discard a trajectory that would succeed while leaving too little budget for its replacement. The authors identify a gap between monitor discrimination, recovery opportunity, and statistical evidence for acting on an alarm (§1).

They study a precise action pair: continue the current attempt or restore the original task state and run one independent fresh attempt with the same remaining budget. Context resets that preserve edits constitute a different intervention (§§1, 4.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Intervention-specific evaluation:** An alarm-to-action identity combines continuation success, failure-detection errors, and residual-budget restartability. Its contribution is operationalizing established decision theory for agent evaluation, rather than introducing restart optimization itself (§§2.4, 4.1).
- **Decision-preserving representations:** A binary-action specialization of information comparison characterizes compression loss: merging histories with opposing action advantages can reduce decision value, even if reconstruction remains faithful (§4.2).
- **Abstaining decision audit:** Simultaneous exact binomial intervals certify restart or continuation only when their action-value bounds separate; otherwise the audit abstains. It uses existing traces without additional model calls (§4.3).
- **Empirical diagnosis and artifacts:** The authors analyze externally verified coding-agent experiments and provide an accompanying analysis package, distinguishing retrospective diagnostics from prospectively frozen intervention screens (§§5–7; Appendix E).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

| Fixed accuracy does not fix restart value | Analytical control: continuation success 0.5, failure TPR 0.9, FPR 0.1 maintain 90% accuracy; changing replacement success from 0 to 0.8 changes success gain from −5 to +35 percentage points. Assumes independent clean restart and zero monitoring overhead. | §4.1, Proposition 4.1; Figure 2 |
| The initial restart gain is small and model-dependent | On seven shared tasks with a 30-call total budget, empirical cutoff-16 replay raises Luna success from 82.86% to 85.14%: +2.29 points, hierarchical bootstrap interval [0.00, 6.86]. For DeepSeek, success falls from 68.57% to 26.29%: −42.29 points, interval [−69.14, −17.71]. | §6.1; Figure 3 |
| The selected cutoff does not replicate prospectively | In the new Luna cohorts, both policies achieve 100% on 35 easy-cohort attempts; cutoff 16 achieves 0% versus 4% for cutoff 30 on 25 difficulty-enriched attempts. Across all 19 Luna tasks, the task-macro difference is −0.21 points, interval [−4.21, 2.53]. | §6.2; Table 6 |
| The apparent initial gain has narrow support | At call 16, one surviving failed Luna attempt has estimated continuation success 0 and fresh success 4/5 within the remaining 14 calls; its weighted contribution supplies the entire +2.29-point initial gain. | §6.3, Eq. 22 |
| No individual comparison is certified | Retrospective audit: 130 traces, 156 task-time comparisons, 103 with live data; zero live comparisons receive a sign certificate under the simultaneous rule with α = 0.05. | §§5.8, 6.3; Figure 4 |
| Longer horizons yield no observed late completion | Among 20 fresh 60-call attempts on four previously unsolved tasks, none first passes during calls 31–60; 17 terminate after repeated invalid tool responses. | §6.4; Table 8 |
| Context recovery changes failure mode without improving success | Under a shared 60-call cap, baseline succeeds on 1/20 attempts versus 0/20 for treatment; task-clustered bootstrap difference −5 points, range [−20, 0]. Mean metered cost increases 56.7%. Authors flag the interval’s boundary degeneracy. | §6.5; Figure 5 |
| Bounded failed-branch reuse produces no verified solution | None of 19 selected verifier-blind patch composites passes; all four semantic-resolver attempts fail under a 12-call cap. The resolver comparison is not a randomized effect estimate. | §6.6 |

The mathematics establishes conditional relationships, while the experiments establish outcomes for the specified runtimes and interventions. The replay gains are estimated policy values, not direct randomized deployment outcomes. Audit abstention does not establish equal action values or optimal continuation, and these negative screens do not invalidate richer learned monitors or different recovery actions (§§5.8, 7.1–7.3).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope:** All tasks are selected SymPy issues; later interventions use one model and one harness. Pooled success rates do not estimate general SWE-bench or production-agent performance (§7.3).
- **Sparse and retrospective evidence:** Few attempts remain after conditioning on survival. The audit was designed after inspecting the experiments, so it is not prospective validation of its coverage guarantee (§§5.8, 7.3).
- **Sampling assumptions:** Provider correlation, routing changes, temporal drift, distribution shift, or uncorrected repeated checking can undermine certification assumptions (§7.3; Appendix F).
- **Outcome and cost limits:** Finite hidden tests measure verifier success rather than complete issue correctness. Per-action verification overhead is excluded from the model-call budget and reported model costs (§7.3).
- **Missing direct monitor evaluation:** There are no matched prefix-conditioned continuation/restart trials, learned-monitor comparisons, or empirical tests of decision-preserving compression (§7.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Because the empirical audit conditions on task and elapsed calls rather than rich trace features, it cannot determine which observable features would support a useful monitor; this follows directly from the stated evaluation design (§§5.8, 7.3).

## Open research questions
1. **Can a learned monitor improve success after accounting for recovery opportunity?** This paper does not test one. A next experiment could freeze a monitor and recovery semantics, then compare it with continuation and calibrated timeouts on held-out tasks with measured restartability (§7.4).
2. **Can certification become more sample-efficient?** The specified conservative procedure certifies no live comparison. A next experiment could prospectively evaluate hierarchical pooling against task-specific intervals, checking coverage and decision regret (§§6.3, 7.2).
3. **Does decision-preserving compression improve agent control?** The criterion is theoretical here. A next experiment could compare compressed representations using matched continuation/restart trials and measured action-value loss (§§4.2, 7.3).
4. **Do results transfer across runtimes?** Model heterogeneity is observed, but repository coverage is narrow. A next experiment could repeat frozen comparisons on another repository and model while charging monitoring and recovery overhead (§§7.3–7.4).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the paper’s stated conditional conclusions; limited confidence in generalization beyond its selected settings.

The supplied text omits table bodies, including full task lists and artifact-manifest entries, and provides no direct RestartLab download location. Raw trajectories, exact prompts, complete per-attempt records, and matched prefix-conditioned intervention outcomes are unavailable; the authors explicitly state that several sensitive materials are excluded from the sanitized package (Appendix E).
