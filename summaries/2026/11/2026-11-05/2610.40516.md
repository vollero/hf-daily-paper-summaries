# How I Aligned Your Model: Scaling Up Multilingual Safety Training with Templates

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-05.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.40516) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40516)


## One-sentence takeaway

MSafeTemp scales multilingual safety data through translated templates and culturally grounded slot fills, exposing model-dependent safety gaps and improving attack resistance after training, with uneven effects on over-refusal and general capabilities (§§3–7).

## Problem and motivation

English-centered safety training and evaluation can miss harmful compliance in other languages, especially where culturally specific entities matter. The authors identify three obstacles: scarce multilingual safety data, costly native-speaker red-teaming, and synthetic generators that refuse to translate or adapt harmful requests. Translation alone also preserves English-centric referents (§1).

The proposed gap is therefore both linguistic and cultural: models need evaluation and training that jointly address harmful compliance and refusal of legitimate requests.

## Approach and contributions

- **Controlled augmentation pipeline:** Replace harm-bearing phrases with typed slots, validate and translate the remaining template, then generate generic harmful fills (TR), benign controls (OR), and selectively localized cultural fills (PH). Separate generator and judge models validate the outputs (§§3, A.1–A.3).
- **Reusable corpus and cultural resources:** MSafeTemp contains a WildGuardMix-derived training/test track in 44 languages and an evaluation-only HarmBench track in 60 languages. Splits follow source seeds, keeping all descendants together. The authors report releasing data, code, cultural cards, and validation metadata (§§4, A.4, A.7).
- **Evaluation and training evidence:** Matched arms test what translation and localization expose; SFT followed by DPO tests whether generated safety supervision improves resistance while preserving helpfulness (§§5–6, F–G).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

| Human validation supports fluency more strongly than benign labels | Across 600 judgments in 11 languages, naturalness averaged 4.43/5 and cultural fit 4.46/5; raters endorsed 91% of harmful labels and 65% of benign labels. | §4, “Human validation” |

| Localization adds distinct failures without consistently increasing ASR | In the twelve-language frozen-template ablation, generic and cultural arms had broken-behavior Jaccard overlap of 0.40–0.45; approximately 45% of failures unique to one arm occurred only under cultural fills. | F.2; Table 42 discussion |
| Training sharply improves wrapped attack resistance, with residual refusal costs | Apertus-8B-Instruct-2509: wrapped HarmBench ASR fell from 31.8% to 0.2% after SFT+DPO, while wrapped benign OR rose from 5.3% to 8.5%. | Independent judges support major directions, but disagree on individual safety labels | On a stratified 5,000-response check, harmful-response agreement with the main judge had raw Cohen’s κ of 0.40 and 0.46 for two alternative judges; IT-to-DPO ASR reductions retained their direction across both tested families and tracks. | F.5; Table 48 |

These results support broader evaluation coverage and useful safety supervision within the tested settings. They do not establish universal safety transfer or uniformly improved helpfulness. In particular, uncertain benign labels complicate OR interpretation, and localization’s value is additional failure coverage rather than consistently stronger attacks.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Automated judging dominates validation and evaluation. Independent checks preserve major trends, but attack-success agreement is moderate and a full judge ablation remains future work (§7; F.5).
- **Author-stated:** Human cultural validation covers only a subset of languages with few respondents per language. Benign-label disagreements may inflate measured over-refusal (§§4, 7).
- **Author-stated:** Harm categories are inherited from English seeds; localization can change severity without changing the inherited label (§7).
- **Author-stated:** The method covers single-turn, fixed-template prompts and drops harms that cannot be expressed through short slot values. Localized coverage is selective (§7; A.2; Table 4).
- **Analyst inference:** Large row counts should not be read as equally many independent harmful behaviors: variants descend from a limited seed set. The paper appropriately uses source-family bootstrap intervals (§A.4; F.4).
- **Analyst inference:** Training benefits are not universal across languages or metrics: Table 55 includes an Amharic ASR increase for Olmo, while Tables 50 and 57 show capability and refusal regressions.

## Open research questions

- **Would native-speaker adjudication change model rankings or OR estimates?** Benign-label endorsement is limited. Next experiment: adjudicate stratified direct controls across resource tiers and recompute rankings (§4).
- **Which data components drive the gains?** The supplied results do not isolate the contributions of cultural fills, generic fills, and benign controls. Next experiment: equal-budget component ablations with shared seeds and training settings (§§3, 6).
- **Do benefits survive adaptive, multi-turn attacks?** Current prompts are single-turn templates. Next experiment: use held-out localized prompts as starting points for budget-matched iterative attacks (§7).
- **Can capability losses be reduced while retaining safety gains?** Reported training changes reduce some capability scores. Next experiment: vary safety-data proportions and training intensity while tracking ASR, OR, and capability jointly (§6; Table 50).

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the core method and several concrete results, but the source was truncated, so coverage is incomplete.

Material missing information includes omitted table bodies—especially aggregate training results and confidence intervals—parts of the human-study protocol, full generation settings, and detailed training-data blend counts. Appendix G.1 also leaves ambiguity between its general description of model-specific on-policy sampling and Table 49’s statement that all DPO pairs came from Apertus. Released artifacts were not independently inspected.
