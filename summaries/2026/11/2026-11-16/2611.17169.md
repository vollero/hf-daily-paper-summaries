# Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errors in Small-Model Semantic Parsing

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.17169) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17169)


## One-sentence takeaway
On PIZZA, plain-word target encodings reduce well-formed but incorrect outputs across two small models, while matched-length comparisons indicate that shortening alone does not explain the improvement (§§6.2–6.3).

## Problem and motivation
A semantic parser can produce a valid tree that misrepresents an order, allowing an error to pass downstream undetected. The authors investigate whether output representation controls these “silent” errors, separating token length from surface vocabulary—two factors ordinarily confounded when comparing formats (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled representations:** Six lossless encodings of identical trees, including approximately matched-length pairs: native s-expressions versus JSON, and symbolic DSL versus ordinary words (§4).
- **Paired fine-tuning study:** LoRA training of Qwen3.5-0.8B and LFM2.5-350M across five training-pool sizes and five seeds, totaling 300 runs. Paired hierarchical bootstrapping and Holm correction assess differences (§5).
- **Reliability accounting:** Every test output is classified as correct, silently incorrect, unparseable, or a refusal. Synthetic off-menu negatives and spoken self-corrections are included in every run (§§3, 5).
- **Reported reusable assets:** The authors state that code, analysis scripts, and trained models are released (§1; Appendix A).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Words reduces silent errors relative to native s-expressions. | At the full pool, mean silent-error rates over five seeds on 1,357 human-written orders: Qwen **4.5% versus 10.3%**; LFM2 **10.0% versus 17.9%**. Authors report significant differences at every pool size for this comparison. | Table 2; §6.3 |
| Words maintains competitive exact match with shorter outputs. | Same setting: Qwen words **87.5% ± 0.8 seed SD**, exr **85.8% ± 1.6**; mean generated tokens **10.9 versus 44.3**. Words–exr exact-match differences do not separate on Qwen at any pool size. | Table 2; §6.2 |
| Matched-length results favor natural vocabulary. | Words beats DSL in exact match in all **10 model × pool-size cells**, separating in **9**. At the full pool, gains are **7.8 percentage points on Qwen** and **5.4 on LFM2**; LFM2 words emits **12.0 versus 11.4 tokens**. | §6.2 |
| Negation contributes to the silent-error reduction. | At the full pool on Qwen, silent negation errors affect **0.2% of orders with words versus 2.0% with exr**. | §6.3; Figure 4 |
| Guardrails reject negatives but also genuine orders. | Across full-pool formats, held-out negative refusal is **95.9–99.0% on Qwen** and **97.2–98.3% on LFM2**, evaluated on 614 negatives per run. Genuine-order refusal is **2.1–5.4%** and **6.3–10.1%**, respectively. | §6.4; Table 4 caption; Table 2 |
| Correction generalization remains imperfect. | On 600 test-derived orders with held-out correction cues, exact match spans **62.9–75.3% on Qwen** and **49.2–62.6% on LFM2** across formats; words ranks highest on both. | §6.4 |
| Shorter outputs improve measured latency. | At the full pool, batch-one median latency averaged over seeds: words/exr **317/1,171 ms on Qwen**, **186/821 ms on LFM2**, measured on a GPU. | Table 2; §6.6 |

The paired comparisons support a representation effect within this task and training setup. They do not establish that natural vocabulary universally improves parsing or directly prove a pretraining-based mechanism. Lower silent-error rates also partly reflect more detectable failures: on Qwen, words has **4.6% unparseable outputs versus 0.6% for exr** at the full pool (Table 2).

The prose following Table 2 is ambiguous about silent-rate significance against other formats; §6.3 explicitly reports separation for words versus exr at every pool size. Broader pairwise significance should therefore be treated cautiously.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** One dataset with a small grammar vocabulary and two sub-billion models; transfer to other tasks or larger models remains untested (Limitations).
- **Guardrail confounding:** Every run includes synthetic negatives and corrections, so the study does not isolate their effect on ordinary parsing accuracy. Correction-cue overlap affects some original test orders (Limitations).
- **Synthetic robustness evaluation:** Negatives, corrections, and probes are author-generated or handwritten; held-out vocabulary and cues provide limited evidence about natural deployment inputs (§3; Limitations).
- **Training and tuning:** Adapter rank is fixed, some learning-rate selections lie at grid boundaries, and some LFM2 runs approach the step limit. Larger pools increase available diversity, not maximum examples consumed (§5; Limitations).
- **Deployment fidelity:** Exported LFM2 GGUF agrees with its trained run on only 93% of orders; reported research results use the trained model (Limitations).
- **Analyst inference:** “Silent” means undetectable by the paper’s parse/refusal checks. The study does not evaluate additional semantic validation or human review (§5).

## Open research questions
- **Does the format advantage transfer?** Only PIZZA and small models are tested. Repeat matched-format comparisons on another semantic-parsing task and larger models, retaining all four outcome metrics.
- **How much does guardrail training alter the format ranking?** No guardrail-free ablation is reported. Compare identical format runs with neither augmentation, each augmentation separately, and both.
- **Can refusal become less costly?** Strict refusal rejects genuine human phrasing. Compare the current rule against human-written negatives and softer handling of greetings, measuring both false refusals and accepted off-menu requests.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the central comparisons and describes substantial integrity checks, but several referenced tables contain captions without their numerical bodies.

Missing information includes complete pool-size results and confidence intervals, detailed guardrail and error-category tables, full encoding examples, explicit decoding settings, and raw outputs. Release contents were not independently inspected.
