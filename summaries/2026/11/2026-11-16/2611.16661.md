# Reducing Batch Makespan for LLM Agent Workloads through Inference-Environment Coordination

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.16661) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16661)


## One-sentence takeaway
Coordinating hybrid-model cache allocation, trajectory-aware inference scheduling, and constrained environment execution reduces live agent-batch makespan by 10.4%–65.0% across the tested Ling model–benchmark combinations, with broadly similar reported evaluation scores (§5.4, Tables 2–3).

## Problem and motivation
Multi-turn agent tasks alternate between inference and environment interaction, while development workflows often wait for every task in a batch to finish. Optimizing individual request latency therefore does not necessarily minimize batch completion time (§1).

The authors identify three interacting bottlenecks: mismatched KV-cache and recurrent-state allocations, routing and queueing that undermine cross-turn cache reuse, and shared concurrency limits that leave inference underused while protecting constrained environments. Long trajectories can then dominate the batch tail (§§1, 3–4).

## Approach and contributions
- **Workload-aware cache allocation:** Estimate per-trajectory cache footprints from historical trajectories, balance KV-cache and recurrent-state residency, and derive a reference concurrency. Allocation remains fixed during serving; local calibration is optional (§3.1).
- **Trajectory-aware inference policies:** Load-bounded consistent hashing (LBCH) balances worker affinity against in-flight request counts. Routing-prefix (RP) scheduling selectively queries prefix caches using recent trajectory labels, reducing scheduling work while promoting reuse (§§3.2–3.4).
- **Inference–environment coordination:** Separate sample admission from environment execution capacity. Chain-Advancing Action Scheduling (CAAS) orders environment jobs by arrival band, observed trajectory depth, and bucketed inference duration to advance potentially long chains (§4).
- **Controlled evaluation:** Replay performs actual inference with fixed historical environment responses, durations, and output lengths; live experiments evaluate the integrated system (§5.1).

The distinctive contribution is this coordinated design and its trajectory-label and observed-progress heuristics, rather than prefix caching or consistent hashing themselves (§2).

Controlled replay supports mechanism comparisons under fixed stage structure, but does not evaluate semantic correctness. Live results support integrated efficiency gains under variable trajectories. The authors describe scores as aligned; the supplied results do not establish statistical quality equivalence. Live MCP-Atlas tail durations also depend on which tasks finish last, so they cannot alone compare admission policies (§§5.1, 5.4).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated modeling limits:** Mean final-turn cache demand is not a strict upper bound. The capacity model ignores allocation granularity and fragmentation, and its balancing ratio may require calibration (§3.1).
- **Author-stated scheduling limits:** RP labels can disagree with actual cache residency, and stored prefix-hit lengths can become stale (§3.3).
- **Replay selection limits:** SWE replay includes only successful historical executions; coordination replay likewise uses successful trajectories. Replay fixes environment behavior and excludes semantic validation (§5.1, Appendix A).
- **Analyst inference:** Generalization remains uncertain beyond two Ling models, H20 deployments, and the evaluated worker configurations. Hierarchical caches and latency-constrained serving remain future work (§§5–6).
- **Analyst inference:** Live comparisons combine several changes; replay isolates components, but live results do not fully attribute gains to each one. Three-run means are reported without dispersion or statistical significance estimates (Tables 2–3).
- **Practical tradeoff:** Relative to the tuned original SWE baseline, Opt improves batch makespan while increasing request-level TTFT and TPOT (Table A3).

## Open research questions
1. **How robust is static allocation to workload drift?** Historical-footprint stability is assumed, and mean demand can misrepresent active trajectories. Test allocation learned from one checkpoint on later checkpoints and shifted task mixtures (§3.1).
2. **When does observed depth reliably identify batch-ending chains?** CAAS uses completed turns rather than remaining work. Test mixtures with weak or reversed relationships between observed depth and remaining execution, sweeping arrival-band width (§4.2).
3. **Can these gains coexist with latency guarantees?** Live SWE exposes a makespan–request-latency tradeoff. Compare configurations under explicit TTFT and TPOT constraints (§6, Table A3).
4. **Do benefits persist with larger distributed caches?** Evaluated routing uses worker-local reuse without cache transfer. Compare LBCH/RP against transfer-aware policies across more workers and cache tiers (§3.2, §6).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported settings and descriptive results; **medium** in broader generalization and quality preservation.

Material missing information includes run-level variability, statistical quality-equivalence tests, public availability of implementation and replay traces, CAAS hyperparameter sensitivity, and evaluations with heterogeneous execution resources or larger worker fleets.
