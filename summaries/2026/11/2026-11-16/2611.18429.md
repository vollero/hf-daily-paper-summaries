# Do Underwater Image Quality Metrics Agree with Human Perception? A Benchmark with Human Choices, Simple Edits and Generative Drift

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.18429) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18429)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across five human references, the authors find that underwater image quality metrics lack consistent perceptual agreement and can reward simple edits, adversarial perturbations, or generative drift (§§4–6).

## Problem and motivation

Underwater enhancement lacks clean reference images, so evaluations commonly rely on no-reference image quality assessment (NR-IQA). These scores are useful only if they rank alternative enhancements of the same scene as people do. The authors identify a gap in combined evaluation across human references, laboratories, score manipulation, generative drift, and metric implementations; they explicitly acknowledge earlier evidence of perceptual disagreement (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Within-scene benchmark:** Compare 73 metric configurations against four published opinion-score datasets and a new paired-choice study. Agreement uses Cohen’s κ over alternatives of the same scene, with bootstrap intervals; cross-laboratory evaluation separates training-set performance from generalization (§§3–4).
- **Manipulation tests:** Compare simple edits and PGD-attacked raw images against each scene’s highest human-rated enhancement. The new human study separately tests raw-versus-edit preferences (§5).
- **Generative drift evaluation:** Repeatedly copy or enhance underwater photos using two editors, measuring score changes alongside noise and color shifts (§6).
- **TrustUIQA release:** The authors report releasing choices, scores, agreement tables, generated images, and reconstruction scripts. The contribution is this combined evaluation resource, rather than a new quality predictor (§8).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No metric agrees consistently across references | Best minimum within-scene κ across five references: Q-Align 0.23; NR-UIQA 0.22, with overlapping intervals. Study rater-pair median κ: 0.41, offered as context rather than an upper bound. | §4.1; Fig. 2 |
| Learned underwater metrics generalize poorly across laboratories | Five metrics with known training references achieve κ 0.60–0.92 on training data, versus at most 0.31 on other laboratories’ data. Removing shared photos changes κ by at most 0.02. | §4.2; Table 2 |
| UCIQE sometimes disagrees systematically with human rankings | Within-scene κ is −0.08 on SAUD, −0.21 on SAUD2.0, and −0.17 on SOTA; all three confidence intervals exclude zero. These values use the authors’ NumPy implementation. | §4.1; Table S7 |
| Simple edits often beat human-selected enhancements under UCIQE | S+S+C scores above the highest-opinion-score enhancement in 60%, 86%, and 95% of UID2021, SAUD, and SOTA scenes, respectively; evaluated on 60, 100, and 200 scenes. | §§3.4, 5.1; Table 3 |
| Direct human choices expose clear edit failures | For the S+S+C chain repeated 20 times, only 1% of human votes favor the edit over raw, while UCIQE favors it on every study photo. Study: 32 photos and 20 raters. | §§3.1, 5.2; Fig. 3 |
| Small targeted attacks reliably raise scores | Ten PGD steps with L∞ ε = 2/255 make attacked raw images outscore the best human-rated enhancement in all 150 tested scenes for each of five attacked metrics. Attacked-image PSNR remains at least 43.8 dB. | §5.3; Table 4 |
| Drift detection depends strongly on the criterion | On Pro replica chains over 20 photos, 22/73 scores fall below step 1 at every checkpoint; 38/73 fall by the final step. Only five scores satisfy the checkpoint criterion on all four chain settings. | §§3.4, 6; Tables 5, S9 |
| Implementation materially changes UCIQE results | Public MATLAB code run in Octave returns NaN on 27%–38% of opinion-score images. After patching, its κ differs from the NumPy port by up to 0.16. | §7; Table 6 |

These results support caution about using higher scores as evidence of better perceptual quality. They do **not** establish that every edit beating a published enhancement looks worse: those comparisons lacked direct human judgments. Likewise, measured noise and color change do not establish perceived degradation. The drift criterion requires scores below the step-1 baseline at each checkpoint, not a monotonic decline between checkpoints (§§5–6).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ stated limitations:** The new study uses a small, single-university population, uncontrolled screens, and uneven rater blocks. Its reference is the raw image, with no direct edit-versus-best-enhancement or early-versus-late-drift comparisons (§8).
- **Agreement sensitivity:** Near-equal opinion scores still generate pairs. Pair ordering changes absolute κ somewhat; excluding metric ties particularly affects VisualQuality-R1. Metric ranking differences are descriptive and were not significance-tested (§§3.3, 4.1).
- **Restricted stress tests:** Edits and attacks cover subsets of scenes; attacks target five general-purpose metrics rather than learned underwater metrics. Drift covers two editors and two prompts, whose outputs may change with versions (§8).
- **Analyst inference:** Counts of passing or failing “metrics” should not be interpreted as counts of independent model families, because checkpoints and UIQM components receive separate entries (§3.2).
- **Causal uncertainty:** The proposed explanation linking learned-metric transformation sensitivity to perceptual disagreement remains a hypothesis (§7).

## Open research questions

1. **Do score-winning edits actually beat the best enhancement perceptually?** That comparison was not collected (§8). Run blinded paired choices between each edit and the highest-rated enhancement.
2. **Does the drift criterion track human judgments?** No early-versus-late human comparisons were performed (§8). Collect separate judgments of quality and content preservation across checkpoints.
3. **What causes the cross-laboratory gap?** Removing shared photos barely changes agreement, leaving its cause unresolved (§4.2). Train controlled variants across laboratories with matched scene splits and rating protocols.
4. **Can transformation stability improve perceptual agreement?** The proposed causal link is untested (§7). Compare original metrics with transformation-averaged variants on held-out human references.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** in the reported central findings, with narrower confidence in their generalization beyond the tested settings.

The supplied text omits the bodies of several referenced tables, including detailed attack results and the complete identities of the five attacked metrics. It also lacks direct human judgments for drift and edit-versus-best-enhancement comparisons. Release availability and reproducibility are author-reported and were not independently verified.
