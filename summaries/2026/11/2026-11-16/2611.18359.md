# Envisioning Agentic Robotics: A New Era of Robotic Systems

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.18359) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18359)


## One-sentence takeaway
The authors propose a functional architecture and evaluation framework for foundation-model-driven robots, arguing that verified physical execution under real-time constraints matters more than model capability alone.

## Problem and motivation
Robots must translate user intent into physical outcomes despite changing scenes, incomplete observations, mechanical limits, and potentially irreversible actions. The authors argue that task-specific policies struggle with open-ended generalization, while emerging foundation-model-based systems lack a shared definition and comparable evaluation protocols (§1).

Their central methodological concern is attribution: reported success can depend on the model, harness, tools, privileged information, action budgets, and whether simulation pauses during reasoning (§§4–4.3).

## Approach and contributions
This is primarily a conceptual framework and evidence synthesis; the supplied text does not establish a new trained policy or original experimental benchmark.

- **Functional architecture:** S2 handles intent, decisions, orchestration, and outcome verification; S1 supplies callable skills; S0 maintains body control. A separate event monitor supports interruption and protective responses (§2.1; Figs. 3–4).
- **Operational definition of Embodied S2:** A foundation model and embodied harness jointly make externally identifiable decisions about both how to proceed and whether outcomes satisfy goals. The authors identify this testable criterion and inclusion of the harness as distinctions from prior definitions (§3.1).
- **Harness specification:** Context, tools, planning, memory, and verification support execution contracts that distinguish accepted requests, terminated execution, verified effects, and committed progress (§§2.2, 3.3).
- **Evaluation framework:** Separate model-level capability from system-level performance across nine dimensions and 53 sub-dimensions, with controlled interfaces, independent verification, matched budgets, and live-world execution (§§4.1–4.2; Fig. 9).
- **Roadmap:** Progress from primitives to tasks, workflows, and standing roles. Only its introductory description is available; the detailed roadmap is omitted by truncation (§1; Fig. 1).

## Evidence and main results
All empirical numbers below are **prior-study results reported by the authors**, not experiments reproduced in this paper (§4.3).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| General models can outperform trained policies on selected simulated suites. | RoboLab-120: Astra direct prompting / agentic session success was 66.3% / 74.8%, versus 51.0% for the best leaderboard policy. These are reported system comparisons, not an isolated model effect. | Table 8 |
| Strong episode memory performance can hide substantial latency. | Frozen Astra achieved 92.1% success over 800 RoboMME test episodes in one run, keeping the episode in context; calls averaged 18.3 seconds while simulation paused. | §3.3 |
| Interface choice can help or constrain the same model. | EmbodiedSkillBench: Astra success was 92% with native control, 61% with selected skills only, and 85% with skills available on demand. | §4.3; Table 8 |
| Live execution changes safety conclusions. | RT-Safe matched hard routes across eight VLMs: safe success fell from 19.8% paused to 0.7% live; collisions increased 12.3-fold while task completion remained essentially unchanged. | §4.2(iii); Table 8 |
| Broader tasks remain difficult. | Reported success was 19.0% for Astra on RobotWorld and 23.2% on RoboQuest; the best of nine VLMs achieved 16.8% on HumanCLAW using whole-body skills. | §4.3; Table 8 |
| Text refusal does not reliably transfer to robot action. | Pooled across the same six frontier models in RoboHarm, refusal was 72% for text requests versus 18% in real-robot trials with end-effector control. | §4.3; Table 8 |

These results support the authors’ emphasis on interfaces, verification, timing, and action-level safety. They do **not** establish universal superiority over learned policies, sustained physical autonomy, or reliable cross-paper rankings: settings differ, some simulations pause, and some comparisons provide unequal information (§4.3).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated evidence limits:** Reviewed results were not reproduced; many come from recent preprints, single runs, or few seeds. Cross-paper rankings and reliability claims are therefore unsupported (§4.3).
- **Authors’ stated comparison risks:** Harnesses, action interfaces, privileged observations, task guidance, and budgets differ. Small model-by-harness studies do not establish which factor generally contributes more (§§4.2–4.3).
- **Authors’ stated coverage gaps:** Real-world continuous operation, long horizons, cross-session memory, and real-user intent fulfillment remain largely unmeasured. The memory benchmarks discussed do not test body or user memory (§§4.1, 4.3).
- **Analyst inference:** The framework offers useful experimental controls, but the supplied text does not demonstrate that adopting the complete framework improves deployment reliability.
- **Source limitation:** The source is truncated near the beginning of §4.4. Detailed later limitations, digital-to-physical analysis, roadmap, conclusions, and much appendix content are unavailable; several table bodies are also missing.

## Open research questions
- **Which harness components improve reliability across models?** Existing factorial studies are small (§4.3). A next experiment could cross several fixed models with independently varied memory, verification, and interface components under matched tasks and budgets.
- **Can concise reasoning preserve decision quality within physical deadlines?** The authors explicitly leave its correctness benefit open (§3.4). Compare reasoning budgets on matched live tasks, measuring safe success, observation age, and missed deadlines.
- **How should persistent memory handle changing worlds and users?** Cross-session evidence is sparse, and discussed benchmarks omit body and user memory (§§4.1, 4.3). Test repeated sessions with moved objects, revised preferences, and changed body capabilities.
- **How can safety survive interruptions and uncertain execution status?** Executable protocols remain incomplete for cancellation, recovery, and takeover (§4.1). Inject interruptions and delayed acknowledgments, then independently score stopping behavior, duplicate actions, and safe recovery.
