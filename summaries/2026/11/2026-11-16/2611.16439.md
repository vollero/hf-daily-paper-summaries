# HALO: Human-Aligned Data Collection for WhoLe-body MObile Manipulation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.16439) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16439)


## One-sentence takeaway
HALO combines direct kinesthetic teaching, a passive mobile platform, and deployment-aligned data processing to collect faster whole-body demonstrations and train policies that succeed on selected dexterous and long-horizon tasks.

## Problem and motivation
Whole-body mobile manipulation requires demonstrations that continuously coordinate two arms with base motion. The authors identify a trade-off in existing interfaces: indirect teleoperation limits physical feedback, leader–follower systems separate the operator from the robot’s viewpoint and end-effectors, and retargeting approaches introduce action-transfer requirements (§§1–2).

HALO aims to preserve direct robot control, aligned perception and action, and natural operator movement simultaneously. Its central challenge is that close physical teaching and base-free collection create visual and control differences from autonomous deployment (§3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Human-aligned collection hardware:** Operators physically guide redesigned robot grippers while moving a low-resistance passive platform. Fixed-direction wheels reproduce the deployed differential-drive base’s nonholonomic constraints (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Deployment-aligned processing:** A hand-segmentation, temporal-propagation, and video-inpainting pipeline removes demonstrator hands. Encoder motion is mapped to deployment-compatible base velocities, with response-delay compensation (§3.3).
- **Integrated policy evaluation:** The authors fine-tune pretrained π0.5 using camera observations and robot state, jointly predicting arm commands and base velocities. Asynchronous real-time action chunking supports continuous execution. These are adopted components; the contribution is their evaluation with HALO demonstrations (§3.4; §4).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

These results support HALO’s effectiveness on the tested hardware and tasks, including a controlled comparison of visual processing. They do not isolate every hardware contribution or establish broad operator and environment generalization. The authors describe zip-tie manipulation as requiring sub-millimeter accuracy, but provide no direct positional-error measurement (§4.2). Generalization examples are qualitative, without reported success rates (§4.4; Fig. 11).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-identified deployment gaps:** Hand visibility and passive-platform dynamics require correction before deployment (§3.3). The authors propose active perception as future work (§5).
- **Analyst inference—operator coverage:** The interface comparison uses one novice operator, leaving population-level usability and learning effects unresolved (§4.1).
- **Analyst inference—limited precision evidence:** Zip-tie success is low, and task completion substitutes for direct measurement of manipulation accuracy (§4.2).
- **Analyst inference—component attribution:** No quantitative ablation separately tests base-delay compensation, affine calibration, passive-platform design, or gripper redesign (§§3–4).
- **Analyst inference—comparison and reproducibility gaps:** Equal collection time measures practical collection-to-policy performance, but different valid-data yields prevent isolating demonstration quality from quantity. Dataset sizes, several trial counts, uncertainty estimates, and processing costs are absent.
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Table 2 labels its comparator “ALOHA,” while the accompanying discussion identifies Mobile ALOHA (§4.2).

## Open research questions
- **Does the collection advantage persist across operators?** Only one operator was compared. A counterbalanced multi-operator study could measure success, collection speed, and learning curves.
- **Which deployment corrections are essential?** Base alignment lacks a quantitative ablation. Independently disabling calibration, delay compensation, and hand removal could separate their effects on coordination.
- **How robust is the learned behavior beyond demonstrated settings?** Generalization is illustrated qualitatively. Held-out rooms and chair–table configurations with repeated trials could quantify it.
- **Can dexterity become reliable?** Zip-tie success remains limited. A controlled demonstration-budget sweep with measured insertion error could distinguish data limitations from control limitations.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly specifies the system and several results, but evidence for broader reliability is limited.

Material missing information includes demonstration counts, most evaluation trial counts, statistical uncertainty, quantitative generalization results, direct precision measurements, calibration coefficients and measured delays, processing/training resource costs, and confirmed code, dataset, or hardware-release locations.
