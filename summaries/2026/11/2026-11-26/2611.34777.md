# When Every Rollout Fails: Splicing Learner-Conditioned Options into Long-Horizon Agent RL

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.34777) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34777)


## One-sentence takeaway

SPLICE turns all-fail GRPO groups into local training targets by testing short proposed corrections with the learner’s own continuations, improving benchmark scores over direct GRPO by 2.8–9.8 percentage points across four evaluated settings (Abstract, §5.2).

## Problem and motivation

When every rollout earns zero terminal reward, GRPO’s reward-based gradient vanishes even if a short correction could let the learner finish the task (§§1, 3). The authors identify two costs of supplying complete solutions from a stronger model. Full trajectories require expensive generation and expose the learner to long histories generated by another policy. The gap is how to focus guidance on failures the learner encounters and evaluate whether it can act successfully after receiving that guidance (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Branch from a learner failure.** For each all-fail group, sample one failed trajectory and ask a proposal model to select a resumable point using the history and verifier feedback. Generate several bounded options there. Mixed-reward groups use GRPO, and all-success groups are skipped (§4, Fig. 1).
- **Value options under the learner.** Resume each nonterminal option with repeated rollouts from the current policy snapshot and estimate its value from terminal success. Verify terminal options directly and discard options with zero estimated return (§4.1).
- **Fit a local, return-weighted target.** Softmax the standardized returns of retained options, then train on their action tokens using a per-option mean token loss. The authors prove that this weighting achieves at least the uniform target’s mean *observed* return on the same retained set (§4.2, Proposition 1, Appendix A).

The experiments support benchmark gains under shared training-task access and a proposal-cost advantage over the tested full-trajectory methods. Training compute differed by method, so the comparisons leave the equal-compute ranking open. The proposition concerns observed returns over fixed candidates, leaving true return after a policy update unproven.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** The default three continuations per nonterminal option introduce sampling error. Additional continuations increase inference cost, and the wall-clock overhead could grow in environments with expensive execution (§6).
- **Analyst inference.** Recovery, mechanism, and timing analyses use SWE with Qwen3-8B-CS, so their findings may differ for Deep Research or other learners. Graduation is estimated from a single rollout per task on a training subset, leaving per-task reliability and held-out recovery unresolved (§§5.1, 5.3–5.5).
- **Analyst inference.** The full-suffix ablation supports the authors’ concern about longer proposal-generated histories, but it provides no direct measurement of covariate shift (§5.5).

## Open research questions

1. **Can adaptive continuation sampling improve option selection at fixed cost?** The default uses three continuations, and §6 flags sampling error. Compare fixed sampling with adaptive allocation under the same total rollout budget, using a larger independent sample to check option rankings.
2. **Does the benchmark advantage persist at equal end-to-end compute?** The main experiment matches task access, while timing evidence comes from SWE (§§5.1, 5.4). Compare score-versus-time curves under equal hardware and wall-clock budgets on both benchmarks.
3. **Do the same mechanisms explain the gains in Deep Research?** The return-weighting and splice-point ablations are SWE-only (§5.5, Appendix F). Repeat both ablations on BrowseComp-Plus and measure answer accuracy, proposal turns, and recovery on a held-out initial-failure subset.
