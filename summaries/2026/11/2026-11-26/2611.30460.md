# CONCOR: Benchmarking Contrastive Decoding for Visual Hallucination Mitigation Beyond Final-Output Performance

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.30460) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30460)


## One-sentence takeaway

CONCOR measures how contrastive decoding shifts the logit margin between grounded and hallucinated object candidates, revealing why stronger contrast improves some methods and worsens others in the tested setting.

## Problem and motivation

Vision-language models can name objects that are plausible in a scene but absent from the image. Contrastive decoding adjusts next-token scores to mitigate these errors, but final task scores mix that adjustment with candidate filtering, changes to the generated prefix, and shifts in Yes/No response tendencies. The authors argue that these effects make it difficult to tell whether the adjustment itself favors visually grounded objects (§§1, 4).

## Approach and contributions

- **Controlled benchmark.** CONCOR builds five-option object-recognition questions from COCO Val2014 annotations and uses co-occurrence statistics to select distractors. It compares candidates at the same first-answer position with a fixed context, using raw logits before filtering. Cyclic option permutations reduce position effects (§§5.1, 6.1, Appendix A).
- **Diagnostic metric.** The Contrastive Grounding Margin (CGM) subtracts the hallucinated candidate’s original-to-reference logit gap from the grounded candidate’s gap. A positive value means the grounded candidate gains relative advantage per unit of contrast strength. The paper also measures how often the adjustment helps, which candidates drive it, and how often predictions flip in either direction (§5.2).
- **Empirical analysis.** The authors test how filtering, prefix changes, and response tendencies affect downstream scores, then use CONCOR to compare four existing decoding methods and examine their sensitivity to contrast strength (§§4, 6).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Candidate filtering changes downstream scores | On LLaVA-1.5-7B, enabling the Adaptive Plausibility Constraint lowered AMBER CHAIR and raised POPE F1 for all four tested methods, with reference branch construction held fixed. | §4, Fig. 2, Appendix C.3 |
| Prefix wording changes later hallucinations | In 120 paired LLaVA-1.5-7B baseline continuations matched for length, switching from a neutral to a descriptive prefix raised caption-level CHAIR from 20.00% to 31.67% and mention-level CHAIR from 23.70% to 24.10%. | Fig. 3b, Appendix C.4.2 |
| Aggregate accuracy can hide a trade-off | On pooled POPE subsets with LLaVA-1.5-7B, SID reached 84.70% overall accuracy versus AvisC’s 84.49%. Relative to standard decoding, SID raised the false-positive rate from 8.11% to 8.91%, while AvisC lowered it to 7.04%. Both raised the true-positive rate. | §4, Table 1, Appendix C.3 |
| A method can fix many errors while introducing others | On single-answer CONCOR with LLaVA-1.5-7B, SID corrected 288 image-level predictions and introduced 81 errors, versus 255 corrections and 15 new errors for AvisC. Their positive-CGM rates were 74.7% and 98.3%, respectively. | §6.2, Tables 2–4 |
| Stronger contrast has mixed effects | Raising α from 1 to 3 on single-answer CONCOR with LLaVA-1.5-7B changed image-level sampling accuracy from 77.5% to 83.2% for ICD, 83.1% to 87.2% for AvisC, 81.5% to 77.2% for SID, and 69.2% to 63.3% for VCD. Standard decoding scored 71.1%. | §6.3, Table 5, Appendix C.2.3 |
| Mean-CGM rankings held across tested settings | AvisC ranked above SID in mean CGM, and both ranked above ICD and VCD across four model variants and both answer formats. With LLaVA-1.5-7B, the full ordering AvisC > SID > ICD > VCD also held across the three tested image datasets. | §6.4, Table 2, Appendix D.1, Table 10 |
| Strong controlled correction can coexist with weaker free-form results | AvisC led mean CGM in the main single-answer LLaVA-1.5-7B setting. In full-response AMBER on the same model, its lower-is-better CH score was 10.7 versus 10.6 for standard decoding and 8.0 for SID. | Table 2, Appendix C.5, Table 7 |

The fixed-context comparisons establish the direction and size of the local logit adjustment. A positive CGM can leave the grounded candidate behind if its initial deficit exceeds the adjustment (§5.2.2). The AMBER comparison shows that a strong local correction carries no guarantee of fewer hallucinations in free-form generation.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-identified.** COCO’s annotations are incomplete, so an object marked as a distractor may actually be present. Simulated annotation removal preserved the mean-CGM ranking under the tested conditions. The actual rate of mislabeled distractors remains unknown (Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference.** First-answer logits on five-option object questions have limited reach as evidence about later decoding steps, attribute or relation hallucinations, and open-ended generation. Multiple-answer accuracy also allows a nonempty subset of correct options, so it can reward predictions that omit visible objects (§6.1, Appendix A.2).
- **Analyst inference.** The contrast-strength comparison covers two values, one model variant, and one answer format. It leaves the shape of the accuracy curve and its transfer across models unresolved (§6.3, Appendix C.2.3).

## Open research questions

1. **Can an adaptive contrast strength improve accuracy without introducing more errors?** The paper tests two fixed strengths on one model and format. Compare a rule based on inference-time scores with a fixed-strength sweep on held-out images and additional models, tracking both directions of prediction flips.
2. **When does first-answer CGM predict hallucination in free-form captions?** AvisC’s results differ across CONCOR and AMBER. Measure margins at aligned object-decision steps, then compare hallucination outcomes under matched and freely generated prefixes.
3. **How much do real annotation gaps affect the comparison?** The missingness study removes existing labels synthetically. Independently verify whether sampled distractors are visible, then recompute CGM and method rankings on the verified subset.

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported benchmark design and comparisons within the tested settings. The supplied text leaves public availability of the benchmark files and implementation code unconfirmed, gives no independent audit of distractor labels, and provides no confidence intervals for the main accuracy or mean-CGM comparisons.
