# Single-Database Rewards Are Hackable: Counterfactual Execution for Trustworthy Text-to-SQL RL

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.34363) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34363)


## One-sentence takeaway

Grading SQL on hidden counterfactual databases reduced full credit for buggy queries and constructed hacks in a synthetic audit, but training with the mean counterfactual reward produced no in-domain accuracy gain and an uncertain improvement on Spider.

## Problem and motivation

Text-to-SQL reinforcement learning often rewards a query when its output matches a reference on one database. A semantically wrong query can pass if the relevant slice lacks rows that expose the error. The authors ask whether hidden database variants can make this reward more reliable and whether a more discriminating reward improves the trained policy (§1, Figure 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Counterfactual execution reward.** The agent explores the original database read-only. The grader runs the final and reference queries on that database and hidden, rule-preserving copies. The strict reward requires every execution to match. The mean reward requires a match on the original and grants credit in proportion to hidden-copy matches (§2).
- **Reward audit.** The authors use correct queries, realistic SQL mutants, and constructed hacks to measure false accepts and false rejects. They compare random copy selection, selection informed by known mutants, and a family-blind generator, and document related weaknesses in public rewards and Spider evaluation (§§2–4, Appendix D).
- **Controlled training comparison.** The main GRPO study changes only the reward across three seeds per arm. It measures how often the rewards disagree and compares performance on held-out retail templates and Spider dev (§§3–4, Table 2, Appendix D).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strict CF grading reduced false accepts | Across 1,302 synthetic audit items, strict single-DB versus strict CF with randomly selected copies paid 29.5% versus 16.8% of 1,814 mutants in full and 54.1% versus 1.9% of 2,839 constructed hacks. CF rejected 0 of 2,847 correct queries. | Table 1, §4 |
| Copy selection affected audit coverage | The family-blind generator paid 17.2% of mutants and 2.3% of hacks in full and rejected 0 of 3,017 correct queries. Selection informed by known mutants reduced mutant full credit to 1.5%, which the authors treat as an optimistic bound. | §4, Appendix A |
| Published Spider systems also showed execution disagreement | Across eight published systems, 8.1–14.9% of original-database execution passes failed a test-suite database under the study’s comparator. The authors treat these shares as upper bounds because some failures involve ties or ambiguous references. | §4, Appendix D, Table 7 |
| Mean CF training yielded no in-domain gain | On held-out retail questions, verified sample accuracy across three seeds was 8.4% for mean CF versus 11.7% for single-DB GRPO. The reported difference was −3.2 percentage points, with a question-bootstrap 95% CI of [−4.7, −1.8] and a seed-level 95% CI of [−13.7, +7.3]. The rewards differed on about 5–7% of training rollouts. | §4, Table 2, Appendix D, Table 8 |
| Spider transfer estimate was positive and uncertain | Across three seeds on Spider dev, test-suite accuracy was 29.3% for mean CF versus 27.8% for single-DB GRPO. The reported difference was +1.6 percentage points, with a question-bootstrap 95% CI of [+0.8, +2.3] and a seed-level 95% CI of [−3.2, +6.4]. The analysis was not preregistered. | §4, Table 2, Appendix D, Table 8 |

The fixed-candidate audit shows stronger discrimination on the supplied queries and copies. Passing the finite suite leaves semantic correctness unresolved. Both seed-level intervals span zero, leaving the direction of the training effects uncertain across runs. The authors identify sparse reward disagreement as a likely explanation for the limited training effect. Its causal role remains untested.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ limitations.** The study uses one synthetic schema, one 3.35B model, three main seeds per arm, and 60 GRPO steps. It covers English only and includes no human red-team evaluation. Spider may overlap with base-model pretraining. The held-out suite catches 54 of 103 mutants that pass the original database, so verified accuracy is lenient (§5).
- **Analyst inference.** The strongest audit result uses strict CF grading, while the main training comparison uses mean CF. The single-seed strict curriculum also changes the prompt set, leaving the effect of reward aggregation unresolved (§§2–4).
- **Analyst inference.** Constructed hacks establish that the reward can be fooled, while the observed policy behavior provides limited evidence about whether training would amplify such strategies. The hand audit found no hacks among 48 wrong test trajectories, and both rewards reduced cache reads in the planted-cache pilot (Appendices B and D).

## Open research questions

1. **Does more frequent reward disagreement improve policy learning?** Disagreement was rare in the main study, and the hard-prompt curriculum used one seed and a different reward form. Run a multi-seed GRPO experiment that varies prompt difficulty and reward form independently.
2. **How well do hidden copies generalize across schemas?** The family-blind generator was evaluated within the same synthetic retail schema. Audit blinded copy generation across several schemas using independently authored references and SQL mutants.
3. **Does the Spider gain replicate?** The transfer analysis was exploratory and its seed-level interval spans zero. Preregister test-suite accuracy as the primary transfer metric and repeat the reward-only comparison with more independent seeds.

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the reported audit and training comparisons. The supplied text gives the main results in prose and captions, but omits the body rows of Tables 1–4 and 6–8, including exact audit confidence intervals and system-level breakdowns. It also leaves Spider pretraining overlap and the effect of strict versus mean CF under matched training conditions unresolved.
