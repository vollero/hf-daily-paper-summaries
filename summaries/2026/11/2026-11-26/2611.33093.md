# AIChipBench: Benchmarking LLM Agents for Automated AI Accelerator Design

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.33093) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33093)


## One-sentence takeaway

In AIChipBench’s matched trials, showing coding agents the first incorrect stored-state value roughly doubled acceptance over output-only feedback, while fresh tests exposed failures in many previously accepted decoder designs (§§4.2–4.3).

## Problem and motivation

A decoder accelerator can produce the right token while corrupting a cache or recurrent state that affects later steps. That delay makes output-only feedback less useful for debugging and lets short tests miss defects. The authors identify a gap in RTL benchmarks for tool-using agents: transaction-level checks of committed state paired with a controlled comparison of what agents see during debugging (§§1, 5).

## Approach and contributions

- **State-aware correctness benchmark.** An executable integer oracle specifies exact logits and committed state at each transaction. Agents build or adapt synthesizable RTL under fixed caps. An isolated evaluator grades frozen submissions with public and held-out tests, integrity and synthesis checks, and bounded controller checks where required (§§2.1–2.3).
- **Matched debugging study and evaluator stress tests.** The authors compare output-only feedback, a state-mismatch flag, and first-mismatch localization on matched instances. They also regrade frozen submissions on fresh sequences and challenge the evaluator with known RTL defects (§§4.2–4.3).
- **Correctness-preserving optimization track.** Agents start from a correct reference and must improve throughput within a LUT budget. The authors check how improvement labels change at achieved clock rates and report FPGA board measurements for selected designs (§§2.4, 3.2, Appendix E).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Locating the first state mismatch improved acceptance and reduced cost. | Across four agents and three matched Qwen3.5 tasks, output-only, coarse-state, and full-state feedback yielded 144, 208, and 295 acceptances out of 384 attempts each. Full-state beat output-only by 25.0–46.9 percentage points in all 12 agent–task cells. Pooled agent-hours per acceptance fell from 13.4 to 6.5. Full-state also beat the coarse flag by 9.4–37.5 points, with intervals excluding zero in seven cells. | §4.2, Fig. 5a |
| Fresh tests exposed failures in accepted RTL. | Among 69 previously accepted submissions across three decoder targets, 45 failed at least one fresh sequence or condition within the original 32-step horizon. Of 85 failing sequences, 17 had only state mismatches within that horizon. All three reference designs stayed exact throughout the regrading. | §4.3, Fig. 5b–c |
| Scaffold starts had higher acceptance. | Pooled across four agents and four full-size targets, 408 of 704 scaffold attempts passed, compared with 239 of 704 interface-start attempts. | §4.1, Table 5 |
| Longer tested horizons reduced acceptance in the nano matrix. | From a common Qwen3.5-nano scaffold at the standard budget, acceptance fell from 252 to 115 of 288 attempts when the requirement grew from one to 16 steps. At 16 steps, increasing all caps to 1.5× raised acceptance to 169 of 288. Counts pool agents and added state conditions. | §4.4, Table 6 |
| Optimization frequently lost correctness, and routing changed which attempts counted as improvements. | Starting from correct RTL on four U250 targets, 571 of 1,536 attempts ended without a correct design. At the nominal 126 MHz clock, 569 attempts preserved correctness, gained at least 5% throughput, and added at most 2% LUTs versus the starting design. At achieved clock, with three completed routes required, 528 attempts met the rule. | §§2.4, 3.2, 4.5, Table 64 |
| The same two agents led across matched splits. | On the product of task-averaged correctness acceptance and target-averaged nominal PPA improvement, Fable 5.1 and Sol 5.6 scored 25.0 and 24.5, ahead of Astra 6 and Opus 5.5 at 16.8 and 16.5. Both halves preserved the top pair in all 200 splits of matched instances. | §3, Fig. 2, Appendix D.9 |

The matched feedback study supports a benefit from both exposing state divergence and locating it on the tested tasks. Regrading shows that benchmark acceptance certifies performance on the specified checks, with exactness on fresh conditions still uncertain. The leaderboard describes the evaluated model–harness configurations, and the lead within the top pair depends on the scoring rule and clock setting (Appendix D.9).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope.** Agents edit RTL within a fixed module structure, and placement and routing happen after submission. Grading extends through 32 decode steps and regrading through 256. Transfer to other stateful hardware remains untested (§6).
- **Author-reported evaluator coverage.** The complete evaluator caught 43 of 48 held-out known defects and accepted all repaired counterparts and correct controls in that study. Local checks use reduced controllers and bounded, in-order memory responses (§4.3, Appendices B.1 and B.3).
- **Analyst inference.** The horizon matrix increases test-suite size and decoding work along with the required sequence length, so the acceptance drop cannot be attributed to sequence length alone. The feedback study uses only Qwen3.5 tasks, limiting evidence about the size of the effect on other decoder families (§§4.2, 4.4).
- **Analyst inference.** Board measurements for agent designs select the largest nominal gain per agent and Qwen3.5 target, limiting how broadly the observed power agreement can be applied to optimization attempts (Appendix E.2).

## Open research questions

1. **Does first-mismatch feedback help on other stateful hardware?** The matched study covers Qwen3.5 tasks. Repeat the three feedback conditions on a non-decoder RTL workload with an executable state oracle and a common held-out grader (§4.2, §6).
2. **Which tests detect the most escaped defects for a fixed grading cost?** Longer output tests caught up to the shorter state check on the reported regrading sample, while the full evaluator still missed some known defects. Compare state checks, longer output tests, and broader protocol stimuli at equal runtime on independently seeded defects and frozen submissions (§4.3, Appendix B.3).
3. **Can agents keep more correct designs during optimization?** The PPA track records regressions but leaves the effect of incremental checking and rollback open. Compare the existing workflow with checkpointed edits and state-aware regression checks under matched budgets, then score both at achieved clock (§§3.2, 4.5).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported protocol and headline comparisons, which are described directly in the supplied text. The source was truncated, so coverage is partial. The supplied text lacks much of the intervening appendix detail, including complete per-cell results and uncertainty estimates for several diagnostic comparisons. It also gives a project repository link without establishing which oracle, evaluator, or attempt records are publicly downloadable.
