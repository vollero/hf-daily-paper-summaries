# Letting Models Manage Their Own Context: Where We Stand

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.34147) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34147)


## One-sentence takeaway

Letting agents choose when and what to compact improved task scores most for some models under tight context budgets, and reusing the unchanged suffix’s KV cache reduced prefill work in a separate serving experiment (§§4, 7).

## Problem and motivation

Agent histories grow as models call tools and accumulate outputs. When the context window fills, a harness typically summarizes older events according to a fixed rule, which can discard evidence the model still needs. The authors ask whether current models can manage that trade-off themselves, when doing so improves task performance, and how much serving work can be saved after a context edit (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Controlled benchmark comparison.** In NOOA, the harness baseline triggers summarization at 80% usage and preserves the initial instructions and 10 latest events. In the model-driven condition, the model can replace a range of events with a summary and retrieve the originals by tag. It also receives a live usage indicator, an instruction to consider compaction at 85%, and a warning near the limit. The authors compare four models on two benchmarks across budgets from 8K to 512K tokens (§§2–4, Listing 2).
- **Behavioral analysis.** The authors measure how often models compact, how much each edit shrinks the request, how often models reopen archived material, and how removing the timing instruction changes when they first compact (§§5–6).
- **Serving evaluation.** The authors integrate an SGLang suffix KV-reuse prototype with NOOA and measure prefill and task outcomes under both context-management policies. The contribution is the integration and evaluation of an existing cache-reuse approach (§§7, 9).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| GPT-6.1 Sol gained most clearly at a tight budget | At 32K on OfficeQA Pro v2, task score rose from 21% with harness-driven compaction to 56% with model-driven compaction. Score counts passes across all 90 questions, including errors, and is averaged across replicates. | p. 2, §4, Fig. 3 |
| Gains varied by model and workload | GPT and Kimi led the harness baseline at small OfficeQA Pro v2 budgets, while Opus trailed until the strategies converged. On MLE-bench Lite, differences in mean leaderboard percentile were smaller and concentrated at the tightest budgets. | §4, Figs. 3–4 |
| Kimi K3 improved score while processing fewer tokens | At 32K on OfficeQA Pro v2, model-driven versus harness-driven compaction used 36M versus 62M cumulative input tokens per run, a reported 41% reduction, and scored 43% versus 41%. The token metric includes cached inputs on every turn. | §6, Fig. 9 |
| Models differed substantially in API use | In model-driven runs pooled across budgets, the share of tasks with at least one compaction was 53% for DeepSeek, 45% for Kimi, 42% for GPT, and 4% for Opus. | §5, Fig. 5 |
| Removing the timing instruction moved compaction earlier | Median context usage at first compaction fell from about 84% to 29% for Kimi at 128K and from 83% to 40% for DeepSeek at 256K. | §6, Fig. 8 |
| Suffix reuse reduced prefill work | With Qwen3.6-27B on SGLang at 32K, suffix reuse under model-driven compaction reduced prefilled input tokens per request versus standard prefix caching by 15.1% ± 0.8 on OfficeQA Pro, 5.6% ± 1.1 on MLE-bench Lite, and 4.3% ± 0.3 on DeepSWE. The authors report no measurable impact on task accuracy across the serving study. | §7, Fig. 13 |

The benchmark results support gains for particular models and budgets under the tested NOOA setup. The policy comparison bundles edit access, usage feedback, and prompting, so it cannot isolate the contribution of each. The serving experiment measures prefilled input tokens. It leaves end-to-end latency and cost open, and the reported task-reward deltas do not establish equivalence.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ limitations.** OfficeQA Pro v2 has 90 questions and MLE-bench Lite has 22 competitions, limiting confidence in small score differences. Smaller context windows only approximate longer tasks, and dropping old events at the limit can affect scores. The four evaluated models’ training histories are unknown. The cache experiment uses one model and one engine, and suffix reuse approximates full recomputation (§8).
- **Analyst inference.** The timing ablation isolates one instruction within the model-driven condition. The supplied results do not isolate the effects of the usage indicator, pressure warning, or archive retrieval (§§3, 6). OfficeQA token comparisons exclude runs below 95% trace coverage, and Fig. 10 does not report how many runs this removed by strategy.

## Open research questions

1. **Which parts of the model-driven setup improve task scores?** The policy comparison changes several inputs together. Independently vary the warning, timing instruction, and archive access while holding the model and compaction interface fixed (§§3, 6).
2. **Do the gains carry over to genuinely longer tasks?** The paper simulates higher context pressure by shrinking the window. Hold the window fixed, vary task length, and track task scores and fallback frequency (§8).
3. **When does suffix reuse improve end-to-end serving?** The prototype measures prefill on one model and engine. Across models and edit sizes, compare prefix-only caching and suffix reuse on latency, throughput, and task reward (§§7–8).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the reported headline comparisons and experimental setup. The supplied text lacks full per-budget numerical results, replicate counts and run-level traces, the definition of Fig. 13’s error bars, and a release location for the suffix-reuse prototype. It also does not specify whether the OfficeQA Pro serving evaluation used the same v2 question set as the main comparison.
