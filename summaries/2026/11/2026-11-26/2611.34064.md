# SPARC: Geometry-Guided Attention Pruning for Long-Horizon Camera-Controlled Video Generation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.34064) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34064)


## One-sentence takeaway

SPARC uses geometric correspondences to prune a video diffusion transformer’s self-attention, improving camera adherence and scene revisitation on the reported benchmarks and reaching a 53.6× per-chunk transformer inference speedup over dense Wan2.1 when combined with four-step distillation (Tables 1 and 4).

## Problem and motivation

Long-horizon camera-controlled video generation must follow a prescribed trajectory while recovering the same scene when the camera returns to a previous viewpoint. The authors identify a shared requirement: both tasks need access to spatially relevant historical evidence. They argue that dense attention over historical frames spends computation on unrelated tokens and may interfere with retrieval, while dedicated camera or memory modules add architectural complexity (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Geometry-guided attention routing.** SPARC projects local meshes built from estimated historical depths into target views and matches latent tokens through shared mesh-face IDs. Observed target tokens attend to matched historical tokens and a local target window, with an additive logit bias favoring the matches. Unobserved tokens retain dense attention, and historical queries attend within their own frames (§§3.2–3.3).
- **Order-independent positioning for retrieved memory.** SPARC greedily selects keyframes for target-view coverage and retains the initial and latest frames. It assigns the initial frame and coverage-selected keyframes a shared temporal RoPE index while keeping chronological positions for recent history and target tokens (§3.4).
- **Efficient autoregressive rollout.** Generated frames are added to the history bank with their latents, camera parameters, and estimated depths. The authors fine-tune native self-attention without adding dedicated learnable camera or memory modules, then apply existing distribution matching distillation to train a four-step student (§§3.1, 3.5, 4.1).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Lower trajectory error | On DL3DV-Evaluation, from the same initial frame and prescribed ground-truth trajectory, SPARC achieved ATE **0.0903** versus **0.1964** for Lyra2, the strongest external baseline on ATE. The authors report a **54%** reduction. SPARC(DMD) reached **0.0760**. ATE uses ViPE-estimated trajectories after Umeyama alignment. | §4.2, Table 1, Fig. 5 |
| Better recovery at both revisits | On **50** sampled WorldScore-Static scenes with closed-loop trajectories, SPARC achieved first/second revisit SSIM of **0.5659 / 0.5479** and LPIPS of **0.1800 / 0.1992**. UCM, the strongest external baseline on those revisit metrics, scored **0.5423 / 0.5011** and **0.2095 / 0.2841**, respectively. | §4.2, Table 1, Fig. 6 |
| Visual-quality gains varied by metric | On DL3DV-Evaluation, SPARC led the table in SSIM (**0.3352**) and LPIPS (**0.5183**), while Lyra2 had lower FVD (**704.54** versus **723.62**). | §4.2, Table 1 |
| Pruning and shared positioning improved the controlled ablation | On Tanks and Temples held-out trajectories, full SPARC achieved ATE **0.018** and LPIPS **0.232** versus **0.039** and **0.274** for dense attention with correspondence bias. Across **four** history permutations, output LPIPS against the original ordering was **0.007** with shared RoPE versus **0.051** with sequential RoPE. | §4.3, Tables 2–3, Fig. 7 |
| Pruning and distillation reduced transformer latency | For a **72-frame**, **480 × 832** chunk with **9** historical latents and **99.0%** observed target tokens on one **96 GB** GPU, dense Wan2.1 took **2,436.1 s** per chunk. The pruned local-window configuration took **1,090.4 s** (**2.2×**), and the four-step student without classifier-free guidance took **45.5 s** (**53.6×**). Both pruned configurations skipped **86.7%** of query–key pairs. | §4.3, Table 4 |

The benchmarks support improved camera adherence and revisit fidelity under the tested protocols, and the controlled ablation supports a benefit from pruning beyond correspondence bias alone. The headline speedup measures transformer inference in a high-overlap setting and combines pruning, fewer sampling steps, and removal of classifier-free guidance. End-to-end rollout speed and performance on trajectories dominated by unseen regions remain unmeasured in the supplied text.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** External geometry estimation adds computational overhead (§5).
- **Analyst inference.** Depth or pose errors can alter which historical tokens are available to a query. The experiments provide no sensitivity sweep for those errors or runtime sweep across visibility levels (§§3.2–3.3, 4.3).
- **Analyst inference.** Dynamic-scene evidence is limited to qualitative foreground-motion control via segmentation and shifted correspondences after static-only training. The text reports no quantitative evaluation of dynamic revisitation (§4.3, Fig. 10).
- **Analyst inference.** Table 4 lacks a dense four-step student, so it leaves the incremental latency benefit of pruning at matched sampling and guidance settings unresolved. Table 1 reports aggregate scores without uncertainty estimates, limiting interpretation of close rankings.

## Open research questions

1. **How sensitive is retrieval to depth and pose errors?** The ablations vary attention design without varying geometry accuracy. Inject controlled depth and pose perturbations on held-out trajectories and measure ATE and revisit SSIM/LPIPS (§§3.2, 4.3).
2. **How much end-to-end speedup survives as view overlap falls?** The runtime profile uses a high-overlap chunk and reports transformer inference. Compare dense and pruned students at matched sampling and guidance settings across visibility ranges, timing geometry estimation, retrieval, generation, and memory updates (§4.3, Table 4).
3. **Does correspondence editing preserve moving objects through occlusion and revisitation?** The dynamic demonstration is qualitative. Test prescribed object and camera trajectories with occlusions, then measure object trajectory error and foreground and background revisit fidelity separately (§4.3, Fig. 10).

## Confidence and missing information
