# Lost in Translation, Not in Confidence: Benchmarking and Calibrating Multilingual Decision Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.33307) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33307)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across 25 open-weight decision models evaluated on matched multilingual items, accuracy declined faster than confidence on average, and a threshold set for 95% accuracy among accepted English answers yielded 90% in Tamil (§4.1–4.2, Table 1).

## Problem and motivation
Decision models assign probabilities to a fixed set of options. Routers and guardrails use those probabilities to decide whether to act, abstain, or escalate. The public Decision Index suite is English-only, leaving open whether confidence tracks changes in accuracy when these models switch languages. The authors identify a gap in multilingual calibration research for models whose option probabilities directly control downstream decisions (§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Parallel benchmark with translation provenance.** The suite spans 75 benchmarks, 17 subcategories, and 14 languages, with 25,626 parallel items. The authors add 10 translated benchmark extensions and one tool-selection benchmark derived from MASSIVE. A paired audit compares human and LLM translations of identical XNLI and Belebele items (§3, §4.5, Tables 7 and 12).
- **Threshold transfer and calibration audit.** The authors measure how much confidence lags accuracy, test English thresholds on matched non-English items, and evaluate language-specific threshold fitting and temperature scaling (§3, §4.1–4.2).
- **Base-model and fine-tuning analysis.** The authors decompose variation by language and base model, evaluate three untuned bases, and run 81 controlled fine-tunes varying training language, task coverage, data volume, and tuning recipe (§4.3, §5, Tables 3–5 and 11).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy declined faster than confidence. | Across 25 models and 13 non-English languages on English-matched items, top-option accuracy fell in all 325 model–language pairs. Mean accuracy fell 3.5 percentage points (95% paired bootstrap CI, −4.0 to −3.0), mean confidence fell 1.8 points, and overconfidence increased in 301 pairs. | §4.1–4.2, Tables 1 and 10 |
| English thresholds admitted more errors in other languages. | Per-model thresholds set for 95% accepted-answer accuracy on English parallel items yielded 92.6% averaged across non-English languages and 90.0% in Tamil. In a separate half-split test, held-out accepted-answer accuracy was 94.9% in English and 92.5% averaged across non-English languages. | §4.2, Tables 1 and 10 |
| Language-specific calibration improved probability estimates, with a coverage cost for re-thresholding. | Temperature scaling fitted on 500 labeled items per language lowered mean pooled held-out ECE across the 13 non-English languages from 4.10% to 2.92%, with unchanged top-option accuracy. Separately, thresholds set for 95% accepted-answer accuracy retained 73.4% of non-English answers on average and 60.0% in Tamil, compared with 85.4% average English coverage at the same target. | §4.2, Tables 10 and 13 |
| Accuracy losses clustered by language and base model. | In the nested analysis of 325 parallel-item comparisons, language accounted for 60% of accuracy-loss variance and base model, including its language interaction, accounted for 34%. All eight Qwen3.8-based models lost 13.6–17.8 points in Tamil versus English, compared with 2.9–5.7 points for the other 17. | §4.3, Figures 1c and 4, Table 15 |
| Target-language tuning narrowed a large gap, and English task transfer varied by task. | In the 24,000-item Qwen3.8 LoRA setting, replacing 0, 2,000, or 8,000 English items with Tamil items yielded Tamil accuracy deficits of 19.6, 13.9, and 11.9 points versus matched English items. In the English-only task-transfer comparison, adding English task data raised Tamil math accuracy from 55.7% to 84.3% and lowered Tamil retrieval relevance accuracy from 93.7% to 67.0%. | §5, Tables 4–5 |
| LLM translations yielded higher scores on matched items. | Averaged across all 25 models, the LLM translation scored higher than the human translation in all 23 paired XNLI and Belebele benchmark–language comparisons. The authors report that 12 paired bootstrap intervals excluded zero. | §4.5, Table 12 |

The matched-item and held-out tests establish a threshold-transfer problem for the sampled models and tasks. The untuned bases already showed a Tamil performance split, supporting the authors’ attribution to base-model differences. The design leaves the responsible pretraining factors unresolved. The paired translation audit establishes a score difference for two benchmark families and one translator. Its magnitude on native-authored evaluation sets remains unknown.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ limitations.** Each benchmark–language cell uses a single sample of at most 100 items, and parallel coverage varies by language. Aggregate translation-source comparisons confound provenance with task and language. The paired audit covers two benchmark families and one LLM translator (Limitations).
- **Authors’ limitations.** The model sample comes from one leaderboard and three base families. Some inference setups differ from reference configurations, and the untuned bases use a different harness from the published models. Most controlled settings use one seed, and the full-parameter sweep uses one epoch and a limited set of learning rates (Limitations, Table 11).
- **Analyst inference.** Translation-induced label changes could contribute to the paired accuracy differences. The LLM translations had no native-speaker review, and XNLI labels were not re-verified after translation, so the comparison leaves the mechanism unresolved (Appendix E).
- **Analyst inference.** The residual Tamil gap is specific to the tested recipes and data. Published Qwen3.5 models achieved a smaller gap than the controlled Qwen3.5 runs, and their training mixtures are undisclosed (§5, Table 3, Limitations).

## Open research questions

1. **Which properties of LLM translations make the items easier for these models?** The paired audit changes translation source and leaves label fidelity and wording effects unresolved. Compare several translators on the same items, have bilingual reviewers verify each gold label, and test whether the accuracy difference persists on the verified subset.
2. **How much coverage can calibration recover on native traffic?** The paper measures coverage on parallel benchmark items. Fit language-specific thresholds and alternative confidence scores on labeled native requests, then compare held-out precision–coverage curves across tasks and languages.
3. **Which training choices narrow the Tamil gap beyond the tested recipes?** Published Qwen3.5 models outperformed the controlled runs, and their mixtures are unknown. Hold the base, task mix, and evaluation set fixed while varying Tamil task coverage and tuning duration across multiple seeds.

## Confidence and missing information
**High** confidence in the benchmark-level summary. Material gaps include the exact training mixtures of the published models, per-item predictions and benchmark texts for independent reanalysis, and independent bilingual judgments of translation fidelity and label preservation.
