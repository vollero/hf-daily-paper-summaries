# Look Back: Reinforcement Learning with Counterfactual World Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.29259) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29259)


## One-sentence takeaway

REINFORCE Look Back uses returns from counterfactual rollouts conditioned on observed experience to reduce policy-gradient variance when shared environmental luck dominates action–noise interactions (§3.2, §D.7).

## Problem and motivation

Ordinary world models must predict both the consequences of an agent’s actions and how the environment evolves. When the latter is hard to model, rollout errors can compound, and even accurate independent rollouts can obscure action effects with unrelated randomness. The authors ask when conditioning on what actually happened can simplify transition prediction and improve credit assignment (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Counterfactual world modeling.** The authors formalize an action-conditioned model that uses a factual transition to predict an alternative outcome under the same underlying environmental randomness. They develop two routes: maximum-likelihood training on shared-noise transition pairs, and inversion of a diffusion world model trained on unpaired observations, followed by generation under an alternative action (§3.1, Fig. 2).
2. **REINFORCE Look Back (RLB).** RLB adapts REINFORCE Leave-One-Out (RLOO) by replacing independent branches with counterfactual branches. The factual rollout and each branch contribute to the update, using the other group members’ returns as a baseline (§3.2, Eqs. 13–14).
3. **Conditional guarantees.** The authors derive conditions for easier counterfactual model learning and lower gradient variance. Exact counterfactuals from any structural causal model consistent with the MDP preserve unbiasedness under the paper’s assumptions. With learned models, the bias bound depends on counterfactual branch-return error (§§3.1–3.2, §A.1).

The results support a conditional benefit in the tested settings. Shared-noise branches can reduce gradient variance, and learned branches can save real interactions during policy training. The interaction savings leave open the end-to-end cost comparison once offline data collection, model training, and inference are included. The Dino and key-to-door comparisons show that strong baselines can match RLB closely on downstream performance.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope.** Direct training requires shared-noise pairs, which may be unavailable in realistic environments. Diffusion inversion and model errors can accumulate over a rollout. The causal setup leaves persistent hidden variables that may confound actions and outcomes to future work (§3.1, §§A.2, A.9).
- **Author-stated failure regime.** Variance reduction depends on the balance between shared luck and action–noise interactions. Compatible causal models can produce different variance reductions even when exact-counterfactual RLB remains unbiased (§3.2, §§D.6–D.7).
- **Analyst inference.** The supplied unpaired-data demonstration uses one modified visual game with obstacle generation independent of the agent’s action. It gives limited evidence about diffusion inversion when action effects and environmental randomness are harder to disentangle (§5.3, §C.3).
- **Analyst inference.** In the sequential tasks, learned-model methods performed about four times as many policy updates per real-interaction budget and used offline model data. The results establish performance under that interaction accounting, while the contribution of extra updates and the end-to-end resource trade-off remain unclear (§D.5).

## Open research questions

1. **Can unpaired diffusion counterfactuals support policy learning when noise strongly interacts with actions?** Dino uses action-independent obstacle generation, while the interaction-bandit stress test uses exact counterfactuals. A next experiment could vary action–noise interaction strength in a simulator, train diffusion models on unpaired logs, and measure branch-return error and gradient bias against simulator counterfactuals (§5.3, §D.7).
2. **Which counterfactual coupling should an agent choose?** RiverSwim shows that variance reduction changes across policies and couplings. A next experiment could select among transition-equivalent couplings using held-out gradient-variance estimates, then test whether the selected coupling maintains its advantage as the policy changes (§D.6).
3. **When do interaction savings translate into lower total cost?** Dino exposes an inference overhead, and the sequential comparisons permit different update counts. A next experiment could compare time and real interactions to reach the same policy score while charging for model-data collection, offline training, and inference (§§D.4–D.5).
