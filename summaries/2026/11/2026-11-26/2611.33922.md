# iMFPO: Improving Mean Flow Policy Optimization with Drift-Aware Velocity Alignment for Embodied Control

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.33922) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33922)


## One-sentence takeaway

iMFPO uses a shared MeanFlow actor to generate locomotion actions in one network evaluation, reporting similar zero-latent returns to 64-evaluation RFPO across four simulated robots and lower GPU actor latency on Go2 (§4.2, Tables 1–2).

## Problem and motivation

Iterative flow integration adds latency to low-level robot control. The authors identify a training gap in moving to one-step generation: online reinforcement learning changes the rollout action distribution, so instantaneous-velocity supervision and the finite-interval prediction used at deployment can become inconsistent (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Shared actor with two velocity roles.** The same interval-conditioned actor estimates instantaneous velocity at equal endpoints and average velocity over the full interval for one-pass action generation. A stop-gradient Jacobian–vector product (JVP) correction connects the two roles during Improved MeanFlow regression (§§3.2–3.3, 3.5).
- **Reward updates through the velocity residual.** The proposed drift-aware surrogate compares current and behavior-policy regression errors on the same stored rollout tuples and applies an asymmetric trust-region objective. A frozen PPO teacher provides auxiliary training targets for the one-step action map. The reported use of the surrogate across robots is inconsistent between the main text and appendix (§3.4, §4.1, Appendix A.2).

The reported returns and timing support a favorable simulation return–latency trade-off against multi-step RFPO. MFPO achieved the same reported Go2 actor latency. The Go2 ablations support the value of JVP and interval regression within the tested recipe. The G1 sweep demonstrates sensitivity to latent noise for the compared policies, but the conflicting training descriptions prevent attributing that gap to the proposed surrogate. The evidence leaves statistical reliability across training runs and physical-robot success rates unestablished.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Quantitative evaluation centers on flat simulated velocity tracking, with no systematic terrain or disturbance tests. Returns come from selected checkpoints, and physical-robot results are qualitative (§5).
- **Analyst inference.** Section 4.1 says all four robots use the residual-based ratio, while Appendix A.2 describes that ratio for Go2 and diagonal Gaussian exploration with advantage-weighted iMF regression for G1, H1, and Spot. This limits reproducibility and cross-robot attribution, especially for the G1 noise sweep.
- **Analyst inference.** The Go2 ablations do not independently isolate the PPO teacher or substitute another policy surrogate while holding velocity supervision fixed. The supplied results also give no training-seed variation, leaving the reliability of small return differences unclear (§§4.2–4.3).

## Open research questions

1. **Does the residual-based surrogate improve robustness independently of the teacher and velocity regression?** The recipe conflict and bundled Go2 ablations leave this open. Compare the residual-based surrogate with a Gaussian-policy update on Go2 and G1, with teacher guidance varied independently and actor, velocity supervision, and training budget held fixed.
2. **How robust is the one-step controller to terrain and disturbances?** Flat simulation and qualitative physical trials leave this open. Compare matched policies on uneven terrain and under controlled perturbations, measuring tracking error and fall rate.
3. **How reliably does the full language-guided pipeline reach its targets?** The reported chair distance comes from scripted commands, and the NaVILA runs are qualitative. Run repeated NaVILA-driven trials across layouts and report goal success, collisions, and falls.

## Confidence and missing information

**Medium.** The actor design and headline comparisons are explicit, but the per-robot optimizer descriptions conflict. The supplied text lacks full table bodies, numeric values for the intermediate noise-sweep points, training-seed counts and uncertainty estimates, exact checkpoint-selection criteria, the timing GPU model, and quantitative physical-robot outcomes.
