# Who Makes the Decision? Auditing an Agentic LLM Pipeline for Illicit Cryptocurrency Transaction Monitoring

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.29829) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29829)


## One-sentence takeaway

In the full-context Elliptic++ audit, a deterministic wallet-score override received about 94% of interventional Shapley credit for flags, while the LLM judge contributed only false alarms and the verification gate canceled most of them (Section V-A, Table III).

## Problem and motivation

Agentic anti-money-laundering (AML) pipelines combine detectors, rules, retrieval, and LLM judgments to flag transactions and explain escalations. Auditors need to know which component caused a particular outcome. The authors identify a gap in end-to-end accuracy and explanation-quality evaluations, which can leave a rule-driven decision attributed to the LLM that wrote its justification (Section I, Figure 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Decision-source attribution protocol.** The authors combine per-case actual causation and responsibility, signed interventional Shapley credit, and mediation of upstream scores. They specify what each disabled component outputs, pair claims with matched controls, and validate the estimators against hand-derived results (Section III, Algorithm 1).
- **Structural propositions.** The paper identifies when attribution follows from the decision logic itself. For example, requiring both a judge flag and a wallet rule for escalation forces their Shapley credits to be equal (Section III, Proposition 2).
- **Empirical audit.** The authors test context truncation, score anchoring, citation applicability, and changes in routing and model backbone. They also run a narrower transfer test on synthetic bank transfers (Sections IV–V).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Wallet rule dominated full-context flag credit | On 120 Elliptic++ cases with Llama 3.1 8B at 32k context, mean flag probability was 0.587. Signed Shapley credit was +0.554 for the wallet override, +0.119 for the judge, and −0.087 for the gate. The judge’s positive credit came entirely from licit controls. | Section V-A, Table III |
| Context truncation suppressed verdicts | In the same 120-case Layer 2 rerun, the judge returned a verdict for every case at 32k and produced no verdict in 70% of cases at 4k. | Section V-A |
| Judge verdicts tracked injected scores on anonymous features | With evidence fixed and the gate and override disabled, all four backbones followed the 0.10-to-0.90 score change on every paired verdict for 24 held-out Elliptic++ transactions under the pipeline prompt. On matched typology cases with readable facts that contradicted the injected score, Llama 3.1 8B followed the wrong score in 96% of calls and Llama 3.1 70B in 16%. | Section V-B, Table IV |
| Citation matching accepted inapplicable clauses | In the score-hidden typology test, the deployed judge flagged 90% of violating cases with swapped, inapplicable clauses versus 100% with matched clauses. Every verbatim quote used to justify a flag under an inapplicable clause passed the substring check. | Section V-B, “Verified citations need not apply” |
| Large judge sensitivity yielded a small change in final flags | In the 120-case frozen-evidence mediation sweep, changing the score from 0.10 to 0.90 changed final flag probability by 0.037 for Llama (95% CI 0.013–0.063) and zero for Qwen. The absence of a direct score path to the flag follows from the routing logic. | Sections III and V-B, Proposition 3 |
| Blind-first still exposed score information | With Qwen2.5 14B on the same attribution set, blind-first routed all 8 transaction-illicit cases to a human. The remaining evidence predicted high versus low transaction-score band with out-of-fold AUC 0.80 versus 0.49 under permutation. | Section V-C, Table V |
| Out-of-sample hard cases showed weak discrimination | On detector-cleared Elliptic++ hard cases at 32k context, the pipeline flagged 12/26 illicit and 18/48 licit transactions. The positive likelihood ratio was 1.23 (95% CI 0.71–2.14), and every flag came from the override. | Section V-C |
| AMLworld transfer flags mostly came from the proxy rule | Among transfers scoring below 0.5, the pipeline flagged 7/20 hard laundering cases, 6 through the proxy override, and 1/40 licit controls through the override (Fisher p=0.001). This test measured flags only. | Section V-C |

The interventions identify which components changed outcomes under the tested routing, null policies, and frozen evidence. The out-of-sample interval includes a likelihood ratio of 1, so that test leaves meaningful discrimination unresolved. The evidence also leaves open operational performance at natural case prevalence and whether the same attribution pattern holds in other AML workflows.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** One pipeline received a full audit. The published systems received targeted checks, and AMLworld used synthetic data, a proxy override, one sample per case, and a test period that excluded the simulation tail (Sections II, IV, VI).
- **Author-stated.** Few attribution cases were transaction-illicit. Investigations were frozen after score-visible collection, and an investigator tool sometimes exposed label-derived fields. The backbone comparison changed family and size together, replies were sensitive to server process, and the authors’ typology cases lacked independent compliance-officer adjudication (Section VI).
- **Analyst inference.** Score-stratified controls and selected hard cases limit estimates of deployment review volume. The BiEGCN baseline also uses aggregate test-timestep illicit rates for recalibration on Elliptic++, while the random forest uses none, which limits a like-for-like comparison under prospective conditions (Section IV, Table II).

## Open research questions

1. **Can a judge discriminate when evidence collection is genuinely score-blind?** The blind-first test retained evidence gathered with scores visible. Recollect evidence with both investigator and retriever blinded, then compare illicit recall, false-positive rate, and human-review volume on the same held-out cases (Sections IV, V-C).
2. **How stable is override dominance across the full temporal test population?** The attribution cohort contains few transaction-illicit cases, and the out-of-sample pipeline test selects detector-cleared hard cases. Run the attribution protocol on the full temporal test set and stratify by transaction label and score band (Sections V-C, VI).
3. **Can a verifier detect whether a clause applies to the observed conduct?** The substring check accepted inapplicable clauses, and the gate lacked behavioral features. Compare it with a fact-aware verifier on matched and swapped clauses independently adjudicated by compliance officers (Sections V-A–V-B, VI).
