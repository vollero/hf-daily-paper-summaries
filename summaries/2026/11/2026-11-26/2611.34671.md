# DemoFoundry: Toward Scalable Photorealistic Robot Data with Coding Agents and Generative Rendering

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.34671) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34671)


## One-sentence takeaway

DemoFoundry uses coding agents to generate simulated manipulation trajectories and a learned renderer to turn them into realistic, action-labeled videos, raising mean real-robot success over matched raw-simulation training and approaching equal-count real-demonstration training on three tasks (Table 4).

## Problem and motivation

Robot policies need diverse demonstrations with realistic observations and synchronized actions. Teleoperation requires human effort for execution and resets, raw simulation has an appearance gap, and video-only generation can distort interactions and requires a separate way to recover robot actions. The authors identify a gap in automating both the construction of aligned rendering supervision and the programming of new interactions across scenes (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Agent-programmed demonstration generation.** Given an RGB scene reference, robot and gripper models, camera calibration, and a task instruction, a coding agent reconstructs task geometry and writes a pose-aware control program. Simulator feedback guides revisions. Successful executions across randomized layouts supply proxy video, states, and actions without target-task demonstrations (§§3.1, 3.3, Algorithm 1).
- **Paired supervision for reusable renderers.** An agent reconstructs and replays existing episodes, refining the geometry against multi-view silhouettes. The resulting 1,884 aligned real–proxy pairs adapt LTX-2.5 and MiniMax-H3 to follow proxy motion while recovering scene appearance from an RGB reference. For new layouts, an image editor produces a matching reference, and the renderer generates each view in overlapping windows (§§3.2–3.3, 4.1, Fig. 3).
- **Controlled real-robot evaluation.** The authors compare raw and rendered observations on identical action trajectories, ablate paired renderer adaptation, and test synthetic data scaling and prompt-based appearance variation (§§4.3–4.5, Table 4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Reconstruction feedback improved pair acceptance | On a fixed, source-balanced episode set, cumulative acceptance rose from 33.1% after initial replay to 39.7% after three refinement rounds. Mean mask IoU on held-out keyframes rose from 0.517 to 0.601. | Appendix A, Table S2 |
| Paired adaptation improved visual fidelity | On held-out real–proxy pairs, adapted LTX and H3 reportedly beat all listed baselines across PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, FID, and FVD. Against their respective released variants, PSNR increased by 5.10 and 7.86 dB, and FVD fell by 48.7% and 68.0%. | §4.2, Table 2 |
| Adapted H3 better preserved interaction timing | On timestamp-matched rendered and proxy videos, EventAlign@0.5 s rose from 80.95% with released H3 to 95.23% with adapted H3. | §4.2, Table 3 |
| Rendering improved real-robot transfer | Across three Marvin M6S tasks with 30 trials per task and policy, mean success for Diffusion Policy / π₀.₅ was 12.2% / 27.8% with raw simulation, 46.7% / 60.0% with DemoFoundry on the same 50 trajectories per task, and 48.9% / 62.2% with 50 real demonstrations per task. With 50 real plus 100 synthetic demonstrations per task, rendered data reached 72.2% / 82.2%, versus 47.8% / 63.3% with raw simulation. | §4.3, Table 4 |
| Paired fine-tuning improved downstream success | With the same 50 trajectories per task and matched renderer inputs and sampling, mean π₀.₅ success across the three tasks rose from 44.4% with released H3 to 60.0% with adapted H3, using 30 real-robot trials per task. | §4.4, Fig. 6 caption |
| More synthetic data improved success | With a fixed optimization budget and 30 real-robot trials per task, mean π₀.₅ success rose from 42.2% with 25 generated demonstrations per task to 85.6% with 200. At 100 it reached 77.8%, versus 62.2% with 50 real demonstrations per task. | §4.4, Fig. 7a |
| Prompted appearance variations improved robustness | For π₀.₅ on can-to-tray placement, DemoFoundry versus simulation domain randomization yielded 21/30 vs. 5/30 successes for a green bottle, 22/30 vs. 10/30 for a changed background, and 18/30 vs. 2/30 for altered lighting. Both training pipelines covered the tested variants. | §4.5, Appendix C, Table S6 |
| Generation cost was projected to fall with scale | For 100 30-second, three-view demonstrations of one task, the authors estimate $0.855 per demonstration using an already trained renderer, about $9 in one-time programming, and $0.765 in recurring rendering cost per demonstration. The cited teleoperation reference is $2.42 per demonstration. | §4.4, Eq. 5, Fig. 7b |

The matched-trajectory comparisons support a contribution from rendered observations in the tested setting, and the H3 ablation supports a further benefit from paired adaptation. The equal-count results are numerically close to real-only training, with statistical equivalence unestablished. The appearance experiment measures robustness to variants covered during training. The cost calculation estimates marginal production with an existing renderer and leaves end-to-end economics open.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Independently rendered views can disagree, and repeated autoregressive windows can accumulate drift (§3.3, §5).
- **Analyst inference.** The real-robot evidence covers three tasks on one platform, and the appearance test covers one task with training-covered variants. Transfer across other embodiments, tasks, and unseen appearance shifts remains open (§§4.3–4.5).
- **Analyst inference.** Silhouette-based screening may favor episodes that are easier to reconstruct or replay. The source-balanced refinement study accepted 39.7% of attempted episodes after three rounds, and the supplied text gives no breakdown of which interaction types were rejected (Appendix A, Table S2).

## Open research questions

1. **How quickly do cross-view inconsistencies and temporal drift accumulate?** The authors identify both problems without reporting error by rollout length. Compare independent and joint multi-view rendering, with and without memory conditioning, on the same long trajectories and measure cross-view disagreement and event timing by window (§5).
2. **How reliably can the agent set up unfamiliar robots and tasks?** The downstream evaluation uses one new embodiment, and the supplied text gives no task-programming success rate across attempted target setups. Run the same pipeline on multiple held-out platforms and tasks, logging setup failures, trajectory rejection rates, and real-robot policy success (§3.3, §4.3).
3. **Does prompt-based randomization help on unseen appearance shifts?** The reported variants were covered during training. Hold out object appearances, lighting, and combinations of the two, then compare policies trained with matched trajectory counts under prompt-based and simulation randomization (§4.5, Appendix C).

## Confidence and missing information

**Medium.** The method and real-robot counts are explicit in the supplied text. Material gaps include the full numerical contents of Tables 2 and 3, the held-out evaluation counts and split grouping, variability across policy-training seeds, target-task programming failure rates, and confirmed availability of the paired dataset, implementation, and adapted checkpoints. The renderer ablation is described in §4.4, where the prose points to Fig. 7 while the Fig. 6 caption identifies the ablation.
