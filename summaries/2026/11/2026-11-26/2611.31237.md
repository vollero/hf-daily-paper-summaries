# Let Vision-Language Models Generate Images through Their Representations

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.31237) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31237)


## One-sentence takeaway

Integral adds image generation and editing to a frozen Qwen3.5-9B while retaining 99.8% of its average understanding performance, and its shared visual tokens outperform separate FLUX.1-VAE latents on GenEval under matched pretraining settings (§§2.2, 3.2, 3.4; Appendix B.1).

## Problem and motivation

Separate generation pathways can reduce interference with a vision-language model’s (VLM’s) understanding capabilities, but they often generate in a different visual representation space. The authors ask whether generation can use the pretrained VLM’s own visual representations while preserving both semantic understanding and the fine detail needed to reconstruct images. Final-layer features can lose that detail, and training the encoder to recover it can degrade semantic quality (§§1, 2.1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Abstraction Fusion (AF).** Attention pools features across the frozen vision encoder’s hierarchy into spatial tokens, which pass through a variational bottleneck. A semantic projector maps the tokens into the VLM’s visual input space, and a pixel decoder reconstructs images from the same tokens (§2.2, Fig. 4).
- **Block-wise Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT).** A flow-matching generator reuses attention keys and values from the frozen VLM at selected block boundaries. This reduces the number of generation blocks relative to coupling a generator to every VLM layer (§2.3, Fig. 5).
- **Representation analysis.** The authors use layer-similarity measurements to motivate the hypothesis that reconstruction training can interfere with semantic abstraction. They then compare native AF tokens with separate VAE latents and tokens from an external semantic encoder under matched generation training (§§2.1, 3.4, Figs. 3 and 6).

The matched comparisons support a generation benefit from VLM-native tokens beyond what the reported reconstruction PSNR alone explains. The semantic-supervision ablation reinforces this interpretation: removing semantic alignment improves reconstruction PSNR but worsens generation on all three reported benchmarks (Appendix C.1, Fig. 10). The benchmark results establish performance in the tested settings. The specific knowledge transferred from the frozen VLM, and how much the tokenizer advantage persists through full training, remain open.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated qualification.** The authors frame the propagation bottleneck as a hypothesis. Greater similarity between early and deep layers under joint training is consistent with it, but the measurement does not isolate the cause of semantic degradation (§2.1, Fig. 3).
- **Analyst inference.** The strongest tokenizer comparison uses short text-to-image pretraining runs, and the end-to-end Integral results use Qwen3.5 backbones. Appendix B.2 extends the tokenizer evaluation to SigLIP2, with zero-shot classification as its semantic measure. Generalization to other full VLM architectures remains uncertain (§§3.4–3.5, Appendix B.2).
- **Analyst inference.** Some Table 3 comparisons use rewritten prompts or explicit reasoning. Editing scores depend on model judges, and KRIS-Bench baseline scores come from its leaderboard. Rankings should be interpreted under those protocols (§§3.1–3.3, Tables 3–6).
- **Analyst inference.** The efficiency comparison is specific to the reported generation settings. AF also increases latency by 2.1% and peak GPU memory by 7.3% in the understanding test with one 800×800 image and 100 generated text tokens (Appendix C.3, Table 12).

## Open research questions

1. **Does the native-token advantage persist through full training?** The matched tokenizer comparison ends after initial pretraining (§3.4). Train AF, SigLIP2-AF, and FLUX.1-VAE variants through the same interleaved pretraining and fine-tuning schedule, then compare generation and editing.
2. **Does the gain transfer to other VLM families?** The full Integral experiments use Qwen3.5, while the SigLIP2 extension tests tokenizer reconstruction and classification (Appendix B.2). Repeat the native-versus-external tokenizer comparison on another frozen VLM family with matched generator capacity.
3. **Does direct latent reuse reduce drift across editing turns?** The authors describe reusing generated tokens as context, but the supplied text gives no quantitative multi-turn comparison (§2.2). Run identical edit sequences with direct latent reuse and with pixel decoding followed by re-encoding, then measure instruction adherence and preservation of untouched content across turns.
