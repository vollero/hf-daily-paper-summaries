# RoboEvo^2: Recursive Robot Self-Improvement via Harness-Policy Co-Evolution

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.34675) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34675)


## One-sentence takeaway

RoboEvo^2 uses verified repairs of robot policy failures to improve both the agent harness and the learned controller, raising standalone task success from 11.0% to 84.1% on 800 unseen LIBERO-PRO evaluation episodes after eight rounds without additional human demonstrations (§4.1, Fig. 3).

## Problem and motivation

Learned robot policies execute efficiently but can struggle when tasks or scenes shift, and adapting them often requires new human demonstrations. Coding agents can discover and repair solutions through robot-control APIs, but repeated deliberation adds latency and API expense. The authors identify a gap in approaches that improve either the harness or the policy in isolation. As the policy learns, both the failures worth investigating and the motor skills available for repair change (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Failure-guided repair across three routes.** A coding agent searches in parallel using executable skills alone, skills guided by an existing demonstration, or skills composed with calls to the current policy. Among verified successes under matched conditions, it selects the trajectory with the lowest normalized motion jerk (§3.2, Fig. 2).
- **Counterfactual Preference Distillation (CPD).** The policy learns from verified successes through supervised training and from matched failure–correction pairs through CPD. CPD favors the successful continuation relative to a frozen reference policy, with penalties that discourage worsening predictions on successful actions or excessively suppressing failed alternatives (§3.3).
- **Reciprocal updates across rounds.** Verified repairs update the harness’s executable skills, routing rules, and memory. The updated policy supplies motor primitives for the next search and exposes new failures. Candidate updates are accepted based on success under matched development conditions (§§3.1–3.3).

The held-out evaluations support adaptation to new seeds within the tested benchmarks, and the controlled LIBERO-PRO ablations support a contribution from joint evolution and CPD in that setting. The API figures measure coding-agent expenditure per round. End-to-end compute and latency savings remain unquantified in the supplied text. Table 2 retains the published baselines’ original protocols, limiting direct rankings. The small physical evaluation leaves the reliability of the standalone advantage over teleoperation uncertain.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** The fixed atomic skills and perception tools omit capabilities such as force-controlled contact and in-hand regrasping. Training-data selection relies on a task-level success check. The authors suggest using a vision-language model for this check at the cost of additional calls (App. H).
- **Analyst inference.** Physical data collection still depends on an operator approximately restoring the robot and scene after failures. The text gives neither the tolerance for accepting these resets as matched nor the fraction that yielded eligible CPD pairs, so the sensitivity of physical CPD to reset error is unclear (§§3.3, 4.4).
- **Analyst inference.** Transfer evidence covers reuse after adapting control interfaces on the same simulated tasks and layouts, plus four tasks on one physical platform. It leaves transfer under simultaneous changes in embodiment and task open (§§4.3–4.4).

## Open research questions

1. **How sensitive is CPD to imperfect physical resets?** The G1-D experiment uses approximate restoration and gives no reset-error analysis. A follow-up could vary measured pose offsets and compare supervised learning with CPD at each offset (§§3.3, 4.4).
2. **What does selecting the lowest-jerk repair contribute to policy learning?** The paper defines the criterion, but the supplied results give no matched comparison against other selection rules. A follow-up could choose the lowest-jerk, first verified, or randomly selected verified repair from the same candidate pool and compare smoothness and held-out success (§3.2, App. C.2).
3. **Can a visual verifier supply reliable training labels at acceptable cost?** The authors propose it as a substitute for the task-level check, but provide no evaluation of that substitution. A follow-up could compare the two verifiers on the same rollouts and measure label errors, API cost, and downstream policy success (App. H).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the method summary and the reported headline results. The retrieved text contains many table captions without their rows. Material gaps include full per-round and per-task results, complete training configurations and execution budgets, numerical agent-backbone and EWC ablation results, physical CPD pair counts and matching criteria, and end-to-end compute and latency measurements. The text announces model and code release, but current availability cannot be verified from the supplied material.
