# Simulator-as-Proxy World Model for Robot Manipulation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.32786) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32786)


## One-sentence takeaway

SimPWM pairs generated manipulation video with explicit simulator state, and frozen-object tests show that MiniMax-H3 responds more strongly to simulated object motion than LTX-2.3 even though both closely follow the rendered arm.

## Problem and motivation

Action-conditioned video world models can produce plausible footage while drifting from commanded arm motion or depicting incorrect grasps. Simulators expose the consequences of actions, but small scene reconstruction errors can change whether a grasp succeeds. The authors identify a gap in evaluating proxy-conditioned video models: when the arm and object usually move together, agreement on recorded trajectories cannot tell whether the generator follows the simulated object or infers its motion from the arm (Sec. 1, Fig. 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Explicit simulator state alongside generated video.** SimPWM takes a calibrated first observation and joint and gripper commands. A rule-based simulator poses the robot mesh and updates object point sets through grasp, rigid carry, release, and gravity. A reference-conditioned video model uses the rendered proxy, first RGB frame, and task text to generate video. The pipeline also returns the simulated state (Secs. 3–3.3, Figs. 4–5).
- **Action-conditioned scene reconstruction.** The method lifts class-agnostic object segments into 3D and uses the commanded gripper closure to adjust an object’s depth while preserving its first-frame image footprint (Sec. 3.1).
- **Tests that isolate object conditioning.** The authors compare full and robot-only proxies, replay actions with the gripper held open, and freeze object points while replaying arm motion. A robot-only control estimates how much of the frozen-object response comes from the accompanying change in finger closure (Secs. 4.4–4.5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| The rendered arm accounts for most of the robot tracking gain. | On 293 DROID episodes from 121 held-out setups, fine-tuned SimPWM variants achieved robot IoU of 0.754–0.777 versus 0.690 for the best end-to-end baseline, scored at 320×192 and 5 Hz. In the separate lower-resolution H3 ablation, robot IoU was 0.341 without a proxy, 0.766 with the robot alone, and 0.767 with the RGB scene proxy. | Secs. 4.1–4.4, Tables 2 and 4 |
| Simulated objects reduce missed lifts, with mixed gains in object identity. | Across 133 DROID episodes with a real lift, the RGB proxy reduced misses from 54.1% to 37.6% versus robot-only, a 16.5-point reduction (95% CI [8.3, 25.6]). Correct-object lifts rose 7.5 points (95% CI [−0.8, 15.8]) and wrong-object lifts rose 9.0 points (95% CI [3.0, 15.8]). | Sec. 4.4, Table 4, Appendix C.2 |
| Frozen object state affects the two backbones differently. | On 96 DROID targets labeled rigid, freezing objects lowered lift rates from 42% to 10% for H3-C and from 57% to 49% for LTX-2.3-C. Across all 133 perturbation episodes, the H3 RGB ablation’s lift-rate drop exceeded the robot-only control’s by 18.8 points (95% CI [10, 28]). | Sec. 4.5, Table 5 |
| Calibration and closure-based depth refinement affect proxy accuracy. | On the DROID simulator ablation, using logged calibration reduced proxy robot IoU from 0.787 to 0.505 and grasp agreement from 69.5% to 55.3% on decidable episodes. Removing depth refinement reduced object IoU from 0.287 to 0.185. | Sec. 4.3, Table 3, Appendix A |
| Cross-robot results primarily demonstrate trajectory tracking. | On the 1,000-episode WorldArena challenge set, the retrained bimanual variant ranked third of 19 on trajectory accuracy, with weaker image quality. On 100 GR1 test windows, the robot-only variant achieved robot IoU of 0.861 versus DreamDojo’s 0.815, with worse LPIPS and FVD. | Appendix E, Tables E1–E2 |

The experiments show that a robot render can anchor arm motion and that simulated object motion adds a measurable lift signal for H3. The correct-object confidence interval includes zero, and the simulator can itself select the wrong object. The frozen-object test therefore measures whether the video responds to the proxy. Real-world correctness under perturbed actions and usefulness for closed-loop control remain untested.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported scope.** The simulator models visible objects and coarse pick-and-place dynamics. It carries cloth and rope rigidly and omits pushing, pouring, folding, and articulation. A slightly misplaced closing point can select a neighbor, and evaluation uses replayed actions (Sec. 5, Appendix D).
- **Author-reported evaluation caveats.** DROID calibration uses some frames from the evaluation window and excludes poorly calibrated episodes before sampling. An outside-window refit reduced generated robot IoU modestly. Ctrl-World’s training data overlap the test set, grasp and lift labels depend on automated extraction and judging, and WorldArena comparisons use unequal training data. GR1 uses a robot-only proxy on windows from sessions seen during training (Appendices A, C.4, and E).
- **Analyst inference.** The paper’s pretraining explanation remains a hypothesis because backbone and fine-tuning setup differ. The comparison of H3 trained on the consistent subset with H3 trained on all episodes also changes simulator version. Keyword-based rigidity labels include some bags in the rigid group, limiting material-specific interpretation (Table 5, Appendices A and C.4).

## Open research questions

1. **Can training make object following reliable across backbones?** Consistent training clips leave arm and object motion coupled. Train each backbone with matched budgets on paired examples that decouple those signals, then test held-out frozen-object and wrong-object interventions.
2. **Would deformable simulation improve object following and visual accuracy?** Rigid carry leaves substantial residual lift errors for deformable targets. Compare rigid and deformable proxies with the same generator on held-out cloth and rope tasks, scoring both lift decisions and tracked shape.
3. **Can the explicit state predict outcomes under changed actions?** The interventions test video agreement with simulated state, while the physical robot data come from recorded actions. Execute matched action variants on held-out robot setups and compare measured outcomes with both simulator state and generated video.

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported DROID comparisons and the direction of the frozen-object effect. The supplied text lacks matched experiments that isolate pretraining from backbone and fine-tuning differences, physical ground truth for the perturbed actions, and closed-loop results. It lists code, but the availability of trained SimPWM checkpoints and evaluation annotations is unspecified.
