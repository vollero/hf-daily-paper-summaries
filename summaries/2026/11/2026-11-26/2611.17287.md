# Negative-Only Policy Optimization for One-Sided Verifiable Rewards

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.17287) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17287)


## One-sentence takeaway

NOPO suppresses responses rejected by partial verifiers and weights those updates by the share of non-falsified alternatives, achieving the highest average oracle-scored pass@16 on both tested Qwen3 backbones (Section 4, Table 2).

## Problem and motivation

Complete correctness checks can be costly or unavailable during training, while public tests, format constraints, and reasoning audits can still expose specific failures. The authors call this asymmetry *one-sided verifiability* (OSV). Standard reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards can directly reinforce incorrect answers that pass partial checks, while verifier-free methods leave reliable rejection evidence unused (Sections 1 and 3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Formalization.** OSV distinguishes the precision of verifier rejections, the fraction of accepted responses that are incorrect, and the fraction of actual errors caught. The authors measure these quantities across three verifier types using ground truth withheld from training (Section 3, Table 1).
- **Optimization.** NOPO adapts existing Negative Preference Optimization (NPO) to on-policy rollouts. Its reference-relative penalty attenuates as a rejected response becomes less likely. The new group weight scales that penalty by the fraction of non-falsified responses raised to an exponent. The default is linear, all-rejected groups produce no update, and accepted responses contribute only to the group weight (Section 4, Algorithm 1).
- **Empirical validation.** The paper compares oracle-scored performance, inference-time scaling, and cross-benchmark transfer, and tests the contribution of the group weight against ungated NPO (Section 5, Appendices D–H).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Rejections were much cleaner than acceptances | Before training, across both backbones, rejection precision was 100% for public tests and constraint checks and 95.8–97.4% for the math self-verifier. Across all settings, 19.7–77.5% of accepted responses were incorrect. | Section 3.2, Table 1 |
| NOPO led the main comparison | Across five benchmarks, mean oracle pass@16 was 52.86% vs. 50.69% for runner-up w-Reinforce on Qwen3-4B, and 52.13% vs. 48.84% on Qwen3-8B. NOPO led in 9 of 10 benchmark–backbone settings. The exception was 8B OlympiadBench, where GRPO scored 76.97% vs. NOPO’s 76.52%. | Table 2, Appendix C.1 |
| Coding gains persisted at a large sampling budget | On Qwen3-4B at 512 samples per problem, oracle pass@512 for NOPO, w-Reinforce, and Base was 66.95%, 63.60%, and 51.05% on CodeContests and 50.86%, 48.57%, and 42.29% on LiveCodeBench. Under that budget, NOPO alone solved 40 and 16 problems, respectively, while Base alone solved 2 and 1. | Tables 3 and 8 |
| Gains transferred to unseen code benchmarks | On Qwen3-4B, mean unseen-target oracle pass@16 was 29.49% for NOPO vs. 27.68% for w-Reinforce after CodeContests training, and 31.93% vs. 30.86% after LiveCodeBench training. NOPO led on every target in both comparisons. | Table 4 |
| The group weight improved on ungated NPO | Under the Qwen3-8B main protocol, NOPO improved oracle pass@16 over NPO by 1.19–4.37 percentage points across the five benchmarks. The linear weight also led the exponent variants tested on Qwen3-4B trained on TACO, both in-domain and on mean unseen-target pass@16. | Tables 6 and 10, Appendix H |

The main results establish oracle-scored gains on prompts reused from training, where the methods had access only to partial verifier rewards. The transfer experiments separately support generalization to unseen code benchmarks. The authors interpret the fixed-budget solve overlap as an expanded capability frontier. It establishes a larger observed solved set at that budget, while leaving open whether Base could solve those problems with additional draws.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors.** Negative feedback identifies failures but leaves the destination of redistributed probability mass unspecified. The authors also report that prolonged negative-dominated training can cause entropy divergence and select the latest checkpoint saved before observed divergence (Section 7, Appendix C.2).
- **Analyst inference.** The main comparison reuses the training prompts, transfer and large-budget scaling cover only coding on Qwen3-4B, and both backbones come from the same model family in non-thinking mode. Evidence for held-out logic or math and other model families remains unavailable (Tables 2–4, Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference.** Each domain uses a different verifier type, so the association between higher rejection recall and larger gains over GRPO leaves verifier recall confounded with task and domain. The exponent sweep is limited to Qwen3-4B trained on TACO (Tables 6 and 9).

## Open research questions

1. **How much rejection recall does NOPO need?** The math verifier catches relatively few errors, but the cross-domain comparison cannot isolate recall. On one code benchmark, randomly mask known rejections at different rates while holding the prompts and sampling budget fixed, then compare NOPO and GRPO on oracle pass@k (Table 9).
2. **Does the support weight generalize to held-out math and logic prompts?** Transfer and inference-time scaling are tested on code. Train on disjoint prompt splits for math and logic, then compare NOPO, NPO, and GRPO across inference budgets on held-out prompts (Tables 3–4 and 10).
3. **Can selective positive feedback improve learning on groups with little negative evidence?** The authors suggest that accepted responses may carry additional information, but test no such extension. Compare NOPO with a variant that adds an uncertainty-filtered positive term and measure both oracle pass@k and the fraction of accepted responses that are incorrect (Section 7, Appendix E).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the objective and reported benchmark comparisons. The supplied text does not report repeated-seed variability or confidence intervals, an operational threshold for entropy divergence, or a release of training code or checkpoints.
