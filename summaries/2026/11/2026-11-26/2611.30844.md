# DyLoMo-DiT: Dynamic Looped Mixture-of-Experts Diffusion Transformers

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.30844) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30844)


## One-sentence takeaway

DyLoMo-DiT combines weight-shared middle layers, sparse experts, and extra passes at low noise to lower ImageNet-256 FID against DiT and DiT-MoE at matched total parameter and average forward-compute budgets (Tables 2–4).

## Problem and motivation

Scaling a dense diffusion Transformer couples capacity and depth to parameter storage and computation. Sparse-expert DiTs increase capacity with limited additional per-token computation, but still store many parameters and use the same depth at every noise level. The authors identify a gap in how diffusion Transformers allocate these resources jointly, especially when extra computation may be more valuable at some denoising stages than others (Secs. 1–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Weight-shared depth.** Repeat a consecutive middle segment, sharing attention, experts, routers, and conditioning weights across passes. Scale each residual update by the inverse pass count and recompute expert routing on each visit (Sec. 3.3).
- **Noise-dependent pass allocation.** The main ImageNet schedule uses three passes below timestep 500 and one at or above it, averaging two passes under uniform sampling. Training with mixed pass counts also allows inference-time schedule selection, with a measured FID penalty under a two-pass comparison (Secs. 3.4, 5.6).
- **Joint budget allocation.** Configure width and layer visits for forward compute, then use sparse expert capacity to fill the storage budget. Each routing head selects top-k experts per token, alongside an always-on shared expert. The claimed novelty is the joint design for diffusion Transformers, building on prior recurrence and routing methods (Secs. 2, 3.1–3.5, Fig. 3).

The matched ImageNet comparisons support a lower FID for the joint architecture under the tested protocols. The schedule controls support allocating more depth to low-noise steps, while the incremental architectural comparisons often change layer and expert layouts together. The text-to-image evidence establishes benchmark scores for one trained checkpoint under the authors’ pipeline. It leaves architecture-level attribution and end-to-end serving efficiency unresolved.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported scope.** The DiT-MoE reruns use a common training recipe that differs from the original paper’s. The main text-to-image benchmark uses one trained model, and the authors use checkpoint fluctuations to gauge GenEval variability. The separate scaling study ablates multi-stream residuals on validation loss, while the benchmark comparison lacks matched loop and routing ablations (App. A, E, J).
- **Author-reported constraints.** Changing inference pass counts substantially degraded fixed-schedule checkpoints in the tested cases. Mixed-pass training enabled schedule changes at a quality cost in the two-pass comparison. The optional multi-stream residual also caused instability with adaLN under the tested ImageNet recipe (Apps. G, I).
- **Analyst inference.** Many ImageNet ablations and the longer training continuations use single runs, limiting precision about effect sizes. ImageNet recall was slightly lower for DyLoMo-DiT in all six main matched comparisons, so the FID gains do not imply higher recall. The video transfer test uses in-house validation loss and a small sample gallery, which leaves comparative video quality unresolved (Tables 2–3, App. B–C, Table H4).
- **Analyst inference.** Active parameters and per-forward GMAC leave peak memory and latency unresolved. The text-to-image pipelines also use different numbers of sampling steps and different guidance settings (Table 5, Apps. A–B).

## Open research questions

1. **How much does noise-dependent depth help at text-to-image scale?** The benchmarked checkpoint uses two passes throughout. Train matched single-pass, uniform-loop, and noise-allocated variants on the same data, then compare GenEval, DPG-Bench, and COCO FID across seeds (Apps. A, E).
2. **Does the sharp routing switch improve generation?** The timestep-swap test identifies what drives routing in the probed layers, while its quality benefit remains unmeasured. Train matched models with and without a penalty on abrupt routing changes and compare FID and per-timestep denoising error (App. F).
3. **Do the arithmetic savings improve serving cost at comparable quality?** The text-to-image comparison measures denoiser GMAC per forward pass, with model-specific sampling pipelines. Benchmark end-to-end latency, throughput, and peak memory on identical hardware across comparable quality points (Table 5, App. A).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported ImageNet comparisons within their stated protocols. Confidence is lower for the size of single-seed effects and for generalization to text-to-image and video. The supplied text does not provide the full text-to-image training corpus and stage recipe, independent training replicates for its benchmarked checkpoint, the composition of the in-house video datasets, or measured end-to-end latency, throughput, and peak memory. The paper lists code and weights locations, but their contents and availability are unverified.
