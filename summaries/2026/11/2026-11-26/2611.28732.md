# Entangle: Scaling Human-Video In-Context World-Action Models across Unseen Tasks and Contexts

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.28732) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28732)


## One-sentence takeaway
Entangle learns to follow a single human demonstration video by training on temporally aligned human–robot pairs and contrasting different executions of the same task, scoring 83.5/100 across six unseen Astribot S1 tasks with fixed weights at deployment (§§3–5.3).

## Problem and motivation
A human video can specify operation order, motion paths, and contacts that a short language instruction may leave implicit. The authors identify two obstacles to learning from video. Task-level human–robot pairs can disagree on motion and timing, so a clip may provide little guidance for a particular robot action. Even with aligned pairs, standard action losses can reward a policy for inferring the task from the initial scene and executing a familiar variant (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Aligned training data.** Ent-Dataset combines synchronized human reenactments with synthetic human clips generated from full robot recordings and screened for visual quality and interaction timing. Each accepted clip retains the original robot action supervision (§3, Fig. 2).
- **Training for procedure following.** A MotuBrain-initialized world-action model jointly predicts future robot frames and executable actions. It places human and robot video tokens sequentially in time, trains on synchronized windows of different lengths, and uses intra-task InfoNCE to favor the demonstrated trajectory over other executions of the same task (§4, Fig. 3).
- **Adaptive execution and evaluation.** On the physical robot, an attention-based heuristic shortens the executed action prefix before replanning when reference attention becomes weak and diffuse. The authors report a data-scaling study and show qualitative transfer across changed layouts and tools (§§4.3, 5.5–5.6, 12.3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Higher RoboTwin success than Zero-WAM | **69.52% vs. 46.95%** mean binary success, a **22.57 percentage-point** gain across seven tasks held out from simulator fine-tuning. Three seeds, 100 expert-feasible scenes per task per seed, one fixed generated reference per task. Zero-WAM’s result comes from its paper. | §5.2, Table 1, §§11.1, 12.2 |
| Real-robot component controls favor the full system | **83.5/100** mean across six unseen Astribot S1 tasks, with 10 trials per task and configuration. Mean scores were **41.3** with same-task mismatched training references, **64.7** without InfoNCE, **62.7** with fixed-length training, and **61.3** with a fixed full execution horizon. The rubric scores goal completion and procedural constraints. | §§5.1, 5.3, Table 2, §§8.3, 9 |
| More aligned training data improves real-robot scores | The mean rose monotonically from **40.8/100 at 10%** to **83.5/100 at 100%** across the tested positive fractions on the same six-task suite, with 10 trials per task. All nonzero fractions used 4,000 continued-training steps. The original backbone scored **0/100** without continued training. | §5.5, Fig. 5, §9 |

| Demonstrated procedures can transfer across scenes | Qualitative rollouts show adaptation to reoriented pens, a different mouse, a stand substituted for a frame, and a banana used as a sweeping tool. | §5.6, Fig. 6 |

The component controls and route-sensitive tasks support the authors’ claim that the model uses the demonstrated procedure. The reported real-robot score measures partial progress and procedural fidelity, while RoboTwin reports binary success after multi-task simulator adaptation. The cross-scene examples show that transfer is possible in the depicted cases. Its reliability across scenes, tools, and demonstrations remains unknown.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors.** Physical deployment covers one robot embodiment, and the paired-data pipeline depends on generated-video fidelity. Cross-scene transfer and generated-context deployment have qualitative evidence. The authors identify longer-task failure recovery as future work (§6).
- **Analyst inference.** The scaling study varies the amount of the mixed real/synthetic corpus. It leaves the relative value of synthetic pairs and human reenactments unresolved (§§3.1, 5.5, 9).
- **Analyst inference.** The Adaptive Horizon control compares against executing the entire chunk, so its gain could reflect more frequent observation and replanning as well as attention-based timing (§12.3).
- **Analyst inference.** RoboTwin’s expert-feasible scenes and fixed reference clip per task constrain the result to that protocol. The RoboICL comparison also uses a smaller scene sample and first-attempt scoring (§§11.1, 12.2).

## Open research questions

1. **How reliable is Context-OOD transfer?** The paper shows selected rollouts (§5.6). A next experiment could vary layout, viewpoint, and tool substitution systematically across held-out tasks and score both completion and procedure fidelity.
2. **When can synthetic pairs substitute for reenactments?** The scaling study uses a mixed corpus (§5.5). A next experiment could match task coverage, pair count, and training steps across real-only, synthetic-only, and mixed conditions.
3. **Does attention select better replanning points?** The published control uses the full execution chunk (§12.3). A next experiment could compare Adaptive Horizon with a fixed shorter horizon tuned to match its average replanning frequency.

## Confidence and missing information
**High** for the method and headline results explicitly reported in the supplied text. Material gaps include the full cells and seed-level variability from Tables 1, 2, and 5, trial-level real-robot scores, quantitative Context-OOD and generated-context results, and confirmation that code, weights, or Ent-Dataset are publicly available.
