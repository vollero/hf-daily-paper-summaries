# ROAM-Bench: Evaluating Embodied Active Spatial Intelligence through Evidence Verification

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-26.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.33143) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33143)


## One-sentence takeaway

ROAM-Bench scores final outcomes separately from required exploration and interaction, revealing that all seven evaluated vision-language models sometimes succeed without completing the required checks and sometimes err after stronger corroboration passes (Table 2).

## Problem and motivation

An embodied agent can report the correct object count while missing an instance or skipping a required room, and it can gather the required observations yet still reach the wrong conclusion. The authors argue that existing benchmarks do not consistently distinguish these failures across a full episode, making it difficult to tell whether an agent needs better exploration, spatial judgment, or object handling (§§1–2, Fig. 1, Table S5).

## Approach and contributions

- **Benchmark.** ROAM-Bench contains 250 manually validated tasks spanning 11 task types and 27 furnished indoor simulations. The authors check feasibility with simulator-informed trajectories and manually verify instructions, goals, and evidence requirements (§§3.1–3.3, Tables S1–S2).
- **Interactive platform and scoring.** Agents choose predefined navigation and manipulation skills under motion, collision, and reach constraints. The evaluator uses simulator state and episode records to score outcome correctness (A), task-required evidence (N), and stronger corroboration (S). Strict success (T) requires A and N. S requires N plus task-specific checks such as depth-based geometry or post-release object support (§3.4, Fig. 3, Appendix A).
- **Diagnostic evaluation.** The authors compare seven VLMs with a stratified human sample, audit the evidence checks, and use oracle inputs and relocation milestones to investigate failures (§§4–5, Appendices E–I).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| GPT-6-astra led the models on strict success | On 250 tasks per VLM, GPT-6-astra achieved 69.2% T and 73.2% A. The other six models achieved 10.4–46.8% T. Humans achieved 84.0% T on a separate stratified sample of 100 tasks, with one person per task. | §4.2, Table 2 |
| Correct outcomes can coexist with incomplete execution | Across 1,750 VLM episodes, 206 passed A and failed N. Of these, 106 missed required path-integration landmarks. Among the remaining 100 cases, 85 missed required object observations. The human sample had zero such gaps. | Table 2, §5.4, Table S9 |
| Errors persist after stronger checks pass | Among each VLM’s own S-passing episodes, outcome error rates ranged from 15.9% (27/170, GPT-6-astra) to 72.9% (43/59, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash). The human rate was 4.1% (3/73) on the stratified sample. | Table 2 |
| Correct pickup was the largest relocation bottleneck | Across 210 relocation episodes, 94 reached correct pickup, 56 subsequently placed the same object at the destination, and 40 passed the destination-support milestone. Of 116 episodes without a correct pickup, 102 included an uncorrected wrong-object pickup. | Fig. S2, Appendix E.2 |
| Oracle views and bundled sensors improved final answers | Across 139 questions and seven models, original accuracy was 45.2% (440/973), compared with 49.4% (481/973) for oracle retrieval, 58.4% (568/973) for target-view supplementation, 64.9% (631/973) for oracle trajectory RGB, and 77.6% (755/973) for the same oracle RGB with depth, pose, and calibration. Each intervention elicited a single answer without further actions. | §5.3, Table 3, Appendices H–I |
| Human audit broadly agreed with automatic checks | In a 200-episode audit with one blinded annotator, agreement was 97.4% for N across 196 ratable episodes and 93.3% for S across 194. | Appendix F.2, Tables S20–S21 |

The evidence shows that outcome accuracy and completion of prescribed exploration capture different aspects of performance in this simulator. S measures whether the recorded episode meets corroboration rules. It cannot establish which observations the model used or whether those observations were sufficient for its reasoning. The oracle gains apply to final answers on the diagnostic subset. Their effect on active exploration and strict task success remains unknown.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-described scope.** Path-integration direction can be inferred from the start and final poses, while landmark checks measure completion of the prescribed exploration. These gaps therefore cannot by themselves show that missing landmarks impaired direction reasoning (§3.4.3, Appendix A).
- **Author-described diagnostic constraints.** The oracle comparisons use a subset with recoverable inputs and vary view counts and coverage by construction. Oracle trajectory RGB also replaces the exploration context. The matched-RGB sensor comparison isolates the bundled addition of depth, pose, and calibration, leaving their individual effects unresolved (§5.3, Appendices H–I).
- **Analyst inference.** The simulator and fixed high-level skills limit what the scores establish about physical robots and low-level control. Human–model differences are descriptive because the quoted aggregates use different task sets, and one annotator per audit episode leaves inter-rater reliability for the N/S audit unmeasured (§4.1, Appendices F.2 and J).
- **Analyst inference.** Numerical scores depend on the chosen tolerances. Tighter tolerances preserve the overall model ordering reported by the authors, while some pairwise orderings reverse within individual numerical task types (Appendix E.3, Tables S14–S18).

## Open research questions

1. **Can agents seek missing evidence on their own?** Oracle supplementation supplies views after exploration ends. Compare the same agent with and without explicit room-coverage and instance tracking under identical action budgets, measuring N, T, and total actions (§5.3, Appendix H).
2. **Which sensor inputs improve spatial judgment?** The matched-RGB experiment adds depth, pose, and calibration together. Hold RGB views and order fixed, vary sensor components in interpretable combinations, and report results by task type (Appendix I).
3. **Does verifying target identity before pickup improve relocation?** The episode analysis identifies uncorrected wrong-object pickup as a common early failure. Compare the same agent with and without a pre-pick identity check and a post-pick confirmation step, measuring correct pickup, recovery, and strict success (Appendix E.2, Table S25).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported benchmark design and descriptive results. The supplied text does not establish public availability or licensing for the code and scene assets, provide a matched human–model comparison on the same 100 tasks, quantify run-to-run variation on repeated episodes, or establish transfer to physical robots.
