# JoVox: Joint Hierarchical Flow Matching for Lightweight Voxel Generation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.10690) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10690)


## One-sentence takeaway
JoVox jointly generates coarse and fine voxel levels, reporting better shape fidelity and substantially cheaper training than TRELLIS.2 in the supplied object-generation experiments (§§5.1–5.3).

## Problem and motivation
Dense voxel generators process empty space and may require a separate autoencoder; hierarchical generators save computation but commit to coarse structure before finer evidence exists. The authors identify these unrevised coarse decisions as a source of errors inherited by finer levels (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Joint hierarchical generation:** Masked discrete flow matching operates across a growing voxel hierarchy, allowing unresolved coarse cells to use already generated finer cells nearby. It directly models surface occupancy without an autoencoder (§4.1).
- **Two-level prediction:** Each processed cell predicts eight distributions over child occupancy codes, covering cells two levels finer while processing only alternating hierarchy levels (§4.2).
- **Age-based remasking:** The sampler revisits the oldest eligible decisions after more context becomes available, removing dependent descendants when their ancestor codes are remasked (§4.5; Appendix B).

A transformer processes tokens from all represented levels together; image conditioning uses frozen DINOv3 features (§4.3; Appendix B).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Better image-conditioned geometry | On 1,000 unseen Toys4k objects at 64³, authors report 11% lower voxel Chamfer distance than TRELLIS.2 and IoU 0.416 versus 0.402, after orientation/scale alignment. | §5.1; Table 2 discussion; Appendix C |
| Better downstream meshes | Using the same TRELLIS.2 mesh stage, JoVox voxels yield 22% lower outer-surface Chamfer distance than TRELLIS.2 voxels on Toys4k. | §5.1; Table 2 discussion |
| Better unconditional distributions | Averaged over four ShapeNet categories, authors report best results on all six metrics; FID_V and FID_VD are respectively 80% and 64% below the best baseline values. | §5.2; Table 3 discussion |
| Cheaper training step | Same backbone, batch 32, one 80-GB A100, checkpointing and mixed precision: 29.0 versus 47.3 GB peak memory and 8× training throughput relative to TRELLIS.2’s voxel stage. | §5.3; Figure 5 discussion |
| Lower forward-pass computation | Authors report average forward cost of 0.82 versus 12.2 TFLOPs for JoVox and TRELLIS.2’s voxel stage, approximately 1/15. | §5.3 |
| Joint modeling improves fidelity | With architecture, training data and step count shared, joint two-level modeling reduces voxel CD by 22% and mesh CD_shell by 32% versus level-by-level two-level generation on Toys4k. | §5.4; Table 4 discussion |
| Two-level prediction improves speed | Level-by-level variants on four A100s, batch 256: 4.03 versus 8.96 seconds per training step, a reported 2.2× speedup over single-level prediction; every reported quality metric also improves. | §5.4; Table 4 discussion |
| Remasking choice matters | On the same image-conditioned model, age-based remasking gives lowest voxel CD and highest IoU among the tested samplers. | §5.4; Table 5 discussion |

These are author-reported results. The controlled backbone and component comparisons support the proposed efficiency and quality benefits within these settings. However, most table bodies are absent from the supplied text, preventing inspection of full scores and margins. The FLOP comparison concerns a forward pass, not complete sampling.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Training resources limit the dataset; independently sampled codes can still disagree despite remasking (§6).
- **Analyst inference:** Official baseline weights provide practical comparisons, but different training datasets and budgets prevent attributing every cross-model quality gain solely to architecture (§§5–6).
- **Analyst inference:** Ground-truth-assisted rotation and scale alignment means the geometry scores do not establish accuracy in the original generated coordinate frame (Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference:** Quantitative evidence centers on individual objects at 64³. The 256³ extension is illustrated qualitatively; large scenes and shape sequences remain future work (§6; Appendices A–B).
- **Analyst inference:** “Lightweight” describes processing cost rather than a small model: the transformer has 1.54 billion parameters (Appendix B).

## Open research questions
- **Does the advantage persist at higher resolution?** The 256³ extension lacks quantitative comparisons. Next experiment: compare quality, memory and complete sampling time against baselines at that resolution (Appendices A–B).
- **Can dependencies between simultaneously sampled codes be modeled better?** The authors acknowledge residual inconsistencies. Next experiment: compare age-based remasking with dependency-aware sampling under matched compute (§6).
- **How does performance scale with training data?** Larger-data gains are expected rather than demonstrated. Next experiment: vary training-set size while controlling architecture and training compute (§6).
- **Can the method handle scenes or temporal sequences efficiently?** These are proposed applications without reported evaluation. Next experiment: measure quality and token/memory growth on multi-object scenes or shape sequences (§6).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** Method descriptions and narrative results are detailed, but numerical bodies of Tables 2–9 are missing. Material unavailable information includes full baseline scores, exact inference timings, uncertainty estimates or repeated-run variability, the final main-model training length, and confirmed code/weight availability. TRELLIS.2’s training-step count is explicitly reported as unavailable (§5.3).
