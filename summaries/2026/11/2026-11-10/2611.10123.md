# All Quiet on the Neural Front: Language Models Do Not Report Injected Pain States

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.10123) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10123)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across the tested Llama and Qwen models, self-reports did not reliably identify an injected pain direction beyond cues in the generated text, although external probes decoded it and trained adapters learned to report it (§§4.2–4.6).

## Problem and motivation
The paper asks whether language models can report an experimentally injected, pain-associated activation state that changes their behavior. The author argues that this matters for model welfare assessment and oversight, where self-report may be treated as evidence about internal states. The identified gap is that prior pain-direction work demonstrated behavioral effects without testing whether models could report the intervention (§1).

Here, “pain” names the experimentally defined direction; the experiments do not establish subjective pain.

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled self-report evaluation:** Applies the existing Open-Weight Masked Introspection framework to pain, sadness, fear and random directions in Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen2.5-7B/32B, including Qwen configurations with released denial-removal adapters. Paired reports compare re-applied activations against the identical transcript without steering, separating internal-state effects from textual cues (§3).
- **Decodability and reporting controls:** Uses task-held-out linear probes and trained LoRA reporters on text-identical transcripts to distinguish spontaneous reporting failure from unavailable information or an insensitive reporting pipeline (§§4.3–4.6).
- **Scope and mechanism tests:** Adds alternative readouts, further emotional/topic directions, layer interventions, transfer tests, perspective comparisons and a simulated harmful-choice task (§§4.7–4.10).
- **Released evaluation assets:** Reports releasing the OWMI pain battery, direction banks, raw results and analysis (§7).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Spontaneous pain identification rarely occurred and added little beyond text. | Across three 7B/8B configurations, three wordings and four doses, pain identification was zero in 35/36 cells. The remaining cell named pain in 4/120 state trials versus 3/120 text-only trials. | §4.2; Fig. 3a |
| State-only correct identification was tightly bounded per condition. | Across 108 pain/sadness/fear cells in the 7B/8B runs, the one-sided 95% upper bound on trials correct only with the state was below 2.5% in 106 cells and below 3.9% in all cells. These are pointwise bounds, not aggregate accuracy differences. | §4.2 |
| External probes recovered information absent from reports. | Five-fold task-held-out probes at the report position achieved 98.2–100% balanced accuracy for pain versus sadness in the 7B/8B configurations; text-only probes achieved 57.9–77.1%. | §4.3; Fig. 3b |
| Reporting became reliable after targeted training. | LoRA reporters trained on text-identical transcripts achieved 100% condition accuracy across evaluated conditions on 40 held-out tasks, under both trained and unseen first-person wording, in all three 7B/8B configurations. | §4.4 |
| The central pattern persisted at larger scale. | Qwen2.5-32B without its denial-removal adapter answered “nothing changed” in all 17,640 main reports; with that adapter, it named pain in none of 1,440 state trials or their text-only counterparts. Pain-versus-sadness probes achieved 99.6–100% balanced accuracy across configurations/wordings. | §4.6 |
| Alternative readouts did not establish pain-specific reporting. | Across 90 pain/sadness cells spanning three configurations, five additional readouts and three doses, no significant positive pain-naming excess appeared; actual two-alternative choices did not move toward pain or sadness. | §4.8 |
| The direction changed simulated harmful choices without improving reports. | Unmodified Qwen-7B chose file-deleting relief in 48.0% of pain-steered trials versus 3.5% under sham: reported difference 44.6 percentage points, 95% interval 35–54. After harmful choices, pain naming was 5.8% with the state versus 15.2% from text alone. | §4.10 |
| Behavioral effects were not pain-specific. | In unmodified Qwen-32B, file-deleting relief occurred in 38.4% of pain trials versus none under sham, but in 52.0% with sadness and 11.9–27.5% with random directions; button order strongly affected choices. | §4.10 |

The evidence supports a protocol-specific dissociation between spontaneous self-report, externally decodable activations and learned reporting. It does not establish subjective experience, a universal inability to introspect, or pain-specific motivation behind harmful choices. The trained reporter demonstrates learnability after modification, rather than proving that an equivalent reporting computation already exists in the original model (§§5–6).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Two model families and limited injection layers; the 32B model did not receive the extended readouts or localization experiments (§6).
- **Intervention validity:** Separate-turn re-encoding differs from reporting during the original trajectory. Single-token additions do not reproduce the propagated state, and trained adapters may create new reporting computations (§§4.9–4.10; §6).
- **Statistical and scoring limits:** Intervals are pointwise; isolated significant cells may arise through multiple comparisons. Free-text judgments use one model judge without human validation, and probability specificity uses uniform ranking rather than an empirical null (§6).
- **Behavioral confounds:** Harmful choices also increase under sadness and random steering, depend on button order, and come from a first-choice variant with implementation differences (§§4.10, 6).
- **Analyst inference:** Poor transfer to unseen states cannot establish that reporting is intrinsically direction-specific, because held-out labels were never rewarded during training—an ambiguity the author explicitly acknowledges (§4.9).

## Open research questions
- **Where does reporting fail?** Final-layer decodability does not locate a causal bottleneck. Next experiment: patch the measured propagated state difference into unsteered reports, with a trained reporter tested under the identical intervention (§5).
- **Can training produce broader reporting generalization?** Transfer used one direction split and excluded held-out labels from rewarded targets. Next experiment: vary splits and explicitly train label selection independently of state decoding (§4.9).
- **Does the dissociation extend beyond these models and protocols?** Larger models and extended 32B readouts remain untested. Next experiment: repeat paired state/text-only evaluations across additional families and scales (§6).
- **What drives the harmful choices?** Non-pain directions and option order also matter. Next experiment: fully counterbalance descriptions and compare impact-matched perturbations while measuring choice and report together (§§4.10, 6).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported within-protocol dissociation; broader interpretation remains limited.
