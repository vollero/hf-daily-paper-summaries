# SWAM: Scaling World Action Models with Cross-Modal Alignment

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.12870) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12870)


## One-sentence takeaway

SWAM’s experiments show that richer video captions, paired human action supervision, and a video-mediated language pathway improve manipulation generalization, reaching 61.56% RoboTwin Hard success and 40.00% real-world Hard success in the main comparison (Table 10).

## Problem and motivation

World action models jointly predict future video and robot actions from language and observations. The authors identify a supervision gap: adding action learning may disrupt the language–video capabilities inherited from video pretraining, yet existing work provides limited guidance on preserving those capabilities while learning control. They study how annotation quality, action labels, source mixtures, and architecture affect downstream generalization (§§1, 4).

## Approach and contributions

- **Multisource corpus and supervision pipeline.** The authors curate 47,016.7 hours of general videos, egocentric videos, in-house UMI demonstrations, and robot demonstrations. Manipulation data receive detailed captions, scene and lighting descriptions, and temporal segmentation where applicable; valid action labels receive additional supervision (Table 1; §§3.2–3.3; Appendix A).
- **Controlled supervision and mixing studies.** Experiments vary caption detail and alignment, UMI action-label coverage, Ego–UMI sampling ratios, and training exposure. These empirical studies are the central contribution beyond assembling data (§§4.2–4.4).
- **Video-mediated action modeling.** A pretrained Wan video branch and randomly initialized action branch retain separate streams, with information flowing from video to action. Only the video branch directly attends to text. Joint flow-matching losses train video on all samples and actions only where labels are valid (§3.1).
- **Resulting recipe and assets.** The combined recipe is evaluated in simulation and on real robots. The authors state that they release curated data, training code, and weights; the supplied text does not establish their accessibility (§§4.5, 5).

## Evidence and main results

All numerical results below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Richer captions improve randomized-scene control | With 20k-step Ego–UMI pretraining, RoboTwin Hard SR is 47.20% with Brief Subtask captions, 53.52% with Detailed, and 55.04% with Detailed + environment information. | §4.2.1; Table 3 |
| Caption correspondence matters | Shuffling Detailed Subtask captions within trajectories lowers RoboTwin Hard SR from 53.52% to 52.00%. | Table 3 |
| UMI actions add value beyond the same videos | Retaining all UMI videos while increasing action-label coverage from 0% to 100% raises RoboTwin Hard SR from 46.72% to 51.28%, under the same pretraining budget. | §4.2.2; Table 5 |
| Mixed human experience outperforms either source alone | At a fixed 20k-step budget, equal Ego–UMI sampling achieves 53.84% RoboTwin Hard SR versus 42.56% for Ego only and 51.28% for UMI only. | Table 7 |
| Language routing affects generalization | Under the same 30k-step multisource setup, the proposed video-mediated conditioning achieves 57.36% RoboTwin Hard SR versus 52.48% for standard Dual-stream and 52.56% for Single-stream. | §4.3; Table 8 |
| Longer pretraining helps, but simulation gains fluctuate | RoboTwin Hard SR peaks at 59.60% versus 34.56% without multisource pretraining, then falls to 56.24% at the last checkpoint. Real-world Hard SR/PSR rise from 33.33%/46.90% at approximately 22.5k training-video hours to 48.89%/61.43% at 90k hours. | §4.4; Fig. 6; Appendix D.2.2 |
| Main simulation comparison favors SWAM on Hard | Across 50 RoboTwin tasks, with Clean-only post-training and 100 episodes per task/setting, SWAM achieves 88.10% Easy and 61.56% Hard SR; OpenWAM achieves 89.30% and 48.70%. | §4.1; Table 10 |
| Main real-world comparison favors SWAM on Hard | Equal average over three tasks, each with 30 trials per setting: SWAM Hard SR is 40.00% versus OpenWAM’s 23.33%; Hard PSR is 50.36% versus π0.5’s 37.78%. | Table 10; Appendix C |

The evidence supports improvements under the tested manipulation and scene-shift protocols. It does **not** directly establish preservation of pretrained language–video alignment: that mechanism is the authors’ interpretation of downstream control ablations, without a reported direct retention metric. Scaling increases exposure to a fixed corpus rather than demonstrating gains from additional unique data (Fig. 6).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Complex reasoning, explicit multistage planning, and long-range tasks remain limited; restricted historical context may contribute (§5).
- **Analyst inference—evaluation scope:** Real-world evidence covers three tasks on one bimanual platform, with separate task-specific models. This limits conclusions about other embodiments or broad task transfer (§4.1).
- **Analyst inference—uncertainty:** No confidence intervals or repeated-training-seed analysis are reported; Appendix B lists one random seed. Small ablation differences therefore have uncertain robustness.
- **Analyst inference—scaling attribution:** Added source exposure sometimes also adds training steps, so those comparisons do not isolate source value from compute. The fixed-budget mixture experiment addresses this more directly. Extra UMI exposure and simulation scaling are not uniformly beneficial (Tables 6–7; Fig. 6).
- **Protocol boundary:** Real-world scores permit up to five additional autonomous replanning attempts after an initial failure; they measure completion under that retry protocol (Appendix C.2).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** The main comparison reports 40.00% real-world Hard SR, while the longest scaling checkpoint reaches 48.89%. The exact checkpoint-selection rationale for the main result is not explained. Table 9 and Table 10 also report slightly different simulation endpoints.

## Open research questions

1. **Does richer supervision actually preserve language–video capabilities?** Control gains are indirect evidence. Next experiment: measure held-out language-conditioned video performance before and after action learning across caption variants.
2. **How robust is equal Ego–UMI sampling?** Its advantage is established at one fixed ablation budget. Next experiment: repeat ratio sweeps across training budgets and seeds.
3. **Does the recipe transfer across embodiments?** Evaluation concentrates on bimanual end-effector manipulation. Next experiment: hold out an embodiment during pretraining and compare adaptation with matched demonstration budgets.
4. **Can memory improve long-horizon control?** The authors explicitly identify historical-context and planning limitations. Next experiment: compare the existing model with memory or hierarchical planning on tasks requiring earlier observations (§5).

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text provides substantial methods, ablations, and evaluation protocols, but causal alignment claims and broader generalization remain less established.

Material missing information includes direct alignment-retention measurements, statistical uncertainty, compute and inference latency, quantitative caption-quality audits, pretraining/evaluation overlap checks, complete baseline training configurations, exact final-checkpoint selection, and verified release locations.
