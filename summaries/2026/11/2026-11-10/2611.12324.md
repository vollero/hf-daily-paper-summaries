# How Many Exposures Does a World Model Need? Learning Unseen Game Mechanics at Test Time

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.12324) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12324)


## One-sentence takeaway
On held-out ARC-AGI-3 games, the authors report that online test-time training lets WARC reach sustained ≥80% changed-cell accuracy on 56 of 69 recurring mechanics, while also improving a fixed explorer’s action efficiency (§6.2; Fig. 11; §7.2).

## Problem and motivation
A world model entering an unfamiliar game must learn its action effects from limited experience. Agent scores mix this learning with exploration and goal discovery, while whole-frame accuracy can conceal failure on meaningful changes: copying the previous frame already predicts 98.8% of cells correctly in this setting (§4).

The authors identify a need for a common adaptation protocol that measures how quickly individual mechanics become predictable, independently of action selection (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **MechEval:** Program instrumentation identifies which mechanics fire and which cells they change. It evaluates 69 recurring mechanics across 20 games and 279 human recordings. Predictions precede training on each transition, and methods follow identical recordings (§4).
- **Exposure-based measurement:** An exposure counts a mechanic’s earlier occurrences. Exposures to criterion (EtC) is the first exposure after which its averaged learning curve remains ≥80% through the available recording horizon (§4).
- **WARC:** A roughly 35M-parameter vision transformer predicts the next frame from three frames and actions. Pretrained on other games, it takes 20 gradient steps after each valid transition, updating all parameters with recent and replayed transitions plus rotations. It adapts VARC’s existing architecture and training approach to an interactive stream (§5; Appendix C).
- **WARC-Agent:** A fixed graph explorer ranks actions by predicted outcome novelty, providing a controlled test of whether adaptation improves play (§7).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Adaptation substantially improves prediction | On 69 mechanics in held-out games, accuracy averaged over all exposures is 69.1% for WARC versus 18.4% frozen, 35.0% from scratch, and 32.9% episodic retrieval. | §§6.2, 6.4; Table 1 discussion |
| Most evaluated mechanics reach criterion | WARC reaches sustained ≥80% accuracy on 56/69 mechanics in each test-time seed; the authors report half reach criterion within about 80 exposures. Frozen WARC reaches 10 mechanics. | §6.2; Fig. 11 caption |
| Learning depends on relevant transitions | Withholding target-mechanic transitions from training leaves accuracy 42.9 percentage points below normal WARC, with reported interval [−48.3, −34.0], across 32 targets in 144 recordings. | Appendix E; Fig. 14 |
| Generalization extends beyond exact repeats | After 10–19 exposures, unseen level-1 occurrences in other recordings reach about 80% accuracy for direct keys and 70% for clicks; level-2 clicks reach only about 40%. | §6.3; Fig. 3 |
| Immediate updates help early learning | Updating every five transitions, with matched gradient-step budget, reduces accuracy over the first four exposures by 10.5 points. LoRA matches full updates overall. | §6.4; Appendix G |
| Better prediction improves fixed-explorer efficiency | Across 25 public games, 10 seeds, and the first 1,000 actions, RHAE rises from 0.15 for Graph to 0.18 frozen and 0.46 adapted, on the official 0–100 scale. Adapted versus frozen gain: +0.27 [0.10, 0.48]. | §7.2; Table 3 discussion |
| Completion improvement is less certain | Level-1 clears rise from 34% frozen to 46% adapted under the same public-game protocol; paired difference is +12 points [−3, 27]. | §7.2; Appendix I.3 |
| Hidden-game performance is separately reported | Kaggle public-leaderboard score is 0.20, evaluated on half of the competition’s 110 hidden games using a model pretrained on all public games. | Appendix A |

These results support adaptation and partial generalization within this benchmark. They do not establish complete rule recovery, arbitrary long-term retention, or general superiority of gradient updates over recurrent adaptation: the recurrent comparison uses pretraining on only 16–17 games (§6.4). EtC describes an averaged curve through a finite observed horizon, not guaranteed accuracy on every future occurrence.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** MechEval covers only levels 1–2 of games whose changes can be attributed to individual objects. Rare mechanics and game goals fall outside its recurring-mechanic evaluation (§8; Appendix B).
- **Incomplete generalization:** Recorded human trajectories sample only some situations. New objects, directions, and levels remain harder; reaching criterion is explicitly weaker than learning a rule generally (§8; Appendix F).
- **Incorrect extrapolation:** On first blocked moves, WARC preserves only 58% of cells that should remain unchanged, versus 89% for frozen WARC (§6.3).
- **Explorer bottleneck:** Even an oracle world model clears no level in five games; novelty-based selection does not explicitly pursue goals (§8; Appendix I.3).
- **Compute and comparison limits:** Equal exposure or gradient-step budgets do not establish equal runtime efficiency. Timing on two key-controlled games gives 0.74 seconds per action, mostly spent updating; click games receive fewer actions under the competition time budget (Appendix A).
- **Scaling-study caveat:** Broader-pretraining evidence uses a different architecture, one pretraining seed, five held-out games, and a recipe developed while inspecting those games (Appendix H).

## Open research questions
1. **Does adaptation transfer to stochastic or continuous environments?** These settings are untested (§8). Next experiment: instrument such simulators and compare exposure curves and erroneous edits under matched compute budgets.
2. **Can stronger recurrent adaptation close the gap?** Current results are limited to a small pretraining game set (§6.4). Next experiment: scale game diversity while comparing recurrent and gradient-based models under matched resources.
3. **Can models learn exceptions without losing useful generalization?** First blocked moves expose overgeneralization (§6.3). Next experiment: vary exposure to blocking events and measure transfer to unseen obstacles.
4. **How much would explicit goal modeling improve play?** The oracle explorer still misses solvable games and goal-completing actions (Appendix I.3). Next experiment: hold WARC fixed and compare novelty ranking with a goal-conditioned selector.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported within-benchmark findings; broader applicability remains untested.
