# Longtail-SWE: High-Quality SWE Tasks for Underrepresented Languages

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.10304) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10304)


## One-sentence takeaway
Longtail-SWE uses agent-guided repair of task descriptions and tests to recover 202 repository-level coding tasks across 13 underrepresented programming languages, increasing retained task yield while leaving final benchmark error rates independently unverified (§§3, 5–6).

## Problem and motivation
Repository-level coding benchmarks provide limited coverage of many language ecosystems. Historical issue–PR pairs also contain incomplete specifications, overly restrictive tests, and insufficient coverage; filtering these candidates can discard valuable tasks from already scarce pools. The authors target recovery of repairable tasks while preserving their intended requirements (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Coordinated task repair:** Building on SWE-rebench V2, a solver attempts each task, sequential judges diagnose task defects, and a repairer revises tests or appends specification clarifications. Reference patches remain fixed, and revised tests undergo execution validation (§§3.1–3.4).
- **Benchmark and released assets:** Longtail-SWE contains 202 tasks from 129 repositories across 13 languages, plus an easier companion set. The authors report releasing environments, tooling, prompts, task revisions, and trajectories (§§3.5, 4.2).
- **Empirical assessment:** Construction comparisons measure recovered yield; evaluations examine nine model–agent configurations, repeated attempts, resource usage, judge reliability, and paired pre/post-repair test outcomes (§§4–5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Combined auditing improves executable yield | On the same 1,096 A-labeled candidates, strict execution-validation passes rise from 377/1,096 (34.4%) with the SWE-rebench V2 installer to 539/1,096 (49.2%) with the combined auditor. | Table 3; §5.1 |
| Expanded screening recovers additional candidates | Including repairable B2/B5 categories yields 1,247 strict-validation passes from 2,640 candidates, versus 539 from the A-only auditor pool. | Table 3 |
| More tasks survive the full funnel | From a common 3,261-candidate pool, the Longtail-SWE path retains 202 final tasks versus 68 available through the original path; downstream review stages are comparison extensions to that baseline. | Table 4 |
| Opus has the highest observed success estimates | Across 202 tasks and three attempts per configuration, Opus 5.5 (high) achieves 81.52% ± 1.16 SEM Pass@1, 89.11% Pass@3, and 73.76% All-3; GPT 6.1 Sol (high) achieves 78.38% ± 0.92 Pass@1. | Table 1; §§4.1, 4.3 |
| Resource usage differs substantially | GPT 6.1 Sol reports the lowest mean total tokens and steps: 1.75M and 27.4, versus Opus’s 4.48M and 53.2, under their evaluated configurations. | Table 1 |
| Additional attempts improve Qwen coverage | In a separate ten-attempt evaluation on the final 202 tasks, Qwen 3.8 27B Pass@1 is 43.8% and Pass@10 is 73.3%. | Figure 2; §4.3 |
| Judge recall exceeds specificity | On 65 human-audited tasks, the sequential protocol detects 49/51 defective tasks: 96.1% recall, 87.5% rejection precision, and 50.0% specificity on 14 acceptable tasks. Initial human agreement is κ=0.15. | §§4.5, 6.1 |
| Test repair changes scores for identical patches | On 63 tasks with unchanged descriptions and the same three Opus trajectories per task, Pass@1 rises from 29.1% ± 2.8 SEM to 67.2% ± 3.5; Pass@3 rises from 36.5% to 77.8%. | Table 5; §5.2 |

These results support improved candidate retention and show that test revisions materially change grading. The paired comparison isolates changed tests, but higher pass rates alone do not establish greater grading correctness; Appendix A.5 supplies illustrative examples of both corrected false rejection and corrected false acceptance. The model results compare specific model–agent configurations, and the judge audit does not measure the final benchmark’s defect rate (§6).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors:** An independent audit of released tasks is still needed. The small, mixed-stage judge audit has low initial human agreement and low specificity (§§4.5, 6).
- **Authors:** Clarifications may alter task contracts despite fixed reference patches. The paired ablation excludes description changes, and other refinement steps lack separate causal ablations (§§6–6.1).
- **Authors:** Qwen-based selection may bias Qwen scores; participation of evaluated models in construction may favor their interpretations (§6.1).
- **Authors:** Public-source contamination cannot be ruled out. Repository filters and small language subsets constrain generalization; language scores do not establish inherent language difficulty (§6.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Reported token and step means support resource comparisons within these configurations, but do not establish monetary cost or latency advantages; those measurements are absent (Table 1).

## Open research questions
1. **What defect rate remains in the released benchmark?** The human audit samples multiple pipeline stages. Next experiment: independently review a stratified sample of final tasks and adjudicate both false acceptance and false rejection (§6).
2. **Does construction-model involvement affect rankings?** The authors acknowledge possible interpretation bias. Next experiment: curate matched task pools using disjoint construction models, then evaluate the same held-out configurations (§6.1).
3. **Which refinement components improve validity?** Only test changes on unchanged-description tasks receive a paired analysis. Next experiment: compare description-only, test-only, and joint repair using independent correctness judgments (§§5.2, 6).
4. **How stable are language-level differences?** Subsets are small and filtered. Next experiment: expand repository coverage within each language and repeat evaluation under matched task-type distributions (§6.1).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported methods and results as summarized from the supplied text; confidence in final benchmark validity remains limited.

Material information unavailable here includes an independent final-task error estimate, isolated effects of description repair and construction-model choice, verified contamination status, end-to-end construction cost, evaluation latency, and the complete prompts and executable artifacts that the authors place in the external release.
