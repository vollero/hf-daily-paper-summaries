# LCM-Promo: An LLM-Based Uplift Model for Promotion Allocation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.37570) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37570)


## One-sentence takeaway
The authors report that a 4B-parameter LLM with monotonic promotion-response decoding achieved 1% relative conversion lift and 6% lower promotion cost than Segment-EMA in a one-month iFood churn-reactivation A/B test (§3.1).

## Problem and motivation
Promotion allocation requires estimating how discounts change a user’s conversion probability under a budget constraint. At iFood, offers vary with session context and discount mechanics; the existing Segment-EMA policy requires exploration within each segment-promotion cell, limiting segmentation granularity. The authors propose sharing information through serialized user histories, context, and offer options (§§1, 3.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Threshold-based response decoding:** Fine-tune a 4B dense LLM to predict one discount identifier or no purchase. Interpret the normalized token probabilities as a distribution over minimum conversion discounts; cumulative probabilities produce monotonic response curves (§2, Equations 1–2).
- **Commerce-conditioned prediction:** Combine LCM-generated behavioral summaries, session context, and discount options in prompts. Training uses observed conversion discounts from production logs as proxies for latent thresholds (§2).
- **Production integration and evaluation:** Use sampled cluster-level response curves with an existing budget-constrained allocator, and evaluate against the production policy. A waterfall soft-label variant is also tested, with worse offline performance (§3).

The randomized online comparison supports the reported policy-level benefit in this deployment. It does not establish accurate individual causal effects or identify which model component produced the gains. Offline results support competitiveness on a narrower task, not superiority; the authors explicitly make this distinction (§3.2).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Results cover one lifecycle stage on one platform; broader transfer remains untested (§4).
- **Threshold-proxy risk — analyst inference:** An observed conversion discount need not equal the minimum discount required for conversion. Users can receive multiple offers during the month, complicating the threshold interpretation (§2).
- **Monotonicity versus validity — analyst inference:** Cumulative decoding guarantees ordered predictions, but does not itself demonstrate calibration or causal identification from production logs (§2).
- **Comparison limits:** Offline models use different training sources, and the fixed-promotion benchmark omits the breadth of production offers (§3.2).
- **Missing attribution and uncertainty:** The supplied text reports no component ablations, online sample counts, confidence intervals, or significance tests. The proposed explanation for waterfall degradation is not experimentally isolated (§3).

## Open research questions
1. **Does the observed-discount proxy recover reliable response curves?** This is assumed rather than directly validated (§2). A next experiment could randomize discount depths and assess calibration and uplift across offers.
2. **Which components drive the online gains?** The reported test compares complete response models (§3.1). An ablation could vary user summaries, session inputs, and threshold decoding under the same allocator.
3. **Does the method transfer beyond churn reactivation?** The authors identify platform and lifecycle scope as limitations (§4). Replication on another lifecycle stage or commerce platform could test this.
4. **Why does waterfall training degrade performance?** The paper offers a hypothesis (§3.2). Controlled comparisons of label smoothing and training objectives could test it.
