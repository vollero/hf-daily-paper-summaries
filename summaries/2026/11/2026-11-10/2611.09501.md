# e-means: outperforming k-means (in one-tenth the epochs)

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.09501) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09501)


## One-sentence takeaway
The authors report that e-means combines mini-batch EMA updates with empirically fitted hyperparameter rules to match Flash-KMeans’ 25-epoch test MSE in 1.4–2.6 epochs, with 9.5–45.5× GPU wall-clock speedups in the evaluated settings (§4.3, Table 4).

## Problem and motivation
Lloyd’s k-means repeatedly assigns the full dataset before updating centroids, making large-scale clustering expensive. Existing mini-batch methods converge faster initially but typically sacrifice final MSE and require batch-size and learning-rate tuning. The authors target this speed–quality gap for clustering fixed vector datasets (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **An improved EMA clustering recipe:** Maintain exponentially averaged per-cluster counts and sums, deriving centroids from their ratio. Combine this existing update family with cosine step decay, low-weight initialization, and revival of underused clusters by splitting live donor clusters (§3.1).
- **Two finalization options:** Average the final epoch’s assigned points to favor training MSE, or maintain a separate slow EMA to favor held-out MSE. The former is the experimental default (§3.2).
- **Empirical hyperparameter laws:** Fit power laws on DEEP to select batch size and initial step from dataset size, cluster count, and epoch budget, then evaluate transfer to other datasets (§3.3).
- **Implementation and benchmark assets:** Provide CPU/GPU implementations, IVF-based approximate assignment for extreme scale, and DINO10B, a byte-quantized image-patch embedding benchmark with search queries and ground truth (§3.4; Appendices J, K, M).

The contribution is the combined recipe and its empirical calibration; EMA updates and donor splitting themselves build on prior methods (§§2–3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Faster matching of baseline quality | Matches Flash-KMeans’ 25-epoch test MSE in 1.4–2.6 epochs, yielding 9.5–45.5× wall-clock speedups; medians over ten seeds through 100M vectors, one seed at 1B; one GPU through 100M, eight at 1B. | §4.3, Table 4 caption |
| Lower MSE at short budgets | Randomly initialized e-means beats Flash-KMeans@25 on all eight configurations after three epochs; after five epochs it also beats the k-means++-initialized baseline, with reported error margins of 0.06–0.33%. | §4.3, Table 5 discussion |
| Advantage beyond a fixed baseline budget | On three datasets over 40 seeds, authors report lower train and test MSE than Lloyd run to convergence, including greedy k-means++ initialization. | §4.4, Fig. 5 |
| Hyperparameter-law transfer | Batch law incurs median 0.19% excess MSE on held-out datasets; its fitting target permits 0.2% above the batch curve’s minimum. Evaluation datasets include SIFT, SPACEV, AG-News, and arXiv. | §3.3; Appendix B.1 |
| Extreme-scale test-MSE result | On full DINO10B with approximately 16.7M centroids, IVF4096 with four probed lists reaches test MSE 0.033% below exact Lloyd@25 in three epochs and 23.2 GPU-hours on one eight-GPU machine; Lloyd uses 6,784 GPU-hours. | Appendix M.4, Table 21 |
| Extreme-scale training-MSE result | A more accurate IVF setting reaches train MSE 12.464 versus Lloyd’s 12.468, in the table’s ×10⁴ units, after four epochs and 43.8 GPU-hours. | Appendix M.4, Table 21 |
| Synthetic performance has exceptions | At equal 25-epoch budgets, e-means has lower training error in all 140 reported synthetic experiments, but lower held-out error in all except ten. | Appendix D.2, Figs. 10–12 |
| Retrieval benefit | On eleven in-distribution VIBE datasets, authors report faster attainment of converged Flash-KMeans recall and similar or better final recall on most datasets, using Recall@10 at fixed selectivity. | Appendix L, Figs. 24–25 |

The supplied evidence supports substantial empirical gains in clustering MSE and time to a specified baseline quality. The converged-Lloyd comparison strengthens the case beyond Lloyd@25, but covers fewer datasets. It does not establish universal superiority, global optimality, or uniformly improved downstream retrieval. Most table bodies and plotted values are absent from the supplied text, so many aggregate claims cannot be checked configuration by configuration.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** The laws are tuned for relatively high-dimensional data and are suboptimal on very low-dimensional or tiny datasets. Intrinsic dimensionality is omitted because fitting a dimension exponent was unstable (§3.3; Appendix B.1).
- **Author-reported overfitting:** Some synthetic settings show worsening test MSE late in training despite continued training-MSE gains (Appendix D.2).
- **Analyst inference—automatic tuning has boundaries:** The default recipe avoids per-dataset sweeps, but uses a large-scale batch-size adjustment; slow-EMA speeds are validation-selected, and Table 3 selects the fastest of three IVF settings (§3.3; Table 15; Table 3 caption).
- **Analyst inference—runtime comparisons need context:** Hardware, numerical precision, storage, and assignment strategy affect gains. Some training-error plots pair baseline runtime with the authors’ own Lloyd error curve rather than each implementation’s measured error (Appendices F, J, M).
- **Benchmark realism:** DINO10B intentionally abstracts away filtering, updates, temporal drift, and deployment structure (Appendix K).
- **Missing evidence:** Numerical ablation tables are largely unavailable, limiting assessment of component effects and uncertainty.

## Open research questions
- **Can intrinsic dimensionality improve automatic tuning?** The authors identify low-dimensional failures and unstable dimension fitting. A next experiment could compare the current laws with laws conditioned on estimated intrinsic dimensionality across controlled synthetic and real datasets (§3.3; Appendix B.1).
- **Can finalization and stopping be selected automatically?** Train and test objectives favor different finalizers, and late overfitting occurs. Test a validation-based stopping and finalizer-selection rule against the fixed-budget default (§3.2; Appendix D.2; Appendix I).
- **When does approximate assignment improve generalization?** The authors hypothesize that assignment noise reduces overfitting. Sweep approximation accuracy under matched compute budgets and measure both train and test MSE across datasets (Appendix M.3).
- **How reliably does lower MSE predict retrieval quality?** Retrieval gains vary across VIBE datasets. Measure the relationship between MSE and Recall@10 across cluster counts, selectivities, and training budgets (Appendix L).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The method and headline comparisons are clearly described, but the supplied text omits most numerical table bodies and figure data.

Material missing information includes fitted scaling-law coefficients, most per-dataset results and ablation magnitudes, full baseline parameter tables, and numerical retrieval curves. The finalization discussion also inconsistently identifies the most affected regime as low **n/k** (§3.2) versus low **k/n** (Appendix I).
