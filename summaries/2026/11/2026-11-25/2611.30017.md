# OWE-Bench: A long-horizon benchmark for measuring real-world engineering

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.30017) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30017)


## One-sentence takeaway

On OWE-Bench, development scores helped agents select stronger engineering submissions, yet those submissions averaged 18 normalized points higher on provided cases than on hidden cases, and models’ post-run forecasts overestimated hidden scores by 21 points (§6, Table 2, §B.7).

## Problem and motivation

Engineering agents must decide what to test, which version to submit, and when further work is worth the time. The authors argue that a final artifact score alone leaves those judgments unmeasured, while access to the final grader lets agents tune directly to the conditions meant to test generalization (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Benchmark and leaderboard.** OWE-Bench gives agents 4–18 hours per task to improve a starting artifact across 26 expert-authored tasks. An isolated verifier evaluates frozen submissions on hidden conditions using continuous scores anchored to task-specific baselines and ceilings. The authors report a 14-model leaderboard and the release of tasks and evaluation trajectories (§§2–5, §9.1).
- **Development-evidence analysis.** Where both sets of cases can be scored, the verifier applies the same scoring rule to measure the development–hidden gap. The study compares selection by development score with random and hindsight selection, and separately measures models’ forecasts from run logs (§6, §B.7).
- **Time-budget experiments.** The authors sweep deadlines, test whether a stated deadline changes behavior before it is reached, and compare one full-length run with two half-length attempts under the same allotted budget (§7, §§B.9–B.10).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

The evidence shows that development scores can rank candidate submissions while overestimating their absolute performance on unseen cases. The size and interpretation of the gap depend on how the cases were constructed. Deployment readiness and the best allocation of time in other settings remain unmeasured (§6.2, §B.5, §9).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ limitations.** The expert-built tasks were filtered for difficulty, and some evaluate performance in simulation. Scores describe this suite and depend on task-specific baselines and ceilings. The gap analysis excludes five tasks, and deliberate distribution shifts can produce gaps even without overfitting (§§2, 4, 6, 9).
- **Authors’ limitations.** Models forecast from logs after the run. The validation experiment gives a new run the finished artifact without the original context, and the budget split tests only one full run against two half runs (§9, §§B.7, B.9, B.11).
- **Analyst inference.** The observed forecast bias cannot be assigned directly to confidence at submission. Likewise, the pooled null estimate for the validation prompt leaves open whether checks designed around genuinely different conditions would improve transfer (§6.3, §§B.7, B.11).

## Open research questions

1. **Does broader validation improve transfer?** The continuation experiment mainly reused provided cases. Randomize equal-budget runs between more checks on existing cases and checks designed to cover new operating conditions, then compare hidden scores and forecast calibration (§6.3, §B.11).
2. **Are agents equally overconfident at submission?** The explicit forecasts were collected afterward from logs. Collect a forecast immediately before submission and a separate log-based forecast for the same run, then compare bias and interval coverage (§B.7).
3. **When does restarting pay off?** The split test covers one restart strategy on five tasks and two models. Compare several budget splits at matched total time and measure whether development scores’ ability to rank attempts predicts which split wins (§B.9).
