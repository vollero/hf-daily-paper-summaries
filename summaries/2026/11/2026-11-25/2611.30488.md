# Do learned image captioning metrics generalize? Auditing duplicated samples in human-judgment benchmarks

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.30488) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30488)


## One-sentence takeaway

Cross-collection duplicates raised Pearl’s Kendall correlation on Polaris but had little measured effect on DENEB, while much of the headline correlation came from detecting lowest-scored captions rather than ranking the rest (§§5–6).

## Problem and motivation

Learned captioning metrics use human ratings to score candidate captions, and their agreement with held-out ratings helps determine which captioning systems appear better. Polaris, Nebula, and Spica reuse images and judgments, so cross-collection evaluations can include labeled examples the evaluator saw during training. Prior work had identified shared image–caption pairs, but the authors identify an unresolved question about whether those pairs cross train–test boundaries. They also ask how much headline Kendall correlation reflects fine-grained caption ranking (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Content-level audit.** Match decoded image pixels and candidate text across three training sets and six evaluation sets, then check how much relaxed matching adds and whether duplicate labels agree (§4).
- **Inflation estimates.** Compare released and deduplicated training sets within the same test set, using random removal, a matched removal control for Pearl, image-level cross-fitting, and per-epoch checkpoints (§5).
- **Benchmark diagnosis.** Decompose Kendall’s \(\tau_c\) exactly into lowest-score detection and ranking above that score. Use paired image bootstraps, Holm correction, and agreement between annotators to assess which differences the benchmarks resolve (§§3.3, 6).
- **Protocol and re-ranking.** Apply split-aware eligibility, separate score reporting, paired tests, and seed averaging to 33 metrics, including a frozen-feature ridge baseline and zero-shot vision–language judges. The authors report releasing exact and relaxed filter lists and analysis scripts (§7, Data availability).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-collection overlap is substantial | Spica and Nebula training contain 68.4% and 50.2% of Polaris test samples, respectively. The Spica overlap is 32% when counted by unique image–caption pair. Mean ratings agree exactly on 96.7% and 99.9% of the duplicated pairs, respectively. | §4.2, Fig. 2, Appendix B |
| Pearl shows a duplicate-specific gain | On Polaris, all-sample \(100\times\tau_c\) was 61.32 with released training and 57.61 after deduplication. The gain on duplicates minus the gain on unseen images was +5.24 points (95% CI +4.08 to +6.42). The released-versus-deduplicated gap on duplicates widened from 2.5 to 10.4 points across observed epochs. | §§5.2, 5.5, Figs. 4 and 6 |
| DENEB shows a smaller effect | On Polaris, the same duplicate-minus-unseen contrast was +0.33 \(100\times\tau_c\) points (95% CI −0.10 to +0.76). The released-versus-deduplicated gap on duplicates peaked at 0.9 points across comparable epochs. | §§5.2, 5.5, Appendix F |
| Headline correlation is dominated by lowest-score detection | Detection accounted for about 70% of the leading metric’s all-sample \(\tau_c\) on Polaris and nearly all of the leading metric’s all-sample \(\tau_b\) on Flickr8k-CF. On Polaris, recent metrics clustered around 0.96 detection AUC. | §6.1, Fig. 7, Appendix H |
| Recent sets rarely separate the learned regressors | For all-sample Kendall correlation, Holm-corrected paired tests separated Polos, DENEB, and Pearl in 1 of 9 comparisons across recent sets and 8 of 9 across earlier sets. SigLIP ridge ranked fourth on all three recent sets, ahead of the three regressors by 0.3–2.1 points, with several gaps within benchmark uncertainty. | §6.3, Fig. 8, §7.2, Appendix I |
| Eligibility changes the leaderboard | Released Pearl exceeded every ranked metric in 99.8% of Polaris bootstrap draws by all-sample \(100\times\tau_c\). Deduplicated Pearl ranked 12th. Zero-shot judges led the recent sets, and Rigel led the earlier benchmarks, with a one-sample overlap exception on Composite. | §7.2, Figs. 9–10 |

The authors’ matched control and epoch curves support memorization as an explanation for Pearl’s duplicate-specific gain. The audit identifies exposure, while the retraining experiments estimate how much it matters for particular learners. The rankings describe agreement with the ratings on these benchmarks. Rigel’s eligibility on the recent sets and possible pretraining exposure for the judges remain unresolved (§§5.7, 7.2, 8).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ caveats.** Deduplication was nonrandom and also reduced performance on clean samples. The image-only subset above the lowest score had wide uncertainty. Rigel had only an inference checkpoint, leaving its inflation estimate indirect. Three evaluation sets lacked an annotator-agreement reference, and judge pretraining exposure and preference for captions from related models could not be tested (§§5.3, 5.7, 8).
- **Analyst inference.** Because Nebula and Spica share underlying judgments, agreement between their rankings provides limited independent confirmation. The repetition explanation is plausible, but the reported comparison changes collections along with repetition frequency, leaving its causal contribution uncertain (§§3.1, 5.6, Appendix C).

## Open research questions

1. **How much inflation is caused by repeated exposure to the same pair?** Spica and Nebula differ in how they store judgments and in their training data. Randomly vary duplicate multiplicity for a fixed learner and fixed pairs while holding training updates constant (§5.6).
2. **Does sharing an image affect scores when the candidate caption is new?** The informative image-only subset was small. Build a larger, independently rated same-image holdout and vary exposure to other captions for those images (§5.3).
3. **Would a clean Rigel retrain retain its lead on recent sets?** The released checkpoint was ineligible there and the indirect test had limited sensitivity. Retrain Rigel on matched leaked and deduplicated splits, then compare it with the zero-shot judges on the same images (§§5.7, 7.2).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the headline findings reported in the supplied text. Several cited tables appear only as captions, leaving some split-by-split counts, complete rankings and rank intervals, and per-epoch contrasts unavailable for inspection. The text also lacks a deduplicated Rigel run, image-level VLM pretraining provenance, and a proprietary-judge comparison under the same protocol.
