# Self-Evolving Evaluation Environments for Computer-Use Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.30962) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30962)


## One-sentence takeaway

CUA-SEE uses agent rollouts to co-evolve desktop tasks and executable verifiers, producing a harder, broader benchmark and targeted capability probes, with 84.0% verifier–human agreement in a Kimi-only audit (§§4.1–4.3).

## Problem and motivation

Fixed desktop benchmarks can lose their ability to distinguish agents as tasks are mastered, and aggregate scores can hide which capabilities or software features cause failures. Expanding these benchmarks by hand is costly because success depends on application-specific state and multiple task requirements. The authors identify a gap in evaluation systems that jointly adapt task difficulty, feature coverage, and verification as new rollout evidence arrives (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Closed-loop evaluation.** CUA-SEE feeds validity-filtered trajectories and checkpoint outcomes into the next round of task and verifier generation (§3, Fig. 3).
- **Cohort-calibrated benchmark evolution.** Aligned rollouts from multiple agents guide task variants that retain a common semantic requirement while expanding measurable difficulty and software-operation coverage across the suite (§3.1).
- **Agent-specific diagnostic evolution.** A planner selects uncertain capability boundaries. Task generators raise intensity after clean passes, lower it after clean failures, and simplify or repeat ambiguous probes (§3.2).
- **Requirement-level verifier evolution.** Application adapters normalize native state into CanonicalState. Executable checkpoints track individual requirements, record observability gaps, and undergo independent successful-state construction and negative-fixture testing (§3.3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Evolved tasks were more demanding overall | Across five agents on 100 matched lineages in eight apps, the macro-average normalized score fell from 60.6 in R1 to 47.5 in R4, and mean trajectory length rose from 27.4 to 39.3 steps under a 50-step cap. Three agents scored higher in R2 than R1. | §4.1, Table 2, Appendix B.1 |
| Difficulty increased for held-out agents | From native OSWorld R0 to evolved R4, overall scores fell by 26.9 points for Qwen and 24.0 points for GPT. Both were held out from benchmark evolution. | §4 setup, §4.4, Table 2 |
| Exact operation coverage broadened | Per-round coverage across the eight apps grew from 127 exact software–category–operation cells in R0 to 464 in R4, or 3.65×. The R4 registry frontier was 487 cells. | §4.1, Table 3, Appendix B.3 |
| Diagnostic profiles varied by agent and capability | In R0–R3 matrices pooling eligible episodes for Gemini, Kimi, and Seed, the authors report stronger Gemini performance on precision and grounding than on high-scale consistency. Matrix values are mean checkpoint scores on contributing tasks. | §4.2, Figs. 6 and 13, Appendix B.3 |
| Verifiers showed agreement with human judgments | On 400 Kimi trajectories across four evolution rounds, binary agreement was 84.0% with Cohen’s κ of 0.640. Reported agreement rose from 81% to 89%. | §4.3, Fig. 9 caption |
| Checkpoints covered most extracted requirements | Across 962 tasks in the diagnostic verifier analysis, 92.5% of extracted atomic requirements were covered, with a mean of 4.82 checkpoints per task. | §4.3, Figs. 8 and 12 |

The paired lineages, longer trajectories, and score declines on held-out agents support the authors’ claim that the evolved benchmark became more demanding in this setting. App-level differences and R2 score rebounds show that difficulty growth varied across rounds. The held-out results cover two agents within the same desktop setup, and the human audit measures agreement for one agent. The authors argue that their validation checks rule out verifier drift (§4.4). My inference is that the switch from native OSWorld verifiers in R0 to CanonicalState verifiers in later rounds leaves the verifier’s contribution to the R0–R4 score change unresolved.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported.** Incomplete adapter state, partial requirement coverage, and state-field mismatches accounted for most audited disagreements. Sound partial verifiers remain eligible in the main analyses, and some diagnostic cells lack clean observations (§§3.3 and 4.3, Appendix B.3).
- **Author-reported.** Diagnostic cells pool tasks that can vary in software, feature, lineage, and supporting demands. The R0 diagnostic labels are reconstructed from R1 profiles. These choices limit causal and baseline interpretation (Appendix B.3).
- **Analyst inference.** The longitudinal results cover eight of the 20 instrumented apps, and the trajectory audit covers only Kimi. Generalization of the reported effects across apps and agents remains uncertain (§4, Appendix B.1).
- **Analyst inference.** The supplied experiment reports lack a matched-budget comparison that isolates the contribution of adaptive probe selection or independent verifier cross-review (§§4.2–4.3).

## Open research questions

1. **Does adaptive probing localize boundaries faster than a fixed probe schedule?** The paper defines bracket metrics and gives case studies, while leaving the matched-budget comparison open. Test adaptive and fixed schedules on the same agent–lineage pairs and compare bracket widths at equal rollout budgets (§4.2, Fig. 15, Appendix B.3).
2. **How much of the cross-round score change depends on the verifier?** R0 and evolved rounds use different verifier families. For matched lineages, have humans score both rounds under a shared rubric and compare those score changes with automated scores (§4 setup, §4.3).
3. **Does verifier agreement transfer across agents and apps?** The human audit covers Kimi, and the authors report app-specific observability gaps. Run a blinded audit stratified by agent, app, and full versus partial verifier status (§4.3, Appendix B.3).

## Confidence and missing information

**Medium.** The supplied text describes the pipeline, matched evaluation panel, and main aggregate results. It omits a direct download location for the reported published artifacts, paired confidence-interval values for the headline changes, and details on the human audit’s annotator count and adjudication. Figure 9’s caption identifies the human-agreement figure, while §4.3 points to Figure 10, whose caption describes evolution costs.
