# Lazy Agents Answer Against Their Own Beliefs: Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Beliefs Teaches Them to Act on What They Believe

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.32884) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32884)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across three Qwen vision-language models and three benchmarks, Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Beliefs (RLVB) improved answer accuracy primarily by teaching agents to gather more evidence before committing.

## Problem and motivation

Agents in partially observable tasks often commit to an answer even when their stated belief still lists several possibilities. A terminal correctness reward gives a lucky guess the same credit as a well-supported answer, while a judge with access to the hidden target can reward claims the agent’s observations do not support. The authors identify a gap in turn-level supervision for deciding when to keep investigating and when to answer (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Posterior-based process reward.** The simulator computes the exact posterior from the evidence available to the agent. A teacher uses lookahead on that posterior to value evidence-gathering and answer actions. The main reward is the log probability the teacher assigns to the chosen action (§§3.1–3.3).
- **Updates that compare turns under the same evidence.** RLVB samples several turns at each history and weights them using a group-relative policy gradient or expectation maximization (EM). The update trains the entire turn, including the stated belief. The main policy-gradient variant scores only the action, while the reported EM variant also scores the belief line (§§3.3–3.4).
- **Benchmark and behavioral analysis.** The authors introduce Pro-Worlds, a visual puzzle benchmark in which agents move a narrow viewing window to gather evidence. They also decompose accuracy gains into shifts in evidence at commitment and changes in accuracy within evidence levels (§§4.1, 4.6).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy improved across the evaluation grid | Both variants beat action-only Base in all nine model–benchmark settings. | RLVB generally beat LLM-judged PPO | RLVB beat PPO with judged belief rewards in eight of nine settings by **9.3 accuracy points** on average. In the Qwen3.5-4B A100 comparison, the reported runs used **74.2 GPU-hours** for RLVB and **120.1** for judged PPO. | §§4.3, 4.5, Tables 1 and 4 |
| More episodes ended with adequate evidence | The share of episodes answered with sufficient evidence was **51%, 12%, and 74%** for RLVB versus **8%, 0.4%, and 36%** for Base on HiddenGames, Pro-Worlds, and RoboQuest, respectively. Results pool the three models and use the §4.6 task subsets. Sufficiency means a top-answer posterior of at least 0.95 where available, or completion of every evidence requirement on RoboQuest. | §4.6, Fig. 5 |
| Agents rarely committed while reporting uncertainty | Among turns with insufficient evidence and multiple answers in the belief line, RLVB committed on **0.1–2.2%** of turns versus **3.2–67.9%** for Base prompted to state a belief, across the model–benchmark settings. | §4.6, RQ3 |
| Direct belief supervision gave mixed behavioral effects | In the matched small-sample Qwen3-VL-4B-Thinking ablation, adding the belief term changed mean episode accuracy from **35.3% to 35.9%** on HiddenGames, **23.9% to 21.7%** on Pro-Worlds, and **55.5% to 45.8%** on RoboQuest. | §4.4, Table 3 |

The authors’ decomposition attributes the accuracy gain primarily to agents answering at higher evidence levels. Per-step evidence gains stayed broadly similar, and belief-line exactness varied by model, benchmark, and which states the agents visited (§4.6, Figs. 5 and 7, Tables 7–8). The lower commit rate when agents report uncertainty supports the authors’ interpretation. Whether the written line itself causally changes the action remains open.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Exact supervision requires a known prior and observation model, and lookahead gets more expensive as the action and observation spaces grow. A set-valued belief line cannot express relative confidence among remaining answers (§5).
- **Author-stated.** Additional steps help only when they bring evidence. RLVB showed no gain on MiniGrid and MineSweeper, and it sometimes exhausted its budget without answering. It also sometimes misread sufficient evidence and submitted the wrong answer named in its belief line (§4.6, §5, Table 30).
- **Analyst inference.** The main baseline comparisons vary reward, turn format, and how samples are grouped, which limits attribution to the posterior reward alone (§§3.4, 4.2–4.4).
- **Analyst inference.** RoboQuest demonstrates transfer to a simulated setting without an exact posterior at evaluation. Its requirement-based evidence measure, and its use of language-model readers on a selected subset for belief-line accuracy, limit direct comparison with posterior-based results (§4.6, Table 7).

## Open research questions

1. **How accurate does an estimated posterior need to be for training?** RoboQuest tests transfer without an exact posterior, while training uses exact supervision. Train with approximate posteriors of varying quality in a simulator that retains the exact posterior for evaluation, then compare early commits and final accuracy (§§4.1, 5–6).
2. **Can the agent learn to choose more informative steps and stop when further search is unlikely to help?** The reported policy gains little in per-step information and can stall. Compare alternative action rewards under matched step budgets on MiniGrid, MineSweeper, and held-out Pro-Worlds (§4.6, RQ2 and Limits).
3. **Does the written belief line itself influence commitment?** The evidence relates stated beliefs to actions, but both are generated from the same history. Hold the history fixed, experimentally vary the belief line before action generation, and measure how often the policy continues or commits (§4.6, RQ3).
