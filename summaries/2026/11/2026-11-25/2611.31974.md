# Coding4Games: Can Modern Coding Agents Play Modern Video Games?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.31974) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31974)


## One-sentence takeaway

On Coding4Games, the best frozen program selected separately for each game beat the stronger of two online GUI baselines on 13 of 15 tasks and tied on one, while same-model comparisons showed mixed results (Tables 3 and 5).

## Problem and motivation

The authors ask whether a general coding agent can learn enough from interacting with a modern game to write a standalone program that plays it. Modern games demand visual perception, precise control, and multi-step planning. The authors identify a gap between online GUI benchmarks, where a language model repeatedly chooses actions during play, and bot-development benchmarks that expose structured game state and abstract actions through game-specific APIs (§1, Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Development–test protocol.** A coding agent explores, writes, and self-tests candidates in a persistent sandbox under token and game-tic limits. Its chosen program is frozen and evaluated in fresh rollouts without language-model inference or public network access (§3.1, Figure 2).
2. **Standardized game suite.** A gym-like server wraps 15 games, one task per title, with screenshots, standard gameplay inputs, host-controlled scenario initialization, and task-specific scores that capture partial progress (§§3.1–3.2, Table 2).
3. **Development-process evaluation.** The authors evaluate 12 coding agents and introduce measures for how programs use game state, how substantially agents revise code, whether self-tests revisit targeted problems, and how fully those problems are resolved (§3.3, §4, Tables 3–4).

The results establish that frozen programs can score competitively on the designated tasks without language-model calls during testing. The headline comparison selects a different coding agent when advantageous, so the paired results are more informative about changing how a given model plays. The evaluation leaves transfer to new scenarios and the end-to-end cost trade-off unresolved.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ observations.** Self-tests often revisit a targeted failure without resolving it. Some programs rely on partial state representations or visual assumptions tied to observed conditions. The illustrated debugging interaction shares also come from uneven runs and budgets, limiting their interpretation as stable model preferences (§4.2, §5).
- **Analyst inference.** Fresh rollouts from the designated scenario measure repeatability there. With one task per title, two same-model GUI comparisons, and one human player, claims about broader transfer or human-level play remain weak. Averaging valid scores also leaves the impact of excluded episodes unclear without per-cell counts (§§3.1–3.2, §4).
- **Reporting ambiguity.** Section 4.1 lists Balatro and Slay the Spire among coding-agent wins over the human, while Table 3 shows ties on both, consistent with §1. Section 4.2 also points to Table 5 for process measures printed in Table 4.

## Open research questions

1. **Do frozen programs transfer to unseen scenarios in the same game?** The tests reuse a designated scenario (§§3.1–3.2). Freeze controllers developed on one scenario and evaluate them on held-out tracks, encounters, or starting states.
2. **Does targeted replay improve repair and final performance?** Relevant self-testing and successful repair diverged (§4.2, Table 4). Compare targeted replay of observed failures with ordinary self-testing under matched budgets, then measure resolution and independently tested game scores.
3. **When does developing a program pay off over repeated play?** The paper tests inference-free deployment and varies development budgets, but the supplied results give no end-to-end break-even point (§4.2, Figure 3). Compare frozen and GUI agents using the same models across repeated episodes and record development and deployment costs alongside game scores.

## Confidence and missing information

**Medium.** The protocol and headline comparisons are documented, but the human-comparison wording is inconsistent and several details needed to assess reliability or reproduce the benchmark are unavailable in the supplied text.

- Supplementary task objectives, exact scoring formulas, and recorded initialization actions.
- Episode-level scores, variability, valid episode counts, and criteria for excluding episodes.
- Numerical budget-sweep outcomes and end-to-end runtime or cost measurements.
- Process-rating procedures and agreement, and public availability of benchmark code, logs, and frozen programs.
