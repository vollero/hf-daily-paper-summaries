# LingSante MedGuard Technical Report

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.25509) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25509)


## One-sentence takeaway

LingSante MedGuard distills a medical risk teacher and six specialist experts into one guardrail, whose 27B model achieved the highest reported mean F1 of 87.4% across the 11 text and multimodal risk-detection benchmarks (Table 4).

## Problem and motivation

Medical AI responses can contain clinically harmful advice, exceed professional authority, or compromise patient rights. The authors argue that general content guardrails lack the patient context and specialized criteria needed to assess these risks, while medical guardrails aimed at individual tasks leave gaps across safety, compliance, and ethics (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Unified assessment.** The guardrail takes a candidate response, available context, and explicit assessment criteria, then returns a Safe/Unsafe label and a supporting reason. Material uncertainty that prevents a supported Safe judgment is classified as Unsafe (§§2.1–2.3).
- **Specialist supervision in one model.** A guardrail teacher supplies judgments and reasons. Six experts supervise rule application, numerical reasoning, multimodal hallucination detection, retrieval-based verification, risk localization, and multimodal entity extraction. Offline supervised fine-tuning uses teacher demonstrations, then task-routed on-policy distillation scores student-generated trajectories. The consolidated model runs without the teacher ensemble (§§2.4, 3.1–3.3).
- **Benchmark and attribution studies.** MedGuardBench separates 11 primary risk-detection subsets from eight representative capability evaluations. The report compares training strategies and includes a 4B leave-one-category-out study of expert training data (§3.4.4, §4.2, Tables 4–6).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Stage 2 led the full-suite comparison at 27B | Stage 2 Overall-Full F1 was 82.3%, 82.4%, and 87.4% at 4B, 9B, and 27B, versus 81.7%, 81.8%, and 84.7% for size-matched Stage 1 models. The metric equally weights all 11 benchmark F1 scores. At 27B, Stage 2 also led the eight-benchmark text aggregate with 86.5%. | §4.1.4, Table 4 |
| Teacher-aligned initialization performed better under a matched budget | In a separate 4B on-policy distillation study, Stage 1 initialization reached 82.3% Overall-Full and 79.2% Overall-Text F1, versus 80.2% and 76.4% from Mixed-SFT initialization. | §4.2.1, Figure 11 |
| Each specialist data category contributed in the 4B Stage 1 ablation | Removing one category at a time lowered estimated Overall-Full F1 from 81.7% to 80.0–81.2%, with the largest drop after removing hallucination-detection data. | §4.2.4, Table 6 |
| Capability gains varied by task and scale | Stage 2 improved five of six task-specific capability scores at each scale versus Stage 1. Entity extraction declined at 4B and 9B, and detection-aware localization declined at 27B. | §4.2.2, Table 5 |
| Safety review transferred to a derived reasoning benchmark | On matched chains with injected process errors that left the final answer unchanged, Safe/Unsafe macro-F1 was 90.28%, 92.11%, and 88.68% at 4B, 9B, and 27B, respectively, gaining 13.77, 17.16, and 5.80 percentage points over size-matched Qwen3.5 baselines. | §4.3, Table 7 |
| Physicians found high agreement with sampled labels | Reviewing 500 randomly sampled test examples per domain, licensed physicians found binary label accuracy of 98.6% for safety, 96.0% for compliance, and 99.2% for ethics. | §3.4.5 |

The comparisons support improved classification under the reported protocols, and the reasoning-chain results show gains even when the final answer provides no signal that an error was injected. Clinical outcomes and the reliability of generated explanations require separate evidence. The physician audit assessed benchmark labels, and the headline F1 scores measure agreement with those labels.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ caveats.** Compliance MedSafetyBench uses different subsets for trained models and historical baselines. The benchmark covers selected taxonomy subdomains, balances Safe/Unsafe labels by construction, and includes related prompt variants and reused images. A full overlap audit across evaluated models’ training corpora is unavailable (§§3.4.4–3.4.5, 4.1.2).
- **Authors’ caveats.** Transfer varied by source, and invalid outputs affected external-benchmark comparisons, especially for Qwen3.5-9B. The benchmark package excludes training corpora, model weights, and standalone entity-extraction assets (§3.4.5, §4.3, Figure 9).
- **Analyst inference.** Balanced, scoped evaluations leave the deployment false-alarm burden uncertain, especially when unresolved cases and confirmed violations share one label. The 4B Stage 1 ablations support category-level attribution in that setting. Their applicability to larger Stage 2 models remains unclear (§2.1, §3.4.4, Table 6).

## Open research questions

1. **How often would the guardrail flag routine cases for review?** Balanced benchmark labels leave operational false-alarm rates open. Run a blinded review of consecutive cases from a defined workflow and report confirmed violations, unresolved concerns, and false alarms separately.
2. **Which specialist signals drive Stage 2 gains across scales?** The visible leave-one-category-out study is limited to 4B Stage 1. Repeat it at fixed training budgets through Stage 2 across scales and measure both domain F1 and capability trade-offs.
3. **Do generated reasons reliably identify decisive evidence?** The headline F1 and physician label audit leave rationale fidelity open. Have clinicians independently score reasons on a stratified sample, then test whether reasons and decisions update when a decisive fact or rule changes.

## Confidence and missing information
