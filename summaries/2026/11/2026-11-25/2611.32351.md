# LookAnything: A Foundation Model for Holistic Gaze Following

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.32351) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32351)


## One-sentence takeaway

LookAnything uses learned geometric queries to unify gaze localization, description, and reasoning, leading the reported HoliGaze-Bench comparisons and achieving the lowest average point error on two unseen datasets (Tables 2 and 5).

## Problem and motivation

Gaze following infers where a person is looking, which can help interactive systems interpret attention and respond to social cues. The authors argue that specialist pipelines often depend on head crops or precomputed depth maps, creating opportunities for cascading errors, and that heatmap-only outputs offer limited information about the attended entity. They also identify fragmented benchmarks and a narrow task scope in prior gaze-oriented vision-language models (§1, Appendix A).

## Approach and contributions

- **LookAnything.** The model adapts Qwen3-VL-2B to answer gaze questions from an image and a prompt. Separate learnable query groups receive supervision for relative depth, source head grounding, and gaze-target geometry. Block-wise attention keeps the query groups from attending to one another, while answer tokens can attend to all groups. The language head generates text or coordinates, and auxiliary decoders can directly produce spatial outputs, including heatmaps (§3, Figure 4, Appendix B.3–B.4).
- **HoliGaze.** A data pipeline converts annotations from eight source datasets into templated questions and answers. It produces 303K training pairs and 8K human-verified benchmark pairs covering nine tasks across five capability dimensions. The benchmark balances open-ended and multiple-choice questions (§2, Figure 3, Table 8, Appendix D).

The tables support benchmark gains for the trained system under the stated protocols. The native results depend on explicit source-head cues, and the unseen-domain tests establish transfer for point localization only. The robot examples show that the model can run in a physical setting, while reliability across subjects, scenes, and motion remains unquantified.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated limitation.** The auxiliary depth maps are relatively coarse because of the decoder’s patch-level resolution (Appendix F.5).
- **Analyst inference.** Table 2 measures the combined benefit of gaze-specific training and architecture against zero-shot VLMs. The component ablations offer more direct evidence, but the supplied results give no run-to-run uncertainty for the smaller differences (Table 4).
- **Analyst inference.** Gaze depth and direction labels are filtered for depth contrast and agreement between model-estimated depth and head orientation. Human verification checks retained examples, while performance on the low-contrast or conflicting cases removed by the filter remains unknown (Appendix D.2–D.4).
- **Analyst inference.** Open-ended description scores measure reference overlap, and refusal tasks use standardized responses. These choices leave semantic correctness under paraphrase and refusal behavior on naturally worded queries uncertain (§4.1, Appendix D.3).

## Open research questions

1. **Do the geometric queries help when training data and compute are matched across backbones?** The reported query ablations use one backbone (§4.4). Train query-equipped and query-free variants of multiple backbones with matched data and optimization budgets, then report variation across runs.
2. **How well does gaze reasoning transfer to independently annotated scenes and prompts?** The unseen-domain experiments score point localization, while HoliGaze uses templated questions and filtered depth labels (Table 5, Appendix D). Collect human-authored questions with independently verified geometry and score spatial reasoning and refusal by difficulty.
3. **What accuracy does the faster auxiliary branch deliver during robot motion?** The paper profiles latency separately from its qualitative robot deployment (Table 12, Appendix E.2). Compare both output pathways on the same labeled stationary and moving robot footage, measuring localization error, temporal stability, and end-to-end latency.

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the architecture, dataset design, and tabulated comparisons as reported. The supplied text leaves public access to code, weights, and data unconfirmed and provides no run-to-run uncertainty or quantitative robot reliability results. It also leaves the exact source-reference protocol for the unseen-domain tests unclear.
