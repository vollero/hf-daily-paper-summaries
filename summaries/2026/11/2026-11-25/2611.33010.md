# Embodied AI Fails the Mirror Test: How a Simple Mirror Breaks Robot Policies

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.33010) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33010)


## One-sentence takeaway

Every tested mirror configuration lowered aggregate manipulation success for all five simulated policies and both Franka policies, and matched matte-panel controls showed that direct robot–panel collisions could explain only part of the degradation in the two simulated policies tested with those controls (Tables 1, 2, and 6).

## Problem and motivation

Mirrors duplicate task-relevant objects at apparent locations where a robot cannot manipulate them, while sometimes revealing objects hidden from direct view. The authors identify a gap between visual robustness benchmarks that vary conditions such as lighting or viewpoint, mirror studies focused on perception or self-recognition, and controlled tests of whether generalist policies can act on physical objects in reflective scenes (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **RoboDojo-Mirror.** The benchmark pairs layouts and policy seeds within each policy across mirror-free, single-mirror, dual-mirror, and mirrored-tabletop scenes. It preserves task instructions, success criteria, checkpoints, and policy-specific settings across conditions (§3, Appendix A).
- **Controls and behavioral diagnostics.** Geometry-matched matte panels probe the role of physical obstruction. Separate diagnostics measure empty grasps, pushing progress when a target adjoins its reflection, and physical-target interaction under main-camera occlusion (§§4.2–4.3, Appendices C–F).
- **Physical-robot validation.** The authors test two additional policies on four Franka tabletop tasks under mirror-free, upright-mirror, and mirrored-tabletop conditions (§4.4, Appendix G).

The paired results establish aggregate degradation in the tested settings, with some task-level exceptions (Tables 10–14). The authors interpret trajectories and contact persistence as consistent with phantom targeting and spatial mislocalization. Those observations leave each policy’s internal target representation unresolved. The Push-T collision control shows that physical access can also determine outcomes, and the occlusion setup leaves open whether successful actions used reflections or direct wrist views (§4.2, Appendices E–F).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope.** Execution budgets differ across policies, so aggregate drops do not support a controlled cross-policy ranking. Layout-bootstrap intervals quantify variation within the fixed task set, which favored tasks with strong RoboDojo leaderboard performance (§4.1, Appendices A.4 and B.1).
- **Analyst inference.** The controlled evidence beyond direct robot–panel collisions is limited to two policies and upright simulated panels. The mirrored tabletop and Franka trials lack corresponding matte controls, leaving the contribution of reflections less isolated in those settings (§3.2, §4.4, Appendix G).
- **Author-stated diagnostic ambiguities.** Push-T contour changes also change object pose and potentially physical accessibility. The patch changes local reflected content and salience as well as contour continuity. Direct wrist views and small prompt-condition samples limit what the occlusion experiment can establish about reflection use (§4.3.3, Appendices E–F).

## Open research questions

1. **Can a policy localize and manipulate an object using reflections alone?** Some targets remained directly visible to wrist cameras. Repeat the occlusion task with matched layouts and all direct target views blocked, then compare rollouts with the reflected view visible versus masked (§4.3.3, Appendix F).
2. **How much does contour continuity affect pushing when physical access is fixed?** The tested contours changed object pose, and the patch changed several visual cues at once. Hold object pose and collision geometry fixed while varying the rendered junction between the object and its reflection (Appendix E).
3. **Does reflection-aware training improve robustness across mirror placements?** The benchmark used fixed policies without mirror-specific adaptation. Compare matched policies trained with and without reflective scenes on held-out mirror placements and a mirror-free baseline (Appendix A.2).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the reported direction of aggregate degradation for the tested policies and scenes. The supplied text leaves unavailable the rollout-level records needed to independently audit the aggregates and example trajectories, uncertainty intervals for the real-world and targeted diagnostic outcomes, and confirmation of which benchmark assets are downloadable from the code repository listed on page 1.
