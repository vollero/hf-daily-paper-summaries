# Mega-MMDF and DeepfakeBench-MM: Benchmarking Multimodal Deepfake Detection Across Diverse Forgery Pipelines

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2510.22622) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.22622)


## One-sentence takeaway

Mega-MMDF and DeepfakeBench-MM provide a million-scale dataset and a unified evaluation protocol that reveal a substantial gap between audiovisual deepfake detection on Mega-MMDF and transfer to external datasets.

## Problem and motivation

Human-centric audiovisual deepfakes can enable fraud, misinformation, and reputational harm. The authors identify two barriers to improving detection: existing datasets often lack scale, forgery diversity, or audiovisual fidelity, and inconsistent preprocessing and evaluation protocols make detector results difficult to compare (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Mega-MMDF.** The authors construct 1.2 million audiovisual clips, including 100,000 real and 1.1 million forged samples. They combine 28 existing forgery methods into 21 forgery pipelines and apply quality checks for audio, video, and synchronization. Labels distinguish whether audio, video, both, or neither were manipulated (§§3.1–3.3, Appendix A.2).
- **DeepfakeBench-MM.** The modular benchmark standardizes preprocessing, training, and evaluation across five datasets and 11 detectors. It includes a two-stage contrastive baseline, an ensemble that averages separate audio and video predictions, implementations of existing detectors, and zero-shot evaluations of two multimodal language models (§4.1, Appendix B.3).
- **Diagnostic experiments.** The authors measure transfer across datasets and forgery pipelines, probe reliance on each modality, and test second-stage backbone fine-tuning and random video masking during training (§§4.2–4.3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| FRADE leads on Mega-MMDF | Held-out Mega-MMDF test AUC was 0.994 for FRADE, 0.987 for the ensemble, 0.970 for MRDF, and 0.969 for the vanilla baseline. The zero-shot Qwen2.5-Omni and VideoLLaMA2 scored 0.568 and 0.534 under their separately specified protocol. | §4.2, Table 2 |
| External transfer varies sharply by dataset | FRADE scored 0.868 AUC on FakeAVCeleb and 0.771 on IDForge, the highest listed values on those external test sets. AVH led on the two partial-forgery test sets with 0.870 on LAV-DF and 0.614 on AVDeepfake1M, versus FRADE’s 0.668 and 0.507, respectively. | §4.2, Table 2 |
| Cross-pipeline transfer is often asymmetric | The vanilla baseline was trained on each forgery pipeline plus real clips and tested across pipelines. The authors report strong transfer in both directions between entire-face synthesis plus reenactment and face editing plus reenactment, alongside asymmetry across many other groups. They interpret the heatmap and feature plots as evidence that some visual artifacts dominate in stacked forgeries. | §4.2, §4.3 Analysis 2, Figures 3–4 |
| Fine-tuning and masking show gains in the tested models | The authors report AUC improvements with second-stage backbone fine-tuning for AVTS, AVFF, and the vanilla baseline. Random video masking with audio-label supervision improved reported in-domain and transfer AUC for AVTS and MRDF and yielded more balanced single-stream results. | §4.3 Analyses 3–5, Tables 3–4 |
| Human judgments favor Mega-MMDF’s realism | In a 60-volunteer study, the authors report that Mega-MMDF had the lowest human detection accuracy and AUC in the judgment task and the highest realism ranking scores in both ranking tasks. | §3.4, Appendix A.3, Table 7 |

Table 2 establishes detector rankings under the specified protocols and shows that strong in-domain AUC can coexist with weak transfer. The pipeline and masking experiments support the authors’ interpretation that many tested detectors rely heavily on visual cues. Causal attribution to particular artifacts or fusion architectures remains open, as does performance on real-world deployments.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope.** The masking experiment covers AVTS and MRDF, and the authors leave its generalizability across datasets, architectures, and bias patterns open. The single-stream evaluation excludes detectors that require both modalities, including AVAD, MDS, and AVH (§4.3 Analyses 4–5).
- **Analyst inference — possible split dependence.** Appendix A.4 describes a demographically stratified split. Identity and source-clip separation are unspecified, so the independence of related real and forged clips across partitions cannot be assessed from the supplied text.
- **Analyst inference — comparison scope.** Several detectors were reimplemented from closed or partially released code, and AVH includes only its supervised variant. Rankings therefore apply to the benchmark implementations (§4.1, Appendix B.2). The quality comparison excludes LAV-DF and AVDeepfake1M because of their localization focus and uses ILLUSION’s manuscript-reported FAD, limiting the comparability of that result (§3.4).

## Open research questions

1. **How much does split design affect in-domain AUC?** Identity and source-clip separation are unspecified. Rebuild the splits by grouping identities and source clips with their derived forgeries, then rerun the same detectors.
2. **Does video masking improve transfer when the dominant modality changes?** The paper tests two detectors trained in a visually biased setting. Compare audio masking, video masking, and an unmasked control across detector families and forgery conditions, using both joint-input and single-stream AUC.
3. **What explains AVH’s relative advantage on partial forgeries?** The authors point to pretrained lip-reading features, while the reported results compare complete detectors. Hold architecture and training data fixed, vary lip-reading initialization, and measure detection AUC by manipulation duration and modality.

## Confidence and missing information

**Medium.** Table 2 and the main evaluation protocols are available in the supplied text. The numeric cells for Tables 1, 3, 4, and 7 and the underlying values in Figures 3 and 6 are absent, so several effect sizes and subgroup differences cannot be checked. The text also leaves quality-filter thresholds, identity and source-clip separation, and practical access details for Mega-MMDF and the benchmark code unspecified.
