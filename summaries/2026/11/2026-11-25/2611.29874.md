# Escriba-OCR: A 1B Vision-Language Model for Error-Preserving Transcription of Handwritten Portuguese Essays

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.29874) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29874)


## One-sentence takeaway

On 199 BRESSAY test pages, fine-tuned Escriba-OCR-1B reduced character error rate from 5.60% for the strongest zero-shot baseline to 2.06% and recovered substantially more edit markup, yet still replaced 60% of dictionary-flagged reference words with dictionary words (§7, §8.3).

## Problem and motivation

Writing feedback and learner-language research depend on seeing what students actually wrote, including misspellings, line breaks, deletions, and insertions. The authors identify a gap in measuring and preventing automatic correction in handwritten Brazilian Portuguese essays. They restrict the study to open-weight systems to support local processing of student work (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Benchmark and baseline study.** The paper adds spelling preservation, invented errors, edit-markup precision and recall, and line-count agreement to CER and WER, with paired page-level bootstrap intervals. It evaluates 21 zero-shot VLMs and one BRESSAY-trained line recognizer (§§4–5, §7).
- **Fine-tuned model.** Escriba adapts LightOnOCR-3-1B using page, paragraph, and line images from 647 training pages. It trains both the vision encoder and decoder against verbatim references with target-token cross-entropy, retaining the image-only interface (§6).
- **Analysis and released assets.** The study audits apparent corrections, tests interventions against normalization, and measures transfer to copied letters and the trade-offs of weight interpolation. The authors report releasing model weights, CPU builds, benchmark and training code, system outputs, review data, and a BRESSAY erratum (§8, Data and Code Availability).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Lower in-domain transcription error | On 199 BRESSAY test pages, capped, case-insensitive CER with markup was 2.06% versus 5.60% for Qwen3-VL 235B-A22B, the strongest zero-shot baseline. The difference was −3.54 percentage points (paired 95% CI −4.19 to −2.94). Text-only CER was 1.63% versus 4.61%. | §7, Tables 3–4 |
| Better edit recovery and line counts | On the same test split, Escriba recovered 38% of legible deletions, 77% of illegible deletions at 68% precision, and 67% of insertions. The best zero-shot insertion recall was 16%. Escriba matched the reference non-empty line count on 97% of pages. | §4, §7, Table 3, Fig. 1 |
| Spelling preservation improved over the base model, with substantial residual correction | Among 496 dictionary-flagged reference words, exact copying rose from about 13% for the base model to 24% for Escriba. Escriba replaced 60% with dictionary words. Its preservation lead over Qwen3-VL 235B was 2.2 points (95% CI −0.8 to +5.1). | §8.3, Fig. 3, Table 4 |
| Reference errors inflate apparent correction | A single teacher reviewed randomly sampled Escriba dictionary substitutions. Among 89 judgeable cases, 45% were reference errors (Wilson 95% CI 35–55%) and 43% were genuine student misspellings that Escriba normalized (33–53%). | §8.4, Fig. 3 |
| Published comparison favors Escriba under reconstructed scoring | On the BRESSAY test pages, Escriba scored 1.99–2.14% CER across reconstructions of the case-sensitive, uncapped competition metric with markup, compared with the best cited DAN-family ablation at 2.24%. | §7, Table 6 |
| Letter transfer exposed a vocabulary trade-off | On 40 BFL letters, CER was 5.1% for Escriba versus 4.9% for the base model after post-hoc removal of the base model’s appended comments. Words absent from BRESSAY training were misread at 39.6% versus 29.8%, respectively. In the matched-resolution interpolation experiment, unseen-word error fell from 39.6% to 34.6%, while BRESSAY illegible-deletion recall fell from 77.6% to 61.4%. | §8.7, Table 10 |
| Quantized CPU inference retained similar overall CER | On 30 randomly sampled BRESSAY test pages, mean capped page CER across llama.cpp variants was 2.54–2.60% versus 2.56% for unquantized vLLM. The 4-bit model and projector took a median 53 seconds per page on a 4-vCPU cloud VM with the prompt cache disabled. | §7, Table 7 |

The paired comparison supports a clear CER improvement on BRESSAY. The text-only results show that the advantage extends beyond markup. Markup matching ignores span position, and the line metric checks only the number of non-empty lines. The spelling audit estimates reference noise among Escriba’s apparent corrections, leaving the true preservation rate and cross-model ranking uncertain. The published-results comparison remains approximate because the official scoring script was unavailable.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Training covered one corpus. The transfer test used a small set of copies of one letter, with model-guided reference revision and too little independent markup annotation to measure recall. Photo robustness was tested on synthetic degradations. CPU latency came from a cloud VM (§8.6–8.7, Limitations).
- **Author-stated.** The dictionary proxy misses agreement errors and can flag names, foreign words, and transcription mistakes. A single non-blind reviewer audited Escriba’s apparent corrections. The page bootstrap excludes training variance, which was assessed separately over three final-model seeds (§4, §8.4, Limitations).
- **Author-stated.** Baseline input sizes and token limits varied, some providers used unknown quantization, and possible exposure to the public test images was unresolved. The study tested one general-VLM prompt and fine-tuned one base model. Early test runs also informed some zero-shot inference settings (§§3, 5, Limitations).
- **Analyst inference.** The encoder ablation supports training the vision encoder for this recipe. The independent value of OCR specialization remains uncertain because the study did not fine-tune a general-purpose VLM under matched conditions (§8.6, §9).

## Open research questions

1. **How often does each model genuinely normalize learner errors?** The audit covered only Escriba’s dictionary substitutions. Independently double-transcribe and adjudicate a stratified test subset, then compare models on verified spelling and agreement errors.
2. **Can multi-genre training improve unfamiliar-word recognition while retaining edit markup?** Interpolation helped vocabulary transfer but reduced deletion recall. Compare essay-only and mixed-genre fine-tuning at a matched training budget on independently annotated pages with deletions and insertions.
3. **Does photo augmentation help on real classroom captures?** The degraded validation images used the same transformation family as training. Evaluate matched scans and classroom phone photos of the same pages, comparing models trained with and without augmentation.

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported BRESSAY CER and markup findings under the stated protocol. Confidence is lower for true misspelling preservation, exact ranking against published systems, and transfer beyond BRESSAY. The supplied text lacks full per-system table rows and provider assignments, the official competition scoring implementation, an independently adjudicated spelling reference across systems, and measurements on real classroom photos or school hardware.
