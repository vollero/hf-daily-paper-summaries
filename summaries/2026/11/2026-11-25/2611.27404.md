# Degradation of Language Models under Controlled Load: Synchronization, Rule Execution and Misfires on the Background

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.27404) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27404)


## One-sentence takeaway

In a controlled word-replacement task across seven language models, word-level alignment separated loss of place from incorrect substitutions and unintended edits, revealing that higher replacement density worsened synchronization and increased misfire odds in every model while its effect on rule execution varied by model (§§3.4, 8.1–8.3).

## Problem and motivation

A model asked to copy a long text while applying many rules can skip or repeat passages, make the wrong substitution, or edit words that should stay unchanged. The author's earlier line-by-line score conflated loss of place with incorrect substitutions and ignored edits to background words. The paper identifies a gap in measuring all three outcomes on the same responses while varying the sources of load independently (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Word-level scoring.** The study reuses a synthetic task in which models copy random-word text and apply explicit substitutions. It varies text length, output position, replacement density, rule count, and rule position. A two-stage alignment uses exact anchors on canonicalized lines, then aligns words within matched lines. It scores synchronization over matched and unmatched positions, rule execution on matched rule words, and misfires on matched background words that receive a rule output (§§3.4, 4, 5).
- **Load-dependent statistical profiles.** Separate logistic mixed models cover the measured range, including settings where all responses at a control point lost structure. Request-level variance can vary with length, density, and rule count. The method falls back to constant variance if the fit fails or a directional check against observed request medians rejects it. The fitted coefficients are smoothed using outlier clipping, weights that account for excess dispersion, and QAIC curve selection (§§3.5, 5–7).
- **Comparable model profiles.** The author reports fitted curves for seven models, defines capacities at the 50% success threshold, and measures density effects as error-odds ratios. The paper reports releasing the scoring and fitting code, all seven profiles, and full measurement records for four models (§§6.6, 8, 10).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Alignment changed the interpretation of earlier failures. | On the earlier GPT-4o mini data, alignment agreed with positional scoring on every trial in the 38% of responses with intact line structure. Across that measurement, the paper attributes at most 16% of failures under the old score to incorrect substitutions at matched positions. | §§3.4, 4.6 |
| Synchronization and rule execution worsened later in responses. | Under the common synthetic task, all seven fitted profiles showed lower synchronization and lower conditional replacement accuracy later in the answer. Conditional replacement accuracy also fell for rules listed later. | §8.1, Figs. 3, 7, 11 |
| Density had different effects across outcomes. | Comparing full density with the rarest replacements, the fitted odds of synchronization failure increased by factors of 100 to \(5.7\times10^{12}\) across the seven models. Misfire odds increased for every model, while conditional wrong-replacement odds fell in both Gemini models and rose in several others. | §§8.2–8.3, Table 5 |
| Capacity rankings depended on the metric. | Fitted instruction capacity at 50% conditional replacement accuracy ranged from 10.0 rules for Nemotron 3.5 Lightning to 36.4 for GPT-4o mini. GPT-4.1 mini had the largest fitted rule-execution fatigue capacity at the same threshold, 169,628 output words, extrapolated beyond the measured region. | §8.3, Table 5 |
| Load-dependent variance changed fitted effects. | For GPT-4o mini, the fitted rule-execution logit fell by 3.4 across the measured rule-count range with load-dependent variance versus 2.1 with constant variance. The observed-median check retained load-dependent variance for rule execution and misfires, and selected constant variance for synchronization. | §§6.4–6.5, Fig. 2 |

The common protocol supports comparisons within this task, particularly where the three metrics move in different directions. The results leave open whether those patterns transfer to natural-text editing or reflect particular internal mechanisms. Extrapolated capacities are fitted threshold crossings, and the extreme density ratios express changes in odds.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Accidental word overlap can pass the alignment threshold, especially for merged lines. The algorithm only considers two-line merges or splits, and some reorderings can appear as substitutions. The choice to index insertions by reference position remains untested (§4.7).
- **Author-stated.** The 50% threshold is far below working quality, and several capacities extend beyond the observations. The adaptive procedure for selecting measurement points is private, and the Nemotron profiles cannot be recomputed from public point records (§§6.6, 10).
- **Analyst inference.** Verification against the previous scorer establishes agreement on structurally intact responses. The supplied text gives no independent ground truth for heavily damaged responses, where plausible alignment choices may change how an error is classified (§§4.6–4.7).
- **Analyst inference.** The misfire metric covers only substitutions that insert a rule output into a background position. The supplied text does not establish how much other unintended background changes affect the paper's aggregate correctness estimate (§§3.4, 5.1, Table 4).

## Open research questions

1. **How sensitive are failure profiles to alignment choices?** The paper flags doubtful matches but does not quantify their downstream effect. Inject known omissions, repetitions, swaps, and substitutions into reference outputs, then compare the current aligner with a probabilistic variant (§4.7).
2. **Does the arrangement of replacement targets matter at fixed density?** The profiles average over immediate word neighborhoods. Compare clustered and dispersed targets while holding text length, rule count, and overall density fixed (§9).
3. **Do the density effects transfer to natural text?** The reported evaluation uses random words. Repeat the same substitutions on natural passages and matched random-word controls, then compare each outcome separately (§§1, 8).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the reported method and the within-task comparisons in the cited tables. Missing information includes the adaptive point-selection algorithm, raw Nemotron measurement records, independent alignment labels for heavily damaged outputs, and transfer evidence on natural text (§§4.6–4.7, 8–10).
