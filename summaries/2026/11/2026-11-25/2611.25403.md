# Antom 3-in-1 Transformer: A Payment Foundation Model

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.25403) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25403)


## One-sentence takeaway

Antom’s model combines transaction histories, tabular features, and user graphs, and the authors report a 37.88% relative reduction in pooled payment loss rate versus XGB-Online in a randomized production evaluation with a shared 0.5% intervention budget (§4.2, Table 1).

## Problem and motivation

Payment platforms must detect fraud across businesses with different transaction patterns, changing fraud tactics, and uneven access to labeled examples. The authors argue that models centered on individual histories can miss risk signals in relationships among users and payment entities, while specialized models often handle behavioral, transactional, and relational signals separately (§1, §5.2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Behavioral pretraining.** Separate Transformers encode user, card, and device histories. Prefix-future contrast matches a history prefix to a statistical summary of later activity and uses candidates with similar coarse activity profiles to encourage finer distinctions (§3).
- **Relational pretraining and prediction.** A graph attention encoder learns from rolling user graphs by aligning edge-perturbed views and reconstructing relation counts. The encoder stays frozen during downstream adaptation, and its time-aligned embeddings add a residual to the risk prediction (§3, §4.5).
- **Task-conditioned fusion.** Field self-attention models interactions among transaction attributes and business features. Learned task queries aggregate sequence and tabular representations and route them through sparse experts for risk prediction and auxiliary tasks. The industrial model has approximately 6.92B parameters, with 1.74B active per task route (§3, Figure 1, §4.1).
- **Transfer and deployment evidence.** The report evaluates adaptation to a business excluded from pretraining, low-label fine-tuning, modality ablations, and real-time production performance (§§4.2–4.6).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Lower production losses | Randomized evaluation covering 30% of platform transactions from June 8 to October 14, 2026. Pooled amount-based chargeback and overall loss rates fell 37.74% and 37.88% relative to XGB-Online, and 28.26% and 24.07% relative to SAINT-style, respectively. The arms shared business policies and a 0.5% intervention budget. | §4.2, Table 1 |
| Better ranking and fewer chargebacks in the two-stage study | In shadow scoring, R@0.5% was 29.79% for Antom, 7.39% for XGB-Online, and 16.52% for SAINT-style. In the live stage at the 0.5% intervention budget, chargeback rate fell 28.28% relative to XGB-Online on pooled traffic and 26.09% under common date weighting. | §4.2, Table 2, Appendix B.2 |
| Stronger synthetic benchmark ranking | On IBM’s temporal test split, AP was 0.3829 versus PRAGMA’s 0.3412, and R@0.5% was 83.22% versus 72.12%. Antom led the five evaluated baseline configurations on all reported ranking metrics. | §4.2, Table 3 |
| Fewer labels needed in the evaluated businesses | With 25% of labels, pretrained versus full-label scratch AP and R@0.5% were 0.5246% and 8.33% versus 0.4205% and 7.81% on Merchant A, and 0.6928% and 10.16% versus 0.6370% and 8.11% on Merchant B. On AlipayHK, with 10% of labels, the pretrained model reached 0.8214% AP and 27.75% R@0.5%, versus 0.1148% and 10.41% for full-label scratch. | §4.4, Figure 3 for Merchants A and B |
| Transfer to an excluded business | With architecture, target labels, fine-tuning budget, and selection rule matched, pretraining improved an anonymized business metric by 34.5% relative to scratch and AUROC by 0.0518 absolute. The business-metric gain was 34.2% after removing accounts with the highest concentration of risk events. | §4.4, Appendix A.2 |
| Component gains | Prefix-future contrast led CoLES, BYB, CPC, and scratch on AP and R@0.5% under matched sequence-training settings. Joint graph-residual fusion averaged rank 1.75 by AUPRC across four public graph benchmarks. With fixed upstream outputs, expert fusion improved R@1% over UMI by 1.72 percentage points on Sampled Industrial and 1.11 points on TransXion. | §4.5, Tables 5–7 |
| All three modalities contributed in the offline ablation | On sampled industrial payments, full-model AP was 3.89%. AP was 1.06% without sequences, 2.94% without graphs, and 2.49% without tabular inputs. | §4.6, Table 9 |
| Production latency and routing | On an NVIDIA A10, monitored model-service P99 latency was 80.16 ms against a 120 ms service budget. The authors also report a 2.16% relative payment success-rate gain over an internal deployed routing model after post-training. | §4.3, Figure 2, §4.4 |

The randomized trial supports a production benefit for the evaluated traffic and policy. The matched scratch comparisons support a contribution from pretraining on the tested domains, and the ablation supports combining all three modalities on the sampled industrial dataset. Generalization to other operators or intervention policies remains untested in the supplied text. The monitored latency is for the model service, so end-to-end latency compliance cannot be verified from that figure alone.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Detailed architecture, training configurations, and dataset construction procedures are outside the scope of the report (§2).
- **Analyst inference.** The live trial comes from one operator, and the full-model public comparison uses synthetic card data. The public graph and fusion benchmarks broaden component testing while leaving external deployment performance open (§§4.1–4.5).
- **Analyst inference.** The date-weighted chargeback analysis addresses shifts in traffic mix, but the supplied text gives no online confidence intervals or outcome-maturity analysis beyond the reporting cutoff. The anonymized transfer metric and brief routing description also limit interpretation of their respective gains (§§4.2, 4.4, Appendices A.2 and B.2).
- **Analyst inference.** Merchant A and B differ in both history length and business composition. The source-mix experiment therefore leaves the cause of Merchant A’s transfer advantage unresolved (§4.4, Table 4).

## Open research questions

1. **Does the model retain its production advantage at another payment operator?** The live evaluation covers Ant International traffic. Run a randomized deployment at another operator with matched policies and budgets, and report loss rates after a fixed outcome-maturation period (§4.2, Appendix B.2).
2. **Does history length explain the cross-business transfer advantage?** The source cohorts differ in both history length and merchant composition. Repeat pretraining with matched history-length distributions and fixed data and compute budgets while varying the source mix (§4.4, Table 4).
3. **How sensitive is graph augmentation to embedding freshness?** The deployment looks up offline embeddings, and the ablation measures graph inclusion. Replay dated transactions with controlled embedding delays and compare fixed-budget recall and serving latency (§§4.3, 4.6).
