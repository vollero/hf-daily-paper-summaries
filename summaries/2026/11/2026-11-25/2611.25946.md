# Point Dynamics as Proxy for Robotic World Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.25946) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25946)


## One-sentence takeaway

Conditioning video world models on simulated 3D scene-point motion reduced tracked object-motion error relative to robot-render conditioning on held-out DROID clips with both tested renderers and on a separately fine-tuned bimanual setup (§§4.2, 4.5).

## Problem and motivation

Robotic world models predict what a camera will see after a sequence of robot actions. The authors argue that rendering the robot helps the video model follow those actions while leaving it to infer how objects slide, deform, or behave when released. These interaction errors matter if predicted videos are used to evaluate policies, plan actions, or generate training data (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Separate dynamics from rendering.** Back-project the initial calibrated RGB-D observation into scene points and use forward kinematics to turn joint and gripper actions into robot surface trajectories. The existing PointWorld model predicts scene-point displacements through an interface that allows the simulator to be swapped independently of the video renderer (§§3.1–3.2, 4.3).
- **Give the renderer a pixel-aligned motion proxy.** Combine a robot render, a dense warp of first-frame appearance, and sparse color-coded point tracks. Fine-tune the video renderer on proxies generated by the simulator so it sees simulation errors during training as well as inference (§§3.3–3.4).
- **Test what the renderer uses.** Compare numeric actions, robot renders, static scene depth, and simulated motion across Wan2.2 and Ctrl-World. With the renderer fixed, swap simulators, perturb their predictions, and edit object trajectories. A matched Wan experiment compares proxy conditioning with an auxiliary point-tracking loss (§§4.1–4.4).

## Evidence and main results

Paired DROID results below are ordered **Wan2.2, Ctrl-World**. Object-motion error is the mean pixel distance between generated and real tracks for selected moving scene points, scored when the real track is visible (§§4.1, 8).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Robot renders mainly improve action following | On held-out DROID, robot renders reduced robot tracking error by **62% and 58%** versus numeric actions. Object-motion error fell **13.0% and 9.0%**. | §4.2, Table 1 |
| The proxy improves object motion beyond robot renders and static depth | On eligible held-out DROID clips, the proxy reduced object-motion error by **8.6% and 6.2%** versus robot renders, and **5.7% and 4.6%** versus robot renders plus static depth. Against robot renders, reductions were **12.3% and 8.2%** on clips whose response was classified as non-rigid relative to the gripper. Overall DROID PSNR changed by **+0.06 and +0.08 dB** versus robot renders. | §§4.1–4.2, 8, Table 1 |
| The DROID gain survives checks across seeds and metrics | Pooled over **three training seeds**, object-motion error fell **7.7% and 6.7%** versus robot renders. With AllTracker on the original evaluation clips and query points, reductions were **11.5% and 7.1%**. SAM 2 mask metrics preserved the conditioning order. | §§9–10, Tables 7–8 |
| The renderer responds to simulated motion and edits | On DROID with the trained renderer fixed, object-motion error declined from static points through a grasp rule and the two learned simulator variants to estimated label flow. In the selected Wan freeze-edit subset, **61%** of scored points followed the edit versus **29%** for the robot-render baseline, which received no edit. Reversal and doubling had lower follow rates. | §§4.3, 11, Fig. 4 for the edit example |
| Proxy conditioning beats the tested auxiliary loss | On held-out DROID with matched Wan training data and budget, point forcing reduced object-motion error **3.4%** to **4.95 px**, and proxy conditioning reduced it **8.6%** to **4.69 px**, both relative to robot renders. The auxiliary head was removed at inference. | §4.4, Table 4 |
| The gain carries over after bimanual fine-tuning | On **265** eligible held-out clips from the single-camera bimanual setup, the fine-tuned Wan proxy reduced object-motion error **8.1%** versus robot renders and **4.3%** versus robot renders plus static depth. On **112** deformable-task clips, the corresponding reductions were **12.7%** and **7.6%**. FVD rose from **14.5 to 15.2** versus robot renders. | §4.5, Table 5 |

The within-renderer controls and fixed-renderer interventions support the authors’ claim that simulated scene motion improves short-horizon video fidelity and can steer depicted motion. The measurements establish agreement with recorded tracks and masks under the tested conditions. Physical validity, reliable control of unusual edits, and downstream benefits for planning or policy evaluation remain untested (§§4, 8–11).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Depth and tracking errors propagate through the pseudo-labels, simulator, and rendered video. Separate training makes the components replaceable and gives the renderer no mechanism to correct the simulator itself (§5).
- **Analyst inference.** The main metric favors visible, first-frame scene points with measurable motion. AllTracker and mask checks reduce concern about dependence on CoTracker3, which also helped produce the simulator’s training labels, but these checks still evaluate image-space agreement (§§4.1, 8–9).
- **Analyst inference.** The renderer comparison covers two backbones under a shared DROID protocol, and the bimanual result covers one camera setup with both components fine-tuned on local recordings. The evidence for transfer is therefore specific to supervised adaptation in that setup (§§4.1, 4.5, 13).
- **Analyst inference.** The matched point-forcing result applies to the tested Wan recipe. It establishes a training-budget comparison, while the proxy additionally requires simulation at inference (§§4.1, 4.4, 12).

## Open research questions

1. **Can joint training improve fidelity while preserving editability?** The paper trains the components separately and shows sensitivity to point noise. Compare joint and separate training on held-out clips using both unedited motion error and edit-follow rates under identical perturbations (§§4.3, 5).
2. **How well does the proxy transfer to unseen robot and camera setups?** The bimanual experiment fine-tunes both components. Evaluate on a held-out setup with the simulator and renderer frozen, then adapt each component separately to measure where local data helps (§4.5).
3. **Do lower video tracking errors improve action selection?** The paper motivates planning but evaluates video predictions. Rank candidate actions with each conditioning variant, execute them on the real robot, and compare predicted rankings with observed task outcomes (§§1, 4).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the direction of the reported short-horizon gains under the tested protocols. The supplied retrieval includes table captions and selected results in prose, but omits full table cells, uncertainty intervals for the headline gains, and confirmation that this paper’s code, fine-tuned checkpoints, or bimanual recordings were released.
