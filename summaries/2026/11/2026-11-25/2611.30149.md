# LeadVLA: Learning Follower-Aware Route Execution for Robot Leading

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.30149) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30149)


## One-sentence takeaway

LeadVLA keeps a prescribed route explicit while learning when to slow or wait and where to deviate locally for a language-designated follower, achieving the highest joint completion rate among evaluated robot policies on LeadBench at 39.6% (Abstract, §4.2, Table 2).

## Problem and motivation

A guide robot must bring a designated person to a destination along a prescribed route, even when that person lags, stops, or briefly disappears from view. The authors identify two gaps. Existing systems often connect follower perception and motion through separate states or planners, while direct trajectory prediction can distort route geometry when responding to a follower, particularly around turns. They also identify a lack of resources that combine scalable action supervision with standardized closed-loop evaluation for this task (§§1–2, Fig. 2).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Structured route execution.** LeadVLA uses front and rear RGB history, a language instruction, and local route coordinates to predict a scalar Pace and waypoint-level Residuals. A deterministic composer applies rate-limited Pace along the route’s arc length, then adds local position and heading corrections (§§3.1–3.2, Fig. 3).
2. **Follower semantics and referent training.** Six auxiliary objectives supervise the shared action representation. Only predicted lateral relation and formation feed directly into Residual. Training uses matched scenes with different language-designated followers and episodes that reassign the follower during execution (§§3.3–3.4, Appendix B.4).
3. **Training infrastructure and evaluation.** LeadInfra generates closed-loop simulation data using an expert that reacts to realized interaction evidence without access to hidden follower scripts. The authors instantiate approximately 4 million training samples and create LeadBench with 108 held-out episodes across disjoint environments, routes, actors, and scene instantiations (§3.5, Appendices B.5–B.6 and C.1).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Highest joint completion among evaluated robot policies | LeadVLA reaches 39.6% Lead Success Rate (LSR) on held-out closed-loop LeadBench, equally averaged across difficulty and person-count conditions. | Abstract, §4.2, Table 2, Appendix C.4 |
| Performance depends on interference and distractors | In single-person Easy → Constrained, LSR stays at 0.50 for LeadVLA, falls from 0.42 to 0.08 for Fiore-style, and falls from 0.33 to 0.08 for matched StarVLA. In Constrained multi-person, LeadVLA scores 0.04 versus 0.75 for the Human Expert reference. | §4.2, Table 1 |
| Explicit Path and Pace improve route fidelity and completion | Across LeadBench conditions, direct waypoints → Path + Pace raises LSR from 0.174 to 0.354 and Route Fidelity (RF) from 0.663 to 0.988. Adding Residual raises LSR to 0.396 and lowers RF. | §4.3, Table 2 |
| Selective fusion improves completion and formation compliance | With auxiliary supervision held fixed and Pace unfused, adding lateral and formation cues to Residual raises LSR from 0.329 to 0.396 and Social Contract Score (SCS) from 0.180 to 0.250 on LeadBench. | Appendix D.4, Table 22 |
| Referent co-training improves completion and shifts who triggers responses | Against training without referent interventions at matched scale and budget, combined co-training raises LeadBench LSR from 0.112 to 0.396. The target’s share of unambiguously attributable behavior changes rises from 60.0% to 86.7%. | §4.3, Table 4, Appendix D.3, Table 20 |
| Route fidelity transfers to physical trials | RF ranges from 0.80 to 0.95 across four simplified Core/Easy single- and multi-person settings, with 20 closed-loop trials per setting on a Unitree G1 and no real-world policy fine-tuning. | §4.4, Table 5, Appendix E.1 |

The matched simulation comparisons support the authors’ interpretation that explicit route structure improves execution and that Residual helps when local deviations are needed. The attribution diagnostic covers only behavior changes with an unambiguous trigger, so it leaves missed responses and ambiguous events unquantified. The physical trials demonstrate transfer under the tested conditions. Physical Success Rate uses a different protocol from LeadBench LSR, and the supplied text gives no physical baseline comparison (Appendix E.1).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported.** Strong interference combined with multiple people leaves a large gap to human performance. Physical failures include obstacle contact, weaker left/right formation control, and occasional responses to visually similar distractors. The evaluation emphasizes structured interference, and the policy adapts primarily through locomotion (§5, Appendices E.2–E.3 and F).
- **Analyst inference.** Held-out LeadBench conditions test generalization within the same interaction contract. They leave transfer to different distance and bearing conventions untested. Target and distractor appearances are also selected to be distinguishable in simulation, limiting what the attribution results establish for visually similar people (Appendices A.2, B.4 and C.1).
- **Analyst inference.** Physical evaluation on one platform, with simplified interference and manual response adjudication, limits conclusions about robustness across robots and harder settings. Without reported adjudicator agreement, measurement variability is unclear (Appendix E.1).

## Open research questions

1. **How does referent grounding degrade as people look more alike?** The physical evidence is qualitative, and simulation selects distinguishable appearances. A matched-rollout experiment could vary appearance similarity and report missed target responses and false distractor responses (Appendices B.4 and E.2).
2. **Does Residual improve recovery around moving obstacles while preserving follower response?** Physical interference was simplified, and obstacle contact remained a failure mode. A controlled comparison of Path + Pace and the full model could vary obstacle motion while measuring collisions, route recovery, and Target Response Score (Appendices E.3 and F).
3. **Can the policy follow a new distance and bearing contract?** Training and evaluation share one configuration, and lateral formation transfer was assessed qualitatively. An experiment could hold out distance and bearing ranges during training and separately measure physical distance and bearing error (Appendices A.2 and E.3).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the method description and the results explicitly reported in prose. The supplied text includes table captions but omits the table bodies, leaving the full per-condition baseline and ablation scores, physical success counts and interaction scores, and numerical distance and bearing ranges unavailable. Training-seed variability, statistical uncertainty, attribution-window counts, physical adjudicator agreement, and actual release availability are also unestablished.
