# LoopedWAM: Looped World Action Models for Elastic Inference

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.25595) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25595)


## One-sentence takeaway

LoopedWAM shares transformer blocks across depth to reduce policy memory, and its distilled variant lets one checkpoint trade action depth and sampling steps for latency while retaining 95.2–96.2% LIBERO success across nine settings measured on one H100 (Tables 4–5).

## Problem and motivation

Closed-loop robot policies need accurate actions under tight memory and latency budgets. World action models can cache visual features once per query, but they still rerun the action expert at every sampling step. The authors identify two gaps (§1): sharing weights reduces stored parameters while leaving computation at a fixed depth intact, and reducing inference steps or depth can hurt success when the policy has not trained for that budget.

## Approach and contributions

- **Weight-shared video and action experts.** Each expert stores a three-block prelude, a six-block shared core, and a three-block coda. Four core passes execute 30 layers using 12 stored blocks per expert. Both experts are initialized from Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B (§§3.2, 3.4, 4.1).
- **Independent recurrence with late alignment.** The video expert caches features once per query. The action expert runs at each sampling step, with its last core loop attending to the deepest video core features even when it executes fewer loops. Future-frame prediction is used during training (§§3.1, 3.3, Fig. 2).
- **Joint depth-and-step self-distillation.** LoopedWAM-E keeps video depth fixed and trains one checkpoint per benchmark across nine action budgets. A frozen teacher supplies fine-step targets starting from the student’s own intermediate states, plus an endpoint target from shared noise (§3.5, Algorithm 1, §4.1).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Compact policy remains competitive on selected benchmarks | With 0.58B policy parameters and no robot pretraining, LoopedWAM reached 96.1% LIBERO success versus 96.6% for re-evaluated Fast-WAM over 6,000 episodes per model. It reached 91.9% on RoboTwin 2.0 versus Fast-WAM’s published 91.8% across clean and randomized settings, and 52.2% on RoboCasa-GR1 versus DiT4DiT’s published 50.8%, both using 50 rollouts per task. | §4.1, Tables 1–3, E1 |
| Weight sharing saves memory at full depth | At ten sampling steps on one H100, batch one, LoopedWAM used 2.05 GB peak versus 15.25 GB for Fast-WAM, a 7.4× reduction. Same-donor Dense-30 used 3.79 GB and ran in 90.5 ms versus LoopedWAM’s 91.8 ms. | §4.3, Table 4 |
| Elastic inference reduces query latency | Across nine LIBERO settings, LoopedWAM-E achieved 95.2–96.2% success in 24.7–86.2 ms on one H100, batch one. At two action loops and two steps it matched two-step Fast-WAM at 96.0% while taking 27.6 versus 37.1 ms. At four action loops and two steps it stayed within 0.5 percentage points of its teacher on all three simulation benchmarks. | §§4.3–4.4, Tables 4–6 |
| Truncation needs budget-aware training in the tested setup | On the shorter LIBERO schedule at ten sampling steps, reducing a fixed four-loop action expert to one loop yielded 12.3% success. The elastic student achieved 93.7% at the same one-loop budget. | Fig. 3a, Table B2 |
| Physical trials show a speed–success trade-off | On four PiPER tasks, LoopedWAM and fine-tuned Fast-WAM each succeeded in 57/80 trials, with 3.7 versus 12.7 GB peak memory. On the RTX PRO 6000 robot computer, the two-loop, two-step student succeeded in 54/80 trials at 46 ms versus the teacher’s 57/80 at 160 ms. | §4.6, Table 9 |

The matched-depth dense control supports the distinction between parameter savings and latency savings. The headline 3.9× speedup compares against Fast-WAM at its native ten steps. Matching both methods at two steps gives a 1.3× speedup at the same measured LIBERO success (Table 4). The preferred budget depends on the benchmark. For example, one action loop at two steps scored 43.2% on RoboCasa-GR1 versus the teacher’s 52.2% (Table 6).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ caveats.** Repeated latency measurements varied by up to 15%, and sequentially timed elastic settings retained GPU memory from earlier runs, complicating direct peak-memory comparisons with the teacher (§4.3). Fixed-depth models and the distilled student received different amounts of training (§4.4). The partition ablation also changed which donor blocks were copied (§4.5).
- **Authors’ observed failure modes.** The largest LIBERO gaps versus the re-evaluated Fast-WAM and Faster-WAM baselines were on the Long suite. On the PiPER, 19 of LoopedWAM’s 23 failures occurred during final contact, and the policy often retried missed grasps from nearly the same pose (§§4.2, 4.6, Fig. 6).
- **Analyst inference.** The physical study covers one arm and four tasks, so the observed latency–success trade-off has limited evidence across embodiments. RoboTwin and several RoboCasa comparisons use published baseline results, so small score differences carry less weight than the matched LIBERO comparisons (§4.1, Tables 1–3, 9).

## Open research questions

1. **Can visual depth also be reduced dynamically?** The elastic student fixes video depth, and the paper varies video depth in separately trained models. Train a student across joint video-depth, action-depth, and sampling-step budgets, then compare success at matched latency on LIBERO-Long and RoboCasa-GR1 (§§3.5, 4.5).
2. **How much does multi-budget distillation contribute at equal training cost?** The fixed-depth controls did not receive the student’s additional training. Compare the elastic student with fixed-budget specialists given matched total training compute and demonstration exposure, then evaluate at the same action budgets (§4.4).
3. **Can the saved inference time improve recovery from failed contact?** The physical trials document repeated failed grasps but do not test recovery strategies. Compare a grasp-aware policy that increases the action budget after a detected miss with fixed-budget policies under the same trial timeout (§4.6, Fig. 6).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the reported method and measured results within the stated protocols. The supplied text omits the full training recipes and hyperparameters, isolated per-setting peak-memory measurements for LoopedWAM-E, and uncertainty estimates for the real-robot success differences. The paper lists code and model links, but the supplied text cannot establish what those links currently provide (§4.1, §4.3, Table 9, p. 1).
