# EEGJev: A System One Decision Model and Benchmark for Multi-Task EEG Question Answering

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.32100) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32100)


## One-sentence takeaway

With EEG and answer choices held fixed across 13 test sources, EEGJev selected a label for the requested familiar attribute 99.70% of the time, versus 49.20% under uniform choice, while achieving 62.04% balanced accuracy on the specific answer (§1.1, Fig. 3a).

## Problem and motivation

One EEG window can carry several annotations, such as the stimulus shown and the response a participant was instructed to make. Task-specific heads fix the decoding target in advance, while question-answering tests that change the options with the question can reveal the target through the choices alone. The authors identify a need to measure three behaviors separately: following the requested attribute, decoding its value, and refusing when the correct option is absent (Introduction, Fig. 2).

## Approach and contributions

- **EEGJev** uses a CBraMod EEG encoder fine-tuned across sources and then frozen, alongside a frozen MPNet text encoder. A two-layer bidirectional transformer jointly scores the offered candidates in one pass, adapting Laya’s candidate-marker design to EEG. A separately trained Act head handles refusal, a further-trained branch handles ordered categories, and the shared scorer uses window or block embeddings for temporal questions (§3.2, Fig. 1).
- **EEGJev-Bench v1.3** reconstructs windows from 19 public EEG sources, 17 of which have scored tasks, with participant-disjoint splits in scored sources. Its six question types cover single and paired attributes, ordered categories, refusal, two-window comparison, and event-half localization. Its distinguishing controls keep EEG and options fixed when switching attributes, and pair refusal questions with equal-size answerable versions (§3.1, Fig. 2, Supplementary Note 10).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Switching the question changes the selected attribute | On 13 test sources with familiar attribute pairs, fixed EEG, and identical ordered options, attribute match was **99.70%** versus **49.20%** for uniform choice. Specific-answer balanced accuracy was **62.04%**, and the source-balanced both-correct score was **44.37%**. | §1.1, Fig. 3a, Supp. Note 10 |
| Text controls distinguish question following from answer selection | On D1 test tasks, substituting a question about the wrong attribute reduced balanced accuracy by **12.6 percentage points** (95% interval −14.1 to −11.1). Letter-coded candidates yielded **43.5%** with or without a mapping, near the no-matching-EEG controls. | §1.1, Fig. 3b, Supp. Table 4 |
| Task-specific classifiers remain competitive | Across 109 D1 test tasks, source-balanced balanced accuracy was **63.8%** for EEGJev and **64.5%** for EEGNet. The participant-bootstrap difference was **−0.69 points** (95% interval −1.77 to +0.26). In standard D2, test joint accuracy balanced over observed answer combinations was **39.3%** for EEGJev and **44.2%** for EEGNet. | §1.2, Table 1, Fig. 5a |
| Held-out wording lowers accuracy on familiar tasks | Across D1 test tasks, shared-encoder EEGJev’s source-balanced balanced accuracy fell from **63.8%** with familiar wording to **61.7%** with two held-out phrasings. | §1.2, Supp. Note 8 |

| Supervised refusal improves on thresholding | On D4 test tasks, Act achieved **59.2%** group-balanced refusal accuracy versus **50%** for a rule constant within each group. On matched tasks, Act beat EEGJev’s confidence threshold by **4.8 points** (95% interval +4.2 to +5.4). With complete known label sets, full-label checking scored **65.4%** for EEGJev and **66.7%** for EEGNet on the same 31 test tasks. | §1.3, Fig. 5e–f, Supp. Note 11 |
| Direct temporal scoring helps on T2 | On T1 test tasks, direct EEGJev scored **77.7%** accuracy versus **78.1%** from composing its own D1 outputs. On 15 matched T2 test tasks from seven sources, direct EEGJev scored **68.31%** versus **58.62%** for each ordered readout trained on the same T2 labels. The **9.69-point** lead over EEGNet had a 95% interval of **+7.43 to +12.22** with participant resampling and **−1.55 to +24.16** when sources were also resampled. | §1.5, Fig. 6 |
| Transfer varies by task and dataset | On held-out validation tasks, balanced accuracy was **78.9%** for eye state versus **48.6%** with shuffled EEG, and **51.7%** for face gender versus **49.7%** with shuffled EEG. | Lower tail latency on matched individual calls | On 192 binary D1 validation questions from Exo and LEMON, answered individually with 576 timed calls per model per platform, P95 time to return an offered option was **394.97 vs 3,677.34 ms** on one CPU core and **9.60 vs 24.99 ms** on one GPU for EEGJev and NeuroLM-B, respectively. Timing started with preprocessed EEG. | §1.9, Supp. Note 6 |

The fixed-option control isolates the effect of changing the question for familiar attribute pairs. The gap between attribute match and answer accuracy shows that choosing the right category of labels is easier than decoding its value. The transfer experiments provide limited evidence for reuse of a familiar concept across sources, while generalization to new concepts or datasets remains unestablished. The latency comparison applies to matched, individual binary queries from preprocessed input, and the T2 advantage is clearest within the evaluated sources.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ limitations.** Most UniEQA-derived sources have one to three test participants, and task-level bootstrap intervals condition on those participants. T2 targets instruction, cue, or scoring times. The held-out task study covers two attributes, and the unseen motor-imagery evaluation requires electrode interpolation and starts from sparse motor-imagery training data (§2, §§1.7–1.8, §3.3.2).
- **Resource and access limits.** The decision-head training stage includes 806.8 million trainable parameters, mostly in source- and window-specific spatial layers. Benchmark questions, labels, and window indices remain private, as do the code and weights in the reviewer package (§3.2, Supp. Note 6, Data availability, Code availability).
- **Analyst inference.** Differences against METIS and LEAF combine model design with benchmark-specific training differences because both baselines use released weights. The spatial layers dominate parameter count, and the supplied comparisons do not isolate whether that capacity is needed for question following or temporal accuracy (§§2, 3.2–3.3).

## Open research questions

1. **Which concepts transfer across sources?** The two held-out validation tasks differ in whether the concept was seen elsewhere and in the attribute being decoded. Hold out multiple source–attribute combinations, stratify by concept overlap, and compare with shuffled-EEG and fully trained controls (§1.7).
2. **Can the model decode answers when candidate wording changes?** Letter codes sharply reduced accuracy despite some training exposure. Compare candidate paraphrases, arbitrary identifiers with explicit mappings, and original labels under matched augmentation and fixed EEG (§1.1, Supp. Note 10).
3. **Does the T2 advantage generalize across sources and event types?** The interval widened to include zero when sources were resampled, and the target timestamps come from cues or scoring. Repeat the matched-supervision comparison on held-out sources and, where available, include independently annotated physiological onset times (§§1.5, 2).
4. **How much source-specific capacity is necessary?** The paper reports where parameters are concentrated but gives no capacity-controlled spatial-head comparison. Train smaller or shared spatial layers with the same encoders and splits, then compare fixed-option answer accuracy, T2 accuracy, and latency (§3.2, Supp. Note 6).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the digest’s account of the reported benchmark results and their settings. The supplied text omits the numerical bodies of several supplementary tables, including the full per-seed held-out-task breakdown. Benchmark manifests, labels, and window indices are private, and the implementation and weights are described as available in a private reviewer package. Independent reproduction and broader transfer therefore remain unverified.
