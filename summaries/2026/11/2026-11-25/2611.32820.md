# Residual Guidance for Real-Time Interactive Video Generation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.32820) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32820)


## One-sentence takeaway

Residual Guidance adds a cache-free correction to the early denoising steps of streaming video generation, improving prompt-switch achievement across the tested models while introducing some scene drift and additional inference cost.

## Problem and motivation

Streaming video generators let users redirect a video through text as it unfolds, but the next chunks often continue depicting the old prompt. The authors call this **context inertia**. Their analysis finds that cached visual content suppresses the new prompt even after LongLive rebuilds its cache under that prompt, and even when only the preceding chunk is visible. The suppression is strongest at the first denoising step. Removing the cache improves responsiveness but can disrupt subject identity and scene continuity (§3, Fig. 2, Supp. A.3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Diagnosis.** The authors hold the noisy input fixed and vary which cached chunks the model can see, measuring how much the prompt switch moves the predicted clean chunk at each denoising step (§3.2, Supp. A.3).
- **Training-free correction.** After a switch, Residual Guidance (RG) re-noises the preceding clean chunk, reconstructs it in one pass under the active prompt with an empty KV cache, and subtracts the original chunk. It adds that residual to the next chunk’s regular cache-conditioned clean predictions at the first two of four steps, with weights 1 and 0.5. It recomputes the residual for each chunk and reuses one noise sample per prompt interval. This costs one extra forward pass per guided chunk and works alongside the model’s native cache strategy (§4.2, Supp. B.2).
- **Evaluation across switching regimes.** The authors apply the same main configuration to seven streaming baselines, including models with dedicated switching mechanisms, and test single-attribute changes, multi-event stories, and longer scripted interactions (§5.1, Tables 1–2).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Cached context suppresses the first-step prompt response | On 100 diagnostic prompt pairs, the full cache retained 0.18 and 0.27 of the cache-free response for Self Forcing and recached LongLive, respectively. Keeping only the previous chunk raised those fractions to 0.28 and 0.42. | §3.2, Fig. 2b, Supp. A.3 |
| RG improves single-attribute switching | On 200 T2V-CompBench prompts adapted to 5-second videos, reported average Achievement rose from 21% to 58% across seven baselines. CLIP and the official dynamic-attribute score improved on every baseline, and VBench Quality stayed within 0.01 of each baseline. | §§1, 5.1–5.2, Table 1 |
| Gains persist over repeated switches | Across five baselines, reported mean Achievement rose from 47% to 66% on 100 three-event, 10-second StoryEval videos and from 66% to 81% on 100 six-prompt, 60-second InteractiveBench scripts. InteractiveBench CLIP improved in every post-switch segment for all five models. | §§1, 5.1–5.2, Table 2 |
| RG trades some continuity for responsiveness | On 100 dynamic-attribute pairs, removing the cache at all denoising steps achieved more switches than RG on Self Forcing and LongLive, but object-masked DreamSim distance was 0.191 and 0.163, versus 0.089 and 0.046 with RG. | Table 3, Supp. F.3 |
| Raters favored RG overall | In a paired study with 20 raters viewing sampled frames from dynamic-attribute videos, RG scored 70.7% for overall preference (95% bootstrap CI 64.7–76.9) and 44.3% for consistency (38.9–49.6). Ties received half credit. | Fig. 3, Supp. C.4 |
| The throughput cost depends on the measurement window | At batch size 1, end-to-end throughput fell by about 6% for 5-second, single-switch videos on the RTX A6000 and H100. On H100 post-switch chunks alone, RG ran at 21.7 FPS for Self Forcing and 18.2 FPS for LongLive, down 13% and 16% from their baselines. | Supp. D.5, Table 8 |

The same-seed comparisons support a consistent gain in responsiveness for the tested models and scripts. Achievement measures VLM-judged success only on targets that the base model can render as an initial prompt. Agreement with CLIP, the official dynamic-attribute metric, and the frame-based user study strengthens the single-switch finding. Similar aggregate Quality scores can coexist with scene drift, as the masked-distance and consistency results show. The timing results establish throughput for the reported hardware and protocols.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported failures.** RG can miss object removal, global lighting changes, and fine-grained pose changes. It can also alter unspecified details, such as adding a handle to a glass. The authors attribute these failures to the trade-off between preserving the previous chunk during reconstruction and making a strong enough correction (§6, Supp. F.5, Fig. 21).
- **Analyst inference — transfer is uncertain.** The seven main baselines share a Wan2.1-1.3B teacher, and the quantitative strength and schedule ablations cover two models on dynamic attributes. The explicit scene-cut examples use a higher noise level than the main configuration. The evidence for one configuration is strongest within the main benchmark settings (§5.1, Table 9, Supp. F.4).
- **Analyst inference — evaluation covers a subset of live use.** Main switches are scheduled, and the human study uses sampled frames. Several temporal quality dimensions exclude the switch chunk, though separate scene-preservation tests examine it. The 240-second extension uses LongLive alone and reports CLIP and Quality rather than switch Achievement (Supp. C.2, C.4, D.2, E.3).

## Open research questions

1. **Can spatially selective guidance improve object removal while preserving unrelated content?** The reported failures show both missed removals and incidental edits. Compare a region-restricted residual with default RG on targeted removals and pose changes, measuring Achievement and object-masked drift (Supp. F.5).
2. **Does the early-step correction transfer to independently trained video backbones?** The main baselines share a teacher. Repeat the cache-visibility diagnostic and compare fixed versus adjusted RG schedules across backbones trained from different teachers and using different denoising schedules (§5.1, Table 9).
3. **How does RG respond when users switch again before the previous change settles?** The main evaluations use scheduled prompts. Run a live study with irregular, closely spaced switches and measure time to visible change, missed switches, and continuity from full clips (§5.1, Supp. C.4, D.5).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** confidence in the reported direction of the benchmark gains and the measured trade-off between responsiveness and continuity. The supplied text does not establish RG code availability, multi-seed variability or uncertainty intervals for the main Achievement comparisons, participant demographics, or full-session latency under unscripted user input.
