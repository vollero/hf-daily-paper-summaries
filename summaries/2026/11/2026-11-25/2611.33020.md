# GRAPE-Bench: When Vision Degrades, Does Video Reasoning Hold?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.33020) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33020)


## One-sentence takeaway

GRAPE-Bench shows that video QA rankings can reverse between simulated and natural driving conditions, that overall accuracy can hide weaker basic judgments, and that synthetic fine-tuning can improve accuracy on naturally degraded footage for the tested backbone (§§3.4–4.2).

## Problem and motivation

Visual degradation can obscure cues needed for video reasoning, including traffic signals and road-user motion. The authors identify two gaps: controlled corruptions may give a misleading picture of relative performance on naturally degraded footage, and evaluations centered on hard questions may underrepresent simpler judgments that matter for driving decisions (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Benchmark.** GRAPE-Bench pairs clean videos with synthetically corrupted versions that retain identical questions and answers. It also includes naturally degraded driving footage with human-finalized questions. Both tracks use three independently answered multiple-choice tiers, from basic scene judgments to questions that integrate more evidence (§§3.1–3.3, Fig. 4).
- **Evaluation.** The authors compare open-weight and proprietary configurations by condition and tier, report paired clean-to-corrupted accuracy losses, and use auxiliary model ratings to examine how question difficulty relates to safety relevance (§§2, 3.4, Apps. C.2–C.5).
- **Adaptation and steering.** The paper compares answer-only and reasoning-based supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with on-policy self-distillation (OPSD), in which a teacher with clean-video access scores responses generated by the student from corrupted video. It also learns two activation vectors per domain to adjust the balance between L1 and L3 accuracy in a frozen model (§§4–5, Apps. D.2–D.4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Synthetic rankings can reverse on natural footage | Averaging L1–L3 within each of four driving conditions, Gemma 4-31B leads Cosmos-Reason2-32B by 3.4–7.0 percentage points on synthetic footage and trails it by 5.2–7.4 points on the corresponding natural subsets. | §3.4, Tables 2–3 |
| Overall accuracy can hide weaker basic judgments | On natural driving footage, Kimi K3 scores 85.4% overall versus 83.2% for Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, while their L1 accuracies are 86.7% and 89.5%, respectively. | §3.4, Table 3 |
| Corruption losses persist across model sizes | All 23 tested open-weight configurations lose 7.8–11.4 percentage points in pooled synthetic QA accuracy against their paired clean inputs. | §3.4, Table 2 |
| Basic questions receive higher mean safety-relevance ratings | Across 1,716 real-world questions, Claude Opus 5.5 assigns mean scores of 73.0, 64.9, and 51.5 out of 100 for L1–L3. GPT-6 Sol reproduces the descending trend using the same questions, options, and rubric. | §2, Fig. 2, App. C.4, Fig. 8 |
| Synthetic training improves natural-footage accuracy for Qwen3.5-9B | Answer-only SFT raises overall real-world accuracy from 76.7% to 81.1%. Under the separate reasoning-prompt protocol, reasoning SFT scores 78.1% and OPSD with a clean-video and reference-answer teacher scores 80.4%. | §4.2, Table 3, Apps. D.2–D.3 |
| Better synthetic validation accuracy can coincide with worse transfer | For Qwen3.5-9B SFT at a learning rate of \(3\times10^{-4}\), increasing training from 0.5 to 2 epochs raises synthetic validation accuracy from 85.7% to 87.4% while real-world accuracy falls from 77.8% to 75.0%. | §4.2, Fig. 5b |
| Steering shifts accuracy across tiers | Moving from L3 to L1 preference on held-out natural driving videos raises Qwen3.5-9B’s L1 accuracy from 79.7% to 82.4% and lowers L3 from 73.4% to 72.2%. Pooled accuracy spans 76.7–77.8% across steering coefficients. Separately trained synthetic vectors show the same endpoint trade-off. | §5.2, Fig. 6, App. D.4 |

The paired synthetic evaluation isolates the effect of changing the video pixels for fixed questions. The synthetic-to-natural ranking comparisons also change source scenes and questions, leaving the cause of the reversals unresolved. The adaptation and steering experiments establish changes in benchmark QA for Qwen3.5-9B. Driving outcomes, cross-backbone transfer, and cross-domain reuse of a single steering-vector pair remain untested in the supplied text.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated.** Each synthetic corruption uses a single intensity, and different corruption types apply to different source content. The observed ordering therefore applies to the tested subsets and presets. The authors also caution that lower clean accuracy at L3 may leave less room for an absolute decline (Apps. A.2, C.2, F.1).
- **Author-stated.** The evaluation focuses on answer accuracy. End-to-end latency, peak memory, and actual driving outcomes remain unmeasured, and uneven coverage of regions, conditions, and road users may hide subgroup failures (App. F).
- **Analyst inference.** Safety-relevance scores have agreement between two text-only model raters, but their absolute ratings differ and the supplied text gives no human or outcome-based validation. Baseline rankings also reflect configuration choices such as frame sampling, input format, and reasoning settings (Apps. C.1, C.4).
- **Analyst inference.** The OPSD comparison changes both the training objective and the amount of training. The supplied text gives no matched additional-epoch reasoning-SFT control, and each reasoning variant is reported from one run, limiting attribution and estimates of run-to-run variation (Apps. D.2–D.3).

## Open research questions

1. **Do ranking reversals persist after matching scenes and questions?** The natural and synthetic subsets differ in both respects. Evaluate standardized questions on matched routes or events, with controlled corruptions and naturally degraded captures (§3.4).
2. **How does corruption severity affect each tier?** A single preset per corruption leaves the accuracy-versus-severity curves unknown. Apply several intensities to the same clips and questions and track paired losses by tier (Apps. A.2, F.1).
3. **How much of the OPSD gain comes from clean-video access?** The reported teacher comparison varies access to the reference answer while both teachers see clean video. Cross clean versus corrupted teacher inputs with answer access on versus off, and include a matched continued-SFT control across multiple seeds (Apps. D.2–D.3).
4. **Does steering improve judgments that driving experts consider consequential?** Steering targets tiers, while safety relevance is rated from text and options. Have experts review the footage and rate decision consequences, then measure steering effects on high-relevance items within each tier (§5, Apps. C.4, F.2).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the benchmark design and the principal results explicitly reported in the text. The supplied rendering includes captions and selected values for Tables 2, 3, and 5–7 but omits their full entries, limiting independent checks of rankings and model-specific settings. It also leaves the real-world steering test size and exact split composition unspecified and provides no run-to-run uncertainty for the reasoning-SFT and OPSD comparison.
