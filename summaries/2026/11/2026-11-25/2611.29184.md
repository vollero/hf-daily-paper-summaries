# PhyCoVE: Benchmarking Physical Counterfactual Video Editing across Diverse Material Dynamics

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.29184) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29184)


## One-sentence takeaway

PhyCoVE benchmarks video edits across rigid, elastic, and fluid interactions, and the best of seven general-purpose editors reproduced only about one seventh of the measured counterfactual change (§4.2, Table 2).

## Problem and motivation

Given a source video and a text instruction specifying a physical intervention and its execution frame, an editor must preserve the preceding video and generate the resulting motion, deformation, or flow, including effects on other objects. The authors identify a gap in paired-reference evaluation: VOID focuses on removals, and PCVE-RigidBench primarily covers rigid-body motion. A real recording captures one outcome, and separate takes are hard to match closely enough to isolate the intervention. Simulation permits controlled changes but requires credible coupled dynamics and rendering (§§1–2, Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Paired benchmark.** PhyCoVE provides 533 cases across 110 source groups and five material interaction patterns, with removals, insertions, and physical-quantity changes. For the 508 simulated cases, a language model proposes scenes, coding agents implement them with material-specific solvers, and humans check each stage. The counterfactual branches from the source state at the execution frame and uses the same rendering setup. The 25 real cases use aligned, exposure-matched takes from a fixed camera (§§3.1–3.4, Fig. 3).
- **Counterfactual fidelity (CF).** The metric uses human-verified tracks to measure how much of the source-to-target change the generated video reproduces in selected quantities such as displacement, deformation, liquid extent, and object presence. It penalizes departures before the execution frame (§4.1, Eq. 1, Appendix A).
- **Comparative evaluation.** The authors evaluate seven general-purpose editors, add specialist comparisons on supported subsets, and use a blind pairwise study to test agreement with human preferences (§§4.1–4.3, Appendix C).

The results show that outputs can look physically plausible while missing the requested change in dynamics. CF’s agreement with human preference is evidence about **model-level rankings** for this panel. Per-video agreement and transfer to other model families remain unestablished. Wan 3.0 led rigid–rigid cases on CF and led none of the patterns involving elastic objects or fluids, so a rigid-only comparison would give a different picture (§4.3, Table 3).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-reported scope.** The authors flag the difficulty of isolating an intervention across separate real takes. The real set has 25 cases, and the explicit-simulation comparison covers only supported classic rigid-body motions (§1, §3.4, §5, Appendix E.3).
- **Analyst inference on measurement.** CF is conditional on visible, measurable changes. The protocol excludes quantities below its thresholds and can omit visible but untrackable objects or outputs with no remaining scored object. If measurability varies by model or material, aggregate scores may reflect which outputs can be scored as well as editing quality (Appendix A.3–A.4).
- **Analyst inference on comparisons.** The general-purpose evaluation uses one output per model and case, model-specific input formats, and reduced case coverage for Seedance 2.5. The specialist also receives perception aids. The reported comparisons leave run-to-run variability and the contribution of those aids unresolved (Appendix E.1–E.3).

## Open research questions

1. **Does explicit simulation improve edits involving elastic objects and fluids?** The specialist comparison covers classic rigid-body motion only. Test a simulation-guided editor on held-out mixed-material scenes with matched perception inputs and report CF by interaction pattern.
2. **Can editors preserve the factual prefix and still generate the correct downstream response?** The delayed-removal results document early deletion, and the benchmark contains no delayed insertions. Compare explicit execution-frame conditioning with instruction-only editing on matched early and late interventions, measuring prefix preservation and post-edit CF separately (§4.3, Appendix D.4).
3. **How sensitive is CF to tracking and scoring choices?** The reported rank agreement comes from one scoring protocol with human track verification. Vary the measurability thresholds and compare manual and automated tracking against human judgments on a material-stratified set, reporting score coverage alongside agreement (Appendices A.3–A.4 and C.4).

## Confidence and missing information

**High** for the benchmark design and results stated in the supplied prose. The supplied text lacks most numerical cells from the main and supplementary results tables, including exact overall per-model scores and detailed real-versus-simulated results. It also leaves model-specific CF exclusion counts, variability across repeated generations, and the benchmark download location unavailable. Appendix D.3 announces the benchmark release.
