# Factored One-sided Adam-Moment for Practical and Scalable SOAP

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-13.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.16905) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16905)


## One-sentence takeaway
FOAM combines input-only preconditioning with factored second moments to approach SOAP’s validation loss at roughly half its optimizer state and substantially lower training time in the reported transformer experiments (§§4–5).

## Problem and motivation
SOAP improves optimization quality but stores two curvature factors, their eigenbases, and a dense rotated second moment, creating substantial memory and eigendecomposition costs. The authors ask whether these costs can be reduced while preserving SOAP’s quality.

One-sided rotation and second-moment factorization already existed; the identified gap is explaining **which side to retain, when factorization is justified, and why the combination works** (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Retain the input side.** FOAM drops output-side rotation and keeps the input-gradient factor \(R\). The authors motivate this through affine-layer Hessian structure and empirically stronger input-side Gauss–Newton alignment (§4.1; §5.4; Appendix D).
- **Factor moments after rotation.** Row and column statistics replace the dense second moment, with trace normalization setting its scale. Under the stated K-FAC assumption, this recovers the input-rotated population second moment; diagonal output curvature additionally supports equivalence to SOAP’s bilateral rotation (§4.2; Appendix C).
- **Combine and evaluate.** FOAM integrates these components with an existing adaptive eigenbasis-refresh rule. Its contribution is the structural justification and combined optimizer, rather than inventing either simplification or the refresh policy (§§2, 4–5).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Close to SOAP’s pretraining loss | FOAM trails SOAP by 0.004, 0.008, and 0.005 nats at 124M, 350M, and 760M; authors describe each gap as within about one seed standard deviation. FineWeb; 11 seeds at smaller sizes, 3 at 760M. | §§5.1–5.2; Table 2 discussed in prose |
| Lower state and peak memory | Half SOAP’s matrix-group optimizer state across the three main scales; at 124M, peak training memory is 7.4% above AdamW versus SOAP’s 12.9%. | §5.2 |
| Faster than SOAP at a matched target | At 760M on an 8×H100 node, FOAM reaches AdamW’s final loss in 10.53 hours versus AdamW’s 10.55 hours; SOAP takes 2.15× AdamW’s time. | Figure 1; §5.2 |
| Input-side choice matters | At 124M, input-only rotation retains about 75% of full SOAP’s reported Gauss–Newton alignment, scoring 0.38 versus output-only’s 0.10; FOAM tracks input-only alignment within 1–2%. | §5.4; alignment caption labeled Figure 4 |
| Factorization errors have small measured consequences | At step 2,000 across 18 instrumented matrices, mean relative factorization errors are 0.162 at 124M and 0.147 at 350M; mean dense-versus-factored alignment differences are +0.005 and +0.004. | Appendix C |
| Supporting larger-scale result | At 1.56B and a reported 31B-token budget, SOAP minus FOAM validation loss is −0.0008 ± 0.0002, paired across three seeds. | Appendix E |
| Limited vision transfer | Single-seed ViT-Tiny/Tiny-ImageNet best top-1 accuracy: FOAM 65.4%, SOAP 67.1%, AdamW 58.6%; Muon and AdaMuon each 65.1%. | Appendix F |
| Larger batches remain useful | At 124M with a fixed 2.49B-token budget, increasing batch size from 0.5M to 2M tokens changes FOAM loss from 3.309 to 3.364, versus AdamW’s 3.370 to 3.956; larger-batch rows use one seed. | Appendix G |

These results support a favorable quality–cost tradeoff in the tested settings. They do not establish statistical equivalence to SOAP, universal speedups, or frontier-scale performance. Time to AdamW’s target loss is a specific metric: Figure 1 also reports FOAM’s complete 760M run taking 1.17× AdamW’s total time.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Pretraining stops at 1.56B parameters; input factors and eigenbases are not sharded. Large input dimensions, particularly MLP down-projections, retain substantial quadratic storage (§6).
- **Author-stated:** Key projections show the weakest factorization fidelity. Embeddings and output heads remain on AdamW, so the experiments evaluate a hybrid optimizer configuration (§6; Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference:** Vision, post-training, and larger-batch evidence is less secure because those experiments use single seeds. Appendix H’s post-training results therefore support feasibility more strongly than superiority.
- **Analyst inference:** Curvature alignment and factorization fidelity provide mechanistic support, but do not prove convergence guarantees or downstream capability improvements (§5.4; Appendices C–D).
- **Source limitations:** Numerical table bodies and plotted data are absent from the supplied retrieval, despite their captions being present. The prose also inconsistently references Figure 5 for Gauss–Newton alignment, whose caption is labeled Figure 4.

## Open research questions
1. **Does the tradeoff survive substantially larger pretraining?** The authors identify scale and unsharded factors as unresolved. A next experiment could compare sharded FOAM, SOAP, and Muon at larger sizes using matched quality targets and measured memory (§6).
2. **Can key-specific factorization improve accuracy economically?** Key projections are residual-error outliers. Compare standard FOAM against a key-only dense moment or attention-aware factorization, measuring loss and added cost (Appendix C).
3. **Can FOAM cover embeddings and output heads?** These remain on AdamW despite the authors suggesting hidden-dimension factors are affordable. Test that extension against the current hybrid configuration (§6).
4. **How robust is transfer beyond pretraining?** Vision and post-training evidence is single-seed. Repeat those comparisons across seeds and tuning budgets, reporting final metrics and uncertainty (Appendices F and H).
