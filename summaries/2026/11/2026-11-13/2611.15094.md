# A frontier machine intelligence for human neuroimaging

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-13.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.15094) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15094)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors report that Neuro-VL, trained on health-system MRI and CT studies, outperforms selected general-purpose and medical models on retrospective neuroimaging interpretation, longitudinal comparison, and misleading-prompt tests, while prospective clinical benefit remains unproven.

## Problem and motivation

Complete neuroimaging examinations contain multiple volumetric scans whose findings must be integrated across anatomy, imaging sequences, and time. The authors identify a gap between narrowly trained medical models and general-purpose models lacking access to private clinical imaging: neither adequately supports broad, interactive, study-level interpretation grounded in clinical evidence. (“Main”; “Learning machine intelligence from neuroimaging”)

## Approach and contributions

- **Study-level vision-language model:** Neuro-VL connects a hierarchical ViT-Large encoder to Qwen3-8B through a token-merging MLP connector. Hierarchical attention comes from prior HLIP work; the proposed encoder contribution is dual contrastive supervision aligning visual features with individual report findings and whole-report summaries. (Methods: “Neuro-VL visual encoder”; Extended Data Fig. 2)
- **Expert-guided instruction training:** Report-derived synthetic examples teach imaging statements, visual reasoning traces, answers, and correction of unsupported prompt premises. The final longitudinal model incorporates 970,000 instruction examples totaling 3.6 billion visual and text tokens. (Methods: “Visual instruction data generation”; “Neuro-VL-Longitudinal”)
- **Longitudinal interpretation:** Additional tuning on paired examinations teaches change, trajectory, and intervention characterization while mixing in single-study examples. (Methods: “Neuro-VL-Longitudinal”)
- **Evaluation assets:** NeuroBench evaluates complete examinations; NeuroJudge evaluates report preferences against reference reports. The authors provide code, weights, and benchmark links, although internal patient imaging is restricted. (“Code availability”; “Data availability”)

## Evidence and main results

All results below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Broad closed-ended advantage | Across 31,975 questions and 17 task-group combinations, Neuro-VL beat GPT-5.4 and Gemma 4 on 16 combinations and NeuroVFM on all 17. Equally weighted mean gains were 15.9, 16.9, and 23.0 percentage points, respectively; all P<0.001. Metrics mix accuracy and specified balanced-accuracy tasks. | Fig. 2b; “Locating, describing, diagnosing, and reporting” |
| Preferred single-study reports | On 3,933 studies across internal MRI/CT, MR-RATE, and CTRG, NeuroJudge wins:losses were 2.4:1 against GPT-5.4 and 5.0:1 against Gemma 4; both P<0.001. These are preference ratios, excluding ties from the ratio. | Fig. 2c |
| Better longitudinal question answering | With current and prior images, change accuracy was 87.5% versus GPT-5.4’s 81.6% (718 questions); trajectory accuracy was 96.9% versus 86.5% (710 questions); intervention accuracy was 80.3% versus Gemma 4’s 68.9% (132 questions). Comparators shown are the best baseline for each task. | Fig. 3b; “Longitudinal disease monitoring” |
| Preferred longitudinal reports | On 734 study pairs, NeuroJudge wins:losses were 1.4:1 against GPT-5.4 and 7.8:1 against Gemma 4; both P<0.001. | Fig. 3c |
| Stronger resistance to false pathology premises | On 320 normal MRI/CT studies, success when prompts assumed pathology was 95.6% versus GPT-5.4’s 64.1%; with an assumed location, 97.3% versus 51.4%. Success required avoiding significant pathology assertions throughout the response. | Fig. 4b; red-teaming section |
| Stronger resistance to incorrect diagnoses | On 246 glioma/infarct MRI studies, discordant diagnosis prompts yielded 90.2% success for Neuro-VL versus 44.5% for GPT-5.4, judged by compatibility with the reference diagnostic category. | Fig. 4d |
| Automated report evaluation has imperfect expert agreement | On 200 internal studies comparing GPT-5 and NeuroVFM reports, NeuroJudge agreement with expert preferences was Kendall’s τb=0.554 (95% CI 0.467–0.641); mean inter-expert agreement was 0.637 (0.557–0.708). Neuro-VL reports were excluded. | Extended Data caption “NeuroBench composition and evaluator validation,” labeled Fig. 5 |

These results support comparative performance on the constructed retrospective tasks. They do not establish improved clinical decisions, workflow efficiency, patient outcomes, or faithful internal reasoning. Report preference measures agreement with reference-report content rather than independently verified clinical correctness.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Development was centered at Michigan Medicine; retrospective international validation cannot fully characterize changing populations, acquisition protocols, or clinical workflows. Prospective evaluation and clinician collaboration studies remain pending. (Discussion)
- **Author-stated:** Neuro-VL lacks independent patient-data retrieval, guideline consultation, tool use, and iterative verification. Reasoning traces and attention visualizations do not establish computational faithfulness. (Discussion)
- **Analyst inference—longitudinal scoring ambiguity:** The supplied NeuroJudge prompt explicitly ignores interval-change statements. No separate longitudinal judging prompt is supplied, so longitudinal report preferences do not clearly establish accurate change reporting. (Supplementary Table 6; Fig. 3c)
- **Analyst inference—evaluation scope:** Segmentation-based benchmark questions exclude lesions at or below each dataset’s median volume, limiting evidence about subtle lesions. Red-teaming focuses on selected normal-study findings and glioma/infarct confusions rather than unrestricted clinical adversaries. (Methods: benchmark development; red-teaming)
- **Analyst inference—judge validity:** NeuroJudge was validated on internal GPT-5/NeuroVFM reports, excluding Neuro-VL outputs; transfer of that validation to Neuro-VL and external or longitudinal reports remains uncertain. Red-teaming also uses automated judges. (Methods: benchmark development; red-teaming)
- **Reproducibility:** Internal training and evaluation imaging is not public, and several referenced supplementary tables contain headings without their substantive contents in the supplied text. (“Data availability”; Supplementary Tables 2–3, 7–9)

## Open research questions

- **Does assistance improve clinical care?** Retrospective model comparisons cannot answer this. A prospective randomized reader study could measure diagnostic errors, interpretation time, and downstream decisions. (Discussion)
- **How robust is performance on subtle lesions and new clinical settings?** Larger-lesion selection and retrospective cohorts leave this open. Evaluate consecutive external examinations, stratified by lesion size, protocol, and site. (Methods: benchmark development; Discussion)
- **Are longitudinal reports accurate about interval change?** The supplied judge prompt excludes that content. Have blinded experts score change statements against paired images and compare with a change-aware evaluator. (Supplementary Table 6)
- **Which training components drive premise resistance?** Premise-correction training accompanies strong results, but the supplied text gives no isolated ablation of its effect. Compare matched models trained with and without those examples on unseen misleading prompts. (Methods: instruction-data generation; Fig. 4)

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the architecture, evaluation design, and reported headline results, but independent validity is less certain.

Material missing information includes complete training hyperparameters and baseline system prompts, numerical results for several graphical ablations, a longitudinal-specific report-evaluation prompt, validation of red-teaming judges against clinicians, and prospective clinical outcomes. Extended-data numbering is inconsistent in places, so references above use descriptive captions where needed.
