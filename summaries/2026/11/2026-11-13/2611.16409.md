# Experience-Handoff Reinforcement Learning for Recursive Self-Improvement

> [← Daily summary](../2026-11-13.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.16409) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16409)


## One-sentence takeaway
ACE-RL jointly trains an LLM to act, reflect, and edit task-local playbooks using downstream verifier rewards, achieving the highest reported held-out reward across four benchmarks under matched update FLOPs, with important limits on compute accounting and evaluation validity.

## Problem and motivation
Outcome-only reinforcement learning compresses an agent’s experience into scalar rewards, while reflection and memory harnesses preserve detailed feedback but usually leave the model’s ability to write useful handoffs untrained. The authors address this gap by training both task execution and the experience artifacts that guide subsequent attempts (§§1–2).

Here, “recursive self-improvement” specifically means rewriting context between attempts under frozen inference-time weights and a fixed harness; it does not mean autonomous weight or scaffold modification (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Experience-handoff RL formulation:** Optimize outcomes of the complete harness, including model-written context consumed by later attempts, rather than only the current actor’s reward (§2.2).
- **Executable credit for intermediate text:** In a fixed task-local ACE harness, one shared model acts, reflects on a trajectory, and curates playbook edits. A fresh actor rollout scores each candidate playbook; its verifier reward credits the edit and, through averaging, its reflection (§3.1).
- **Sparse role-specific sampling:** Compare reflections of the same trajectory and edits of the same reflection using GRPO-style advantages. Candidate evaluation rollouts also train the actor, keeping the maximum actor-rollout count equal to GRPO’s while adding reflection and curation calls (§3.2; Appendix D).
- **Empirical handoff evaluation:** Compare against actor-only RL, ERL, and delayed-joint PPO; test longer inference horizons and discard edits to assess the playbook’s contribution (§5).

The supplied derivation explicitly characterizes the implementation as an approximate surrogate, not an exact gradient estimator of the full sequential objective (Appendix D.1).

These results support the reported benchmark ranking and a useful contribution from model-written handoffs in several settings. They do not establish consistent superiority across training seeds, equal total computational cost, or uniformly beneficial memory at longer horizons. Reward is also not uniformly a solve rate: Mastermind and MBPP allow partial credit, while held-out AppWorld and MiniSWEBench use binary success (Appendix E).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope:** Four benchmarks, models up to 4B, one training run per method, task-local memory, and handoffs that can be separately sampled and evaluated. Cross-task transfer and frontier-scale behavior remain untested (Appendix H).
- **Compute comparability:** “Equal compute” counts actor and critic update FLOPs but excludes generation. ACE-RL adds training calls and inference-time reflection/curation, so total cost parity is not established (Appendices E, F.1, H).
- **MBPP validity:** Feedback exposes the scored assertions, and some learned solutions hardcode them. Most reported evaluations predate a grader fix preventing premature process exits; inspected ACE-RL and PPO checkpoints contained no exit calls. **Analyst inference:** the near-ceiling result provides limited evidence of correctness beyond those visible tests (Appendices E, G.1).
- **Credit approximations:** Current-reward actor training omits effects on future evidence; one-handoff rewards omit later consequences. Admissibility filtering, empirical normalization, mixed actor inputs, and token weighting further qualify gradient equivalence (Appendix D.1).
- **Mechanism uncertainty:** Grouping lacks a clean isolated ablation or direct empirical variance measurement; the AppWorld edits-discarded control is outside its compute budget (Appendices B.7, H).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** §4.2 gives evaluation actor temperature as 0.7, while Table 5 specifies 0.6 for MiniSWEBench.

## Open research questions
1. **Does same-input grouping itself improve training stability?** Existing comparisons change several factors. Hold prompts, actor credit, and rollout budgets fixed; vary only grouping across multiple seeds and measure advantage variance (Appendix B.7).
2. **Do gains survive hidden correctness tests?** MBPP exposes its scored assertions and exhibits hardcoding. Repeat training with separate feedback tests and hidden evaluation tests (Appendices E, G.1).
3. **When does retained experience become harmful?** Mastermind’s edit benefit fades beyond the training horizon. Extend evaluation horizons while varying reset intervals and tracking incorrect or stale tips (§5.4).
4. **Is ACE-RL worthwhile under a total compute budget?** Generation is excluded from the comparison. Match training plus inference generation cost against independent sampling and actor-only training (Appendices F.1, H).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text gives detailed methods, benchmark settings, and qualified derivations, but evidence rests largely on single training runs.

Material information unavailable includes cross-seed uncertainty, a controlled grouping-only ablation, hidden-test MBPP performance, total generation cost and wall-clock comparisons, exact numerical compute budgets and main checkpoint selections, and confirmation that the promised code release is available.
