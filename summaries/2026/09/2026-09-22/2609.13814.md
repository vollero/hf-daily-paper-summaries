# Realtime-Venus: A full-duplex interaction system with asynchronous delegation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-22.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.13814) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13814)


## One-sentence takeaway
Realtime-Venus combines continuous audio–visual conversation with asynchronous tool execution, reporting strong benchmark understanding and continuity during non-interruptive speech, while interruption handling and complete tool-task success remain weaker points.

## Problem and motivation
Live assistants must keep perceiving and conversing while slower reasoning or tool calls execute. Background work needs stable evidence from the original request, but its result arrives after the conversation may have changed. The authors address this coordination gap by separating live interaction from background execution while placing both on a shared causal timeline (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Integrated conversational frontends:** Two separately trained 9B models adapt MiniCPM-o 4.5: Omni handles audio and video; Audio handles speech. One-second chunks combine perception, conversational control, response generation, and private delegation requests. The architecture is inherited; the contribution centers on integrating delegation into streaming interaction (§§3–4).
- **Asynchronous execution harness:** Requests capture evidence at their opening boundary, execute through registered capabilities, and return privately to the originating session. The harness prepares reply wording; the frontend controls speech timing. Freshness checks and playback acknowledgments track eligibility and delivery (§5).
- **Coupled training pipeline:** Scenario planning, speech realization, and temporal alignment link overlapping speech to conversational actions and delegation outcomes. Post-training mixes understanding, duplex interaction, and delegation data; only the Thinker is updated, with the acoustic decoder fixed (§§4.5–4.6, 6).
- **Training-free video memory:** Motion-based frame filtering, query relevance and visual novelty scoring, and retrieval of adjacent audio reconstruct historical context beyond the rolling window (§4.2).

The authors claim priority for asynchronous backend invocation during native full-duplex omni interaction; the supplied evidence does not independently establish that priority (§1).

## Evidence and main results

All results below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Omni leads most compared online video models | Highest score on six of eight benchmarks. Accuracy: StreamingBench **70.2% vs 67.9%**, OVO-Bench **64.7% vs 60.7%**, Daily-Omni **81.3% vs 79.4%**, against MiniCPM-o 4.5. Omni trails that baseline on ProactiveVideoQA PAUC and WorldSense accuracy. | §7.3, Table 2 |
| Memory improves long-video accuracy | Omni with vs without memory: **47.06% vs 41.18%** on LVOmniBench’s 60–90-minute bin; **61.90% vs 57.14%** on LongVideoBench’s 40–60-minute bin. All reported Omni duration bins improve. | §7.3, Table 3 |
| Audio understanding remains competitive | Audio reaches **78.0% MMAU** and **63.2% MMAU-Pro** accuracy, versus **77.5% and 61.2%** for Qwen3-Omni, the strongest listed alternative on those tasks. Audio does not lead MMAR or MMSU. | §7.4, Table 4 |
| Spoken QA gains are task-dependent | Audio leads Llama Questions at **83.8%** and Speech CMMLU at **67.8%**, versus best listed alternatives of **83.4%** and **67.7%**. Its **4.81** AlpacaEval judge score ties MiniCPM-o 4.5. | §7.4, Table 5 |
| Strong continuation comes with weaker interruption response | Full-Duplex-Bench v1.5 Audio continuation rates: **97% / 88% / 86%** for backchannels / other-directed / background speech, versus Gemini 3.1 Live’s **95% / 66% / 66%**. Audio interruption response is **75%**, below Joy-Duplex **88%**, GPT-4o **78%**, and Gemini **77%**. | §7.5, Table 6 |
| Tool selection exceeds complete-task reliability | FDB-v3 tool F1 / argument accuracy / Pass@1: Omni **86.0% / 53.1% / 43.0%**; Audio **82.0% / 52.2% / 42.0%**; GPT-Realtime **87.6% / 68.0% / 60.0%**. Tasks use human-recorded disfluent speech and deterministic mock APIs. | §§7.1, 7.5, Table 7 |
| Delegation errors differ by model | Internal benchmark: Omni/Audio overall routing accuracy **75.93% / 68.89%**; external-capability recall **68.33% / 92.22%**; routine-request specificity **84.44% / 39.44%**. Input modes have different difficulty. | §7.6, Table 8 |

These results support benchmark-specific understanding, overlap handling, and tool-use performance. They do not isolate the causal benefit of asynchronous delegation or establish reliable conversational integration of delayed results. Routing accuracy measures the decision to delegate, not successful execution; tool F1, argument accuracy, and Pass@1 have different success criteria and cannot be subtracted to estimate stage-specific failure rates (§§7.3, 7.5–7.6).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Training-component contributions lack controlled ablations. Strong continuation does not imply strong interruption handling; ambiguous overlaps and response-transition timing need further evaluation (§§7.3, 7.5).
- **Author-stated:** Spoken-QA aggregates cannot separate speech interpretation errors from answer-generation errors. Delegation routing evaluation does not establish execution or result-integration success (§§7.4, 7.6).
- **Analyst inference:** The routing comparison cannot isolate modality or model quality because the two input modes explicitly differ in difficulty (§7.6).
- **Analyst inference:** “Realtime” responsiveness is not quantitatively established: the supplied text reports no measured end-to-end latency, throughput, or runtime hardware. One-second chunks specify processing granularity, not achieved response latency (§§4.3, 7–8).
- **Analyst inference:** Some overlap baselines come from prior reports, and no uncertainty estimates are provided. Deterministic mock APIs also leave real-service delay and failure robustness unresolved (Table 6 notes; §7.1).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Earlier prose emphasizes interpreting results against changing intent, whereas the detailed return protocol assigns reply wording to the harness and timing to the frontend. The extent of semantic revision after return is unclear (§§1, 5.3).

## Open research questions
- **Which training components produce the gains?** The paper explicitly lacks component attribution. Run matched-data ablations removing delegation trajectories, duplex supervision, and their coupling, measuring understanding, overlap handling, and tool completion.
- **Can continuity improve without missing genuine interruptions?** Current results expose a trade-off without transition-latency measurements. Evaluate ambiguous overlaps across chunk sizes, measuring interruption response, false interruption, and stopping delay.
- **Does asynchronous delegation remain coherent after intent changes?** Routing scores do not test reintegration. Introduce controlled backend delays, mid-task corrections, cancellations, and out-of-order results; score final-answer relevance and delivery correctness.
- **How efficiently does memory preserve brief events?** Accuracy gains are reported without storage-cost or component ablations. Vary gating and retrieval budgets, measuring brief-event recall, answer accuracy, storage, and retrieval latency.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The architecture and benchmark tables support the main descriptive findings, but causal attribution and deployment reliability remain uncertain.

Material missing information includes training hyperparameters and compute; evaluation uncertainty; internal benchmark size, examples, and labeling procedures; evaluated backend/router configurations; measured runtime latency and hardware; memory budgets and costs; and confirmed availability of model weights, training data, and the internal benchmark.
