# ShieldVLA: Feasibility-Aware Safety Alignment for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-22.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.13231) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13231)


## One-sentence takeaway
ShieldVLA combines a learned reachability critic with rubric-based visual safety supervision to improve safe task completion across five simulated benchmarks, but provides empirical rather than formal safety guarantees and contains material reporting inconsistencies.

## Problem and motivation
The authors argue that Lagrangian VLA fine-tuning couples task reward to expected safety cost, allowing residual violations while encouraging conservative behavior. Visual control also lacks reliable, dense safety annotations. ShieldVLA addresses both problems by estimating where safe continuation is feasible and generating safety supervision from structured VLM judgments (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Feasibility-gated fine-tuning:** An online, off-policy Hamilton–Jacobi safety critic estimates observation–action safety values. Its threshold gates policy updates: PPO reward optimization on estimated feasible samples, safety-value improvement elsewhere. An adaptive safety coefficient still uses dual ascent, but affects only gated unsafe samples (§4.1).
- **Weakly supervised safety margins:** A frozen Qwen3-VL-8B scores weighted hazard rubrics. Logistic calibration against episode-level safety outcomes converts scores into per-frame margins; no manually annotated per-step labels are required. Offline refinement uses a larger VLM to propose criteria for misranked examples (§4.2; Appendix E).
- **Integration across embodiments:** The framework is evaluated with several control backbones and deploys the trained policy without a runtime critic or shield. The contribution is the integration of existing reachability and rubric techniques into VLA fine-tuning, rather than inventing those underlying techniques (Fig. 1; §§2, 5).

Table 2 supports improvement over SafeVLA on both metrics in every environment. **Analyst calculation:** its mean SR increase is 0.13, matching the headline; averaging its five relative CSC reductions gives approximately **47.5%**, not the claimed **57%**. The text does not explain that discrepancy.

These results establish empirical improvements under the reported simulator settings. They do not establish zero violations, formal safety, or universally superior safety: VisionCBF has lower CSC on Safety-CHORES Nav, while rubric supervision sometimes increases CSC relative to binary labels. Table 3 reports three-seed variability for ShieldVLA, but equivalent baseline variability is unavailable.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** The contraction proof does not guarantee safety with neural approximation, finite data, and noisy VLM labels. The learned safe region reflects the rubric signal rather than verified physical constraints (Appendices A–B).
- **Author-stated:** Small scorers can collapse on difficult frames; rare unsafe events limit boundary learning and leave the gate threshold dependent on tuning. All evaluations are simulated (Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference—supervision validity:** Broadcasting an unsafe episode label to every frame can mislabel safe portions. Safety-CHORES quality metrics use episode-derived targets, so they do not independently establish accurate frame-level hazard localization (§4.2; Appendix E.3).
- **Analyst inference—limited causal evidence:** Gating versus penalty is isolated only on TurtleBot-Nav. Downstream policy ablations do not isolate rubric refinement across all environments (Tables 5, 8–12).
- **Reporting conflicts:** The Dubins zero-cost statement contradicts Table 2; Table 7’s purported matching default configuration also gives different results. Appendix D.1 defines SR as goal reaching, unlike §5.2’s safe completion definition.
- **OOD ambiguity:** Section 5.5 describes “+All” as joint color and lighting changes, whereas Appendix E.3 describes averaging individual variants. Its explanation invokes test-time critic gating despite the stated shield-free deployment (Fig. 1; §5.5).
- **Implementation ambiguity:** The continuous-action safety-gradient description does not clearly specify its adaptation to discrete Safety-CHORES actions. The Safety-CHORES scorer prompt also presupposes a detected violation, leaving its use on all frames unclear (§4.1; Appendices D.3, E.4.4).

## Open research questions
- **Does the method transfer to physical robots?** Only simulation is tested. A next experiment could recalibrate on physical observations and compare paired safe-completion and violation outcomes against SafeVLA.
- **How much does episode-label noise distort the safe region?** Frame-level correctness is only partially validated. Audit held-out trajectories with independent per-frame labels and compare episode-calibrated versus frame-supervised critics.
- **Does gating help consistently across action spaces?** The controlled penalty comparison covers one environment. Repeat it across the other embodiments with matched supervision, tuning budgets, and multiple baseline seeds.
- **Does better rubric discrimination improve policy safety?** RTD diagnostic gains do not isolate downstream effects. Run a factorial policy-training study crossing scorer size and rubric version on separate calibration and evaluation scenes.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The method and main tabulated comparisons are reasonably explicit, but conflicting metrics and protocols reduce confidence in aggregate and robustness claims.

Material missing information includes the calculation behind the 57% headline, resolution of the conflicting Dubins and OOD results, baseline seed uncertainty, an explicit discrete-action recovery update, clearly separated calibration/RTD evaluation splits, and release availability for code, checkpoints, and data.
