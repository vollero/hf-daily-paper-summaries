# onPanda: Efficient Annotation of On-Policy Alignment Data for LLMs and Agents via Token-Level Correction

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-22.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.24983) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24983)


## One-sentence takeaway
onPanda’s token-correction-and-continuation workflow halved median annotation time versus manual post-editing in a small image-description study, but downstream training benefits remain untested (§3.1; Limitations).

## Problem and motivation
Alignment-data annotation trades off human effort, proximity to the generating model’s distribution, and supervision detail. The authors argue that manual editing is costly and shifts responses off-policy, while response ranking provides coarse feedback and cannot repair a set of uniformly poor candidates. Agent annotation adds the requirement to execute corrected tool calls and incorporate actual environment feedback (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Interactive correction:** Annotators identify the first inappropriate token, select a probability-ranked alternative or type a replacement, and regenerate the suffix from the corrected prefix. The system preserves earlier tokens and records each branch (§§2.2–2.3).
- **Structured annotation infrastructure:** Model-specific response templates expose reasoning, content, and tool calls for correction; MCP connections execute tools and return feedback. Session files support exports for SFT, response preferences, and position-specific token corrections (§§2.1–2.4).
- **Dataset and benchmark:** The authors release Panda-CVL, a predominantly Chinese vision-language dataset with 7,491 sessions, split into 6,839 training and 652 test sessions. Its benchmark evaluates acceptance decisions, first-error localization, and correction (§4).

Correct-and-continue interaction predates this work, as the authors acknowledge. Their contribution is its integration with candidate-token selection, structured agent execution, and traceable alignment-data exports (Appendix C).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Faster annotation than post-editing | Three annotators, 21 image-description prompts, Latin-square assignment, Qwen3.5-35B-A3B rollouts: median time **330 s** for onPanda versus **681 s** for POTATO, a **51.5% reduction**; Argilla took **336 s**. Means were **515.6**, **711.1**, and **684.5 s**, respectively. | §3.1; Table 1 |
| Favorable judged quality and workload | GPT-5.5 pairwise win rates against the other workflows: **66.7%** onPanda, **54.8%** POTATO, **28.6%** Argilla. Human evaluators preferred onPanda over POTATO in **54.8%** of pairs. Adapted NASA-TLX workload scores were **3.1**, **6.8**, and **5.4**, respectively, on a lower-is-better 0–10 scale. | §§3.1–3.2; Table 1 |
| Perplexity remained close to sampled responses | Under the rollout model, onPanda PPL was **1.181**, **+0.86%** relative to the **1.171** resampling baseline; POTATO was **1.596**, **+36.31%**; Argilla was **1.161**, **−0.83%**. | §3.1; Table 1 |
| More usable outputs within the workflow | SFT coverage was **21/21** prompts for both editing workflows, by design, versus **11/21** for ranking. Mean derivable preference pairs per prompt: **7.43** onPanda, **0.95** POTATO, **6.00** Argilla. | §3.1; Table 1 |
| Production use involved sparse direct intervention | Across qualified production responses, authors report **97.0%** model-generated tokens, **2.1%** candidate-selected tokens, and **0.9%** manually typed tokens; deployments covered vision, audio, and agents. | §3.2; Table 2 |
| Automated correction remained difficult | Panda-CVL expands **652** test conversations into **2,126** instances: **652 good**, **1,474 not good**. GPT-5.5 achieved best overall **F1 17.09%**; GPT-6 achieved best first-error localization **24.46%** and joint localization/correction **15.83%** on not-good instances. | §4; Appendix A, Table 3 |
| Human references show substantial variation | Four annotations per prompt on **21** image-description prompts: exact pairwise location agreement **30.95%**, versus **0.20%** uniform chance; replacement agreement **69.44%**, conditional on identical locations. | Appendix B; Figure 3 |

The evidence supports improved annotation efficiency in the tested workflow and documents operational deployment. Low perplexity change supports the authors’ claim of distributional proximity, but **does not establish equivalence to unmodified on-policy sampling**. Production volume and exported pair counts do not establish training value. Benchmark F1 is specifically the harmonic mean of good-response acceptance accuracy and exact correction accuracy on not-good responses (§4).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** The controlled study uses few annotators, one rollout model, and image-description tasks. Participants are in-house professionals; quality judgments primarily use an LLM, with a small supplementary human comparison (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** Comparisons change both interface and annotation paradigm, preventing attribution of gains to individual components. There is no controlled agent study or direct Reptile comparison (§3.1; Appendix C; Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** Benefits depend on sparse errors and access to prefix continuation and logprobs. New structured models require response-template adaptation. Distributional proximity may weaken when training another model or updating weights (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** No training experiment validates the proposed token-level supervision (Limitations).
- **Analyst inference:** Single-reference exact matching can penalize plausible alternative corrections, given incomplete human agreement. Low benchmark scores therefore combine correction difficulty with reference sensitivity (§4; Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference:** No component ablation isolates candidate selection, probability shading, and suffix regeneration; their individual contributions remain uncertain (§3).

## Open research questions
1. **Does token-level correction improve learning per annotation hour?** No downstream training is reported. Compare SFT, response-level preference training, and token-level objectives under matched human-hour budgets.
2. **When do dense errors eliminate the efficiency advantage?** The sparse-error boundary is acknowledged but not quantified. Vary rollout-model capability and task difficulty while measuring correction density, time, and final quality.
3. **Do benefits transfer to agent trajectories?** Agent evidence is limited to capabilities and deployment statistics. Run a controlled tool-use study measuring annotation time, execution success, and correction-induced failures.
4. **How sensitive are model rankings to reference choice?** Human first-error annotations vary. Re-evaluate the same models using multiple references and independent adjudication of alternative valid corrections.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly describes the system and reported results, but generalization and training utility remain uncertain.

Material information unavailable includes uncertainty intervals or significance tests for workflow gains, detailed human-comparison procedures, component ablations, controlled audio/agent comparisons, downstream training results, and benchmark sensitivity to multiple acceptable references.
