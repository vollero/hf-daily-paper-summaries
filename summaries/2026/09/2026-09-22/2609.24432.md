# 1% of Tokens Can Be Enough: On Gradient Estimation in On-Policy Distillation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-22.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.24432) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24432)


## One-sentence takeaway

Selecting tokens using estimated gradient reliability alongside usefulness can match or exceed full on-policy distillation at nominal 0.1%–1% supervision budgets in tested reasoning settings, but does not produce proportional computational savings (Tables 1–3).

## Problem and motivation

Sparse on-policy distillation (OPD) selects which student-generated tokens receive training supervision. Existing selectors emphasize usefulness, but a useful teacher correction can still yield a noisy gradient when estimated from a single sampled next token. The authors address this distinction between supervision usefulness and estimation reliability, which matters particularly when very few tokens contribute to updates (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Reliability analysis:** At a fixed prefix, the authors derive gradient signal and sampling noise in Fisher geometry. Their information-efficiency ratio (IER) divides signal by noise under the optimal action-independent scalar baseline; its reciprocal is the single-sample relative mean-squared error (Theorem 1; Appendix B.3).
- **Practical selector:** Approximate IER over the union of teacher and student top-16 candidates plus the sampled token. Missing logits receive a fixed value, distributions are renormalized, and likelihood ratios are clipped. This produces a ranking proxy rather than an exact full-vocabulary score (§4; Appendix C.1).
- **Combination with usefulness:** Combine normalized IER ranks with usefulness scores through soft OR, \(1-(1-u)(1-r)\), or AND, \(ur\). Select tokens under a batch-level budget while retaining at least one per response and keeping the sampled reverse-KL objective (§§4–5.1).

The results support selective supervision as effective in several tested settings, with no universally best selector. They do not establish universal superiority, causal attribution of gains to reduced gradient noise, or large compute savings. Reported standard errors are 0.4–1 for mathematical results and approximately 0.5/1 for HealthBench overall/hard, so small score differences should not be read as established improvements (Tables 1–2).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** High IER guarantees neither gradient magnitude nor task alignment nor beneficial parameter updates; usefulness must be assessed separately (Appendix B.3).
- **Author-stated:** Both usefulness scores and practical IER are approximations; gains vary by selector and setting (§§5.3, 6).
- **Author-stated:** Complete trajectories are still generated and scored, so retaining fewer supervised tokens does not proportionally reduce computation (Appendix C.3).
- **Analyst inference:** The fixed-prefix, optimal-baseline result does not establish reliability of the actual aggregated parameter update. The supplied experiments do not directly validate that relationship (§3; Appendix B.3).
- **Analyst inference:** No reported sensitivity study establishes robustness to candidate-set size, missing-logit replacement, or clipping, despite their role in the proxy (Appendix C.1).
- **Evaluation boundaries:** Experiments cover three teacher–student pairs and two domains. Medical evaluation uses an automated judge whose reported HealthBench meta-evaluation macro F1 is 0.6614; clinical deployment validity is not established (§5.1; Appendix C.2).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** The one-token-per-response minimum can affect nominal budgets; realized retention fractions are not reported. Also, Figure 3’s caption names TIP whereas §5.4 names TA-OPD for the corresponding budget comparisons.

## Open research questions

1. **Does approximate IER predict actual update reliability?** The theorem concerns a local optimal-baseline estimator. Compare proxy rankings with repeated-sample gradient errors and parameter-update alignment at held-out prefixes.
2. **How sensitive is selection to candidate approximation?** Candidate size and numerical substitutions are fixed. Sweep these choices and compare rankings against full-vocabulary IER on a tractable subset.
3. **When should OR or AND be used?** Neither consistently wins. Test a combination-selection rule on held-out model pairs and budgets.
4. **Can reliability selection save computation?** Current training retains complete rollouts. Test the authors’ proposed reliability-triggered truncation against full-rollout selection at matched wall-clock budgets (Appendix C.3).

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly supports the method and setting-specific outcomes, but broader reliability and efficiency claims remain limited.

Material missing information includes realized token-retention fractions, training-seed replication for most methods, per-result uncertainty beyond summary ranges, approximation ablations, direct measurements connecting IER to actual update noise, and numerical values for several plotted analyses. Code availability is announced but was not independently verified.
