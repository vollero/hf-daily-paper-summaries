# VideoGen-Agent: Reinforcing Video Generation Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-22.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.24997) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24997)


## One-sentence takeaway
VideoGen-Agent learns tool use through supervised fine-tuning and multitask reinforcement learning, improving its base generator’s score from 56.5 to 75.6 on the authors’ VABench, with further gains from upgraded generation tools (Table 2).

## Problem and motivation
Visually convincing video generators can still misrepresent specialized procedures, named subjects, physical motion, scene relationships, and ordered events. The authors identify a gap between task-specific augmentation workflows and a shared agent that learns which tools to invoke and how to use their outputs across different generation requirements (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Shared video-generation agent:** A Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct policy coordinates retrieval, simulation, generation, and verification through multi-turn interactions. Workflows include reference-conditioned identity preservation, simulation-guided motion, detection-guided regeneration, and sequential generation using preceding segments’ final frames (§§2.1, 3.1; Table 1).
- **Training recipe and trajectory dataset:** The authors construct 24K teacher trajectories across six balanced categories, using 16K for supervised fine-tuning and the remaining 8K prompts for fresh RL rollouts. GRPO combines format validity, video quality, and task-appropriate tool utilization, followed by task-level advantage normalization (§§2.2–2.4).
- **Benchmark and tool-upgrade evaluation:** VABench contains 600 held-out prompts, equally distributed across six capabilities. The same trained policy is evaluated with two compatible toolsets, testing whether stronger generation backends help without further agent training (§3.1; Table 5).

The contribution is principally this integrated training and evaluation framework; GRPO, the base policy, retrieval services, and generation backends are existing components (§4).

## Evidence and main results
Unless otherwise noted, scores below are VABench’s normalized 0–100 category scores, averaged equally across six categories on 600 prompts. Gemini 3.1 Pro supplies rubric scores, supplemented by code-based motion checks for physics.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Agent improves over its base generator | Toolset 1 scores **75.6**, versus **56.5** for standalone Seedance 1.0: **+19.1 points**. | Table 2; §3.2 |
| Stronger tools help a fixed policy | Replacing generation tools raises overall score from **75.6 to 86.1** without retraining; augmentation and verification tools remain fixed. | Tables 2, 5 |
| Upgraded agent leads reported baselines | Toolset 2 scores **86.1**, versus **73.2** for Seedance 2.0, and leads every category. Multi-shot scores are **94.6 versus 68.9**; physics scores are **69.2 versus 60.2**. | Table 2 |
| Both training stages contribute | With Toolset 1 fixed: zero-shot agent **59.5**, SFT **69.2**, full SFT+RL **75.6**. Prompt rewriting alone scores **57.3**. | Table 3 |
| Both substantive reward components help | Full agent scores **75.6**; removing VLM reward yields **73.3**, and removing tool-use reward yields **71.2**. | Table 3 |
| Multitask sharing does not outperform specialization overall | Separately trained single-task agents average **76.3**, versus **75.6** for the shared multitask agent. | Table 3; §3.2 |
| Human preference supports upgraded configuration | Across **100 randomly sampled pairs**, with **four raters per pair**, Toolset 2 receives **84.3%** preference against Seedance 2.0 across ratings. | §3.2 |
| Judge agreement varies by capability | Across a separate **600-pair** assessment with **three raters per pair**, Gemini agrees with human majority **92.0%** overall, but only **72%** on procedural knowledge. | Appendix B.2 |

These results support useful learned tool orchestration within the authors’ benchmark and compatible backend substitutions. Fixed-toolset ablations provide stronger evidence for training benefits than standalone comparisons alone. They do not establish superiority under equal inference budgets, broad real-world generalization, or exact physical fidelity. Robotic manipulation and unseen task combinations receive qualitative descriptions without aggregate quantitative results (§3.2).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Generation quality and latency, limited verification coverage, and the six-task training scope constrain the system. Correct retrieved instructions can still produce incorrect motion, illustrated by persistent Tai Chi stance errors (§6; Figure 3).
- **Analyst inference—evaluation dependence:** Training and benchmark scoring use the same judge and rubrics. Human comparisons offer supporting evidence, but weaker procedural agreement leaves that category less secure. Retrieved procedural references are treated as ground truth by the rubric, creating sensitivity to reference errors (§3.1; Appendix A.5; B.2).
- **Analyst inference—comparison scope:** Standalone baselines generate once, while agents can retrieve, use specialized conditioning, and regenerate. No matched-cost or matched-call comparison is reported, so system gains cannot all be attributed to policy learning (§3.1).
- **Analyst inference—limited generalization evidence:** Prompts deliberately simplify non-target requirements; combined requirements are explored without reported sample counts or quantitative scores (§§2.2, 3.2).
- **Analyst inference—physics and timing scope:** The disclosed collision simulator restricts motion to a plane; physical plausibility dominates the physics rubric. Multi-shot evaluation uses adjusted timing when requested durations are unsupported, so scores do not establish exact adherence to original durations (Appendices A.4–A.5).
- **Analyst inference—missing isolation and operational evidence:** There is no reported ablation of task-level advantage normalization or individual verification tools. Ultimately failed external-tool rollouts are excluded from RL, but failure rates and deployment reliability are not quantified (§§2.4, 3.1; Table 3).

## Open research questions
- **How much gain survives equal inference budgets?** Current standalone comparisons do not match calls or cost. Compare the trained agent with fixed workflows and repeated standalone generation under identical budgets.
- **Does orchestration generalize to systematically combined requirements?** Existing combination evidence is qualitative. Evaluate a held-out suite combining identity, procedural, physical, and temporal constraints, reporting success and tool-selection errors.
- **Do gains persist under independent evaluation?** The training judge also supplies benchmark scores. Repeat evaluation with independent expert annotations, especially for procedures, and fully specified quantitative motion measurements.
- **Which feedback mechanisms drive refinement?** Reward ablations do not isolate detector, depth, or normalization effects. Remove each component separately while holding tools and training budgets fixed.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** Reported scores and the central method are clearly documented, but broader validity is less established.

Material missing information includes uncertainty estimates and repeated-run variation; inference latency, cost, and call counts; benchmark provenance and overlap checks; detailed motion-tracking scoring procedures; quantitative combination/robotics results; and confirmed dataset/model release availability. Table 5 lists depth estimation, but the supplied agent system prompt does not expose a corresponding tool interface.
