# DRACO: Fine-Grained Credit Assignment with Dynamic Rubrics for Long-Horizon Agent Training

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-04.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.04094) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04094)


## One-sentence takeaway

DRACO combines rollout-adaptive rubrics with step-level GRPO advantages to improve Qwen3.6-27B’s AppWorld TN task completion from 69.4% to 85.3% without ground-truth training rewards, though attribution correctness remains unvalidated (Table 2; §6).

## Problem and motivation

Long-horizon agents need useful training feedback even when no programmatic success checker exists. A single rubric-derived trajectory reward assigns identical credit to helpful, redundant, and mistaken steps. The authors identify a gap in combining adaptive evaluation criteria with fine-grained credit assignment that requires neither ground-truth outcomes nor a trained attribution model (§§1–2).

“Outcome-blind” here means withholding ground-truth outcomes during training; judges still assess apparent task achievement from trajectory text (Appendix F).

## Approach and contributions

- **Dynamic reward construction:** Generate criteria from the instruction and sampled rollouts, merge and deduplicate them across each task’s rollout group, and discard criteria that nobody fails. Reward is `(passes − failures)/(passes + failures)`, excluding inapplicable criteria (§3.2).
- **Rubric-conditioned credit:** Obtain criterion-level step citations from a judge. Reinforce higher-rated steps in trajectories with positive group-relative advantage and suppress lower-rated steps in negative-advantage trajectories. A closed-form rule distributes each step’s allocation across its tokens, preserving the scalar sum of advantages over credited tokens—not the gradient vector (§3.3; Appendix E.4).
- **Empirical decomposition:** Compare dynamic versus static rubrics, with versus without step credit, alongside verifier-reward training and self-judging. The distinctive contribution is their combination in outcome-blind agent training; the authors’ broader priority claims are not independently verified here (§§2, 4).

The implementation includes a separate trajectory-level attribution judge call after scoring; “closed-form” describes the redistribution arithmetic, not the generation of attribution evidence (Appendix F).

## Evidence and main results

Unless specified otherwise, results use Qwen3.6-27B trained on 90 AppWorld tasks. Trained scores average the final three epoch checkpoints, each evaluated three times. `p1` is mean success; `p3` requires success in all three trials (§4.1; Table 4).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong in-domain improvement | AppWorld TN, 168 tasks: TGC/SGC p1 reaches **85.3%/70.6%**, versus **69.4%/41.1%** base and **80.0%/59.3%** verifier-reward GRPO. | Table 2 |
| Improved repeated success | AppWorld TN TGC p3: **72.8%**, versus **47.6%** base and **63.3%** verifier-reward GRPO. | Table 2 |
| Component combination matters | AppWorld TN TGC/SGC p1: full DRACO **85.3%/70.6%**; dynamic rubrics alone **82.1%/64.9%**; static rubrics without credit **81.1%/59.9%**. | Table 2; §4.3 |
| Challenge-split gains are mixed | AppWorld TC, 417 tasks: DRACO TGC p1 **61.5%**, versus **49.7%** base; its SGC p1 **40.7%** trails static rubrics without credit at **42.4%**. | Table 2 |
| Zero-shot transfer | τ-bench Banking, 97 tasks: SR p1 **20.4%** with frontier-judge training and **21.1%** with self-judging, versus **15.8%** base and **17.6%** verifier-reward training. | Table 2 |
| Second-model improvement, below SALT | Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct on AppWorld TN: TGC/SGC p1 **62.9%/42.3%**, versus **35.7%/17.3%** base and published outcome-aware SALT **66.2%/47.9%**. | Table 2 |
| Lower inference and judge costs in specific settings | AppWorld TN evaluation: **$8.27** and **14.7 turns** versus base **$10.77** and **18.7 turns**. Self-judging reduces judge cost over 100 training steps from **$1,607 to $316**. | §4.4; Table 3; Figure 8 |

The results support improved benchmark performance from training without verifier rewards and an interaction between adaptive rubrics and credit assignment. They do not establish correct causal attribution, general superiority across metrics, or robustness across independent training seeds. The abstract’s **5.3-point** transfer gain corresponds to self-judging; frontier-judge DRACO improves SR by **4.6 points** (Table 2).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ limitations:** No human validation of rubric fidelity or step attribution; judge consistency can conceal systematic error. Group-dependent criterion selection introduces unmeasured variance, and evaluation repeats do not characterize training variability (§6).
- **Analyst inference—limited external validity:** Training uses one simulated benchmark, with transfer tested only on banking customer service. Real-world unverifiable-task effectiveness remains unestablished (§§4.1, 7).
- **Analyst inference—credit mechanism is not fully isolated:** No reported ablation separates quality-based attribution from inverse-step-length weighting. Appendix B.3 calls unanimous-verdict updates identical to baseline GRPO, but Appendix E.7 gives equal step totals, which differ from uniform token advantages when step lengths differ.
- **Analyst inference—length claim needs qualification:** The rule fixes each step’s *fraction* of total advantage independently of its length; absolute step totals still contain total trajectory length \(N\). Step splitting also changes allocations, as the authors acknowledge (Appendix E.9).
- **Analyst inference—implementation ambiguity:** Attribution can override scoring verdicts, but the supplied text does not clearly specify whether those overrides also recompute trajectory rewards. Separate attribution calls also complicate interpreting credit ablations as changing only redistribution (Appendix F).

## Open research questions

- **Does credit reach causally responsible steps?** End-task gains do not answer this (§6). Next experiment: compare judge citations with controlled step interventions and human annotations.
- **How much improvement comes from attribution versus length weighting?** These effects are not separately ablated. Next experiment: compare uniform token advantages, equal step totals, genuine citations, and shuffled citations under matched judge budgets.
- **How stable is dynamic rubric selection?** Training-seed and dropout-induced variance remain unmeasured (§6). Next experiment: repeat training across seeds while varying rollout-group size and criterion filtering.
- **What judge capability is necessary?** Self-judge replay measures agreement with a frontier judge, not correctness, and uses a weaker configuration than the main self-judge run (Appendix B.1). Next experiment: independently vary generation, merging, and attribution judges against human-reviewed criteria.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text provides detailed methods, prompts, configurations, and benchmark tables, but causal interpretation is limited by unvalidated attribution and internal inconsistencies.

Material missing information includes independent training-seed results, human rubric/attribution validation, an isolated length-weighting ablation, clear handling of attribution verdict overrides, and total training compute cost beyond judge charges. Code availability is author-reported; repository contents were not checked.
