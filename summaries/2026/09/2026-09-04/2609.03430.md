# Random Attention: Rethinking KV Cache Eviction for Efficient Reasoning

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-04.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.03430) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03430)


## One-sentence takeaway
Protecting the prompt and randomly retaining generated KV entries is competitive with scored eviction on the tested reasoning benchmarks and delivers 32–43% higher throughput than TriAttention in the reported vLLM setting, but loses isolated facts and sometimes trails scored methods (§§4–6; Appendix A).

## Problem and motivation
Long reasoning traces make the KV cache a memory bottleneck. Existing eviction methods rank cached tokens using estimated importance; the authors ask whether those signals improve accuracy beyond simply protecting the input. Their identified confound is that baseline methods differ in whether they preserve the prompt, mixing selection quality with protection policy (§§1–2, 5.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Eviction method:** Preserve the entire prefill, including system instructions and question, plus the recent buffer; fill the remaining persistent budget by uniform random selection independently per layer and KV head. Evicted entries are permanently discarded. The implementation uses random values and top-k, avoiding content-dependent importance scoring (§3; Appendix F).
- **Controlled comparisons:** Add identical prompt protection to existing selectors to isolate its contribution from their ranking rules (§5.1; Appendices C–D).
- **Mechanistic diagnostics:** Use planted facts, shared selections across heads, and contiguous-block retention to test how surviving copies support retrieval. The authors interpret the results as evidence for redundancy within traces and across heads (§5.2).
- **Serving integration:** Evaluate the method through shared vLLM paging and compaction infrastructure, isolating the overhead of content-dependent selection (§6; Appendix G).

Repeated random eviction creates an implicit preference for recent tokens; it is not uniform sampling over all previously generated tokens (Appendix D).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Competitive main-grid accuracy, with a code exception | Authors report 31 significant wins and one loss across 60 baseline comparisons on three models. On Qwen3-32B LiveCodeBench at K=3072, pass@1 is 0.806 versus TriAttention’s 0.834 and full attention’s 0.886. | §4.2; Table 1 |
| Additional scale reveals more exceptions | On Qwen3-14B, TriAttention significantly leads on MATH500 and LiveCodeBench; VaSE significantly leads on pooled AIME. Table values are respectively 0.891 vs 0.870, 0.843 vs 0.820, and 0.668 vs 0.642. | Appendix A; Table 5 |
| Prompt protection explains substantial deficits | On Phi-4 GPQA-D at K=2048, protecting the prompt raises SnapKV accuracy from 0.442 to 0.667; Random Attention scores 0.678. On Phi-4 LiveCodeBench at K=3072, SnapKV pass@1 rises from 0.314 to 0.666 versus Random Attention’s 0.667. | Tables 2, 6 |
| Multiple surviving heads improve planted-fact retrieval | For a fact placed 1,536 tokens before the query on Qwen3-4B, the best single head retrieves it in 3% of trials, a selected pair in 60%, three heads in 83%, and all eight in 99%. | §5.2; Figure 3a |
| Cross-head diversity adds little in the tested natural traces | Qwen3-4B MATH500 accuracy with shared versus independent random selections is 0.871 vs 0.874 at K=1024 and 0.788 vs 0.789 at K=512. | Appendix D |
| Isolated delayed facts expose failure | After 57 compression rounds, passcode retrieval is 0.000 for Random Attention, 0.836 for R-KV, and 0.344 for VaSE. | §5.3; Table 3 |
| Paged serving gains depend on workload | On one H200, K=2048, 1k-token prompts and nominal 32k-token outputs, Random Attention delivers 32–43% more output tokens/s than TriAttention across four models. Phi-4 actually uses 31.5k outputs. At 8k outputs, Random Attention achieves only 0.52–0.96× full-attention throughput across the models. | Table 4; Appendix G |

The evidence supports prompt protection as a major contributor and random retention as a strong baseline in these settings. It does not establish universal parity with scored eviction or full attention. The planted-fact experiments demonstrate useful surviving copies, but the natural-trace diversity ablation does not establish that independent head selections are necessary for benchmark accuracy.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-acknowledged boundaries:** Long code prompts consume substantial protected capacity; LiveCodeBench is excluded from the tight-budget sweep. Rare, unrepeated facts can be lost completely (§§4.2–4.3, 5.3).
- **Incomplete controls:** The protected-baseline grid excludes Qwen3-14B. Text-level redundancy is an explanatory interpretation rather than a directly isolated natural-trace intervention (Appendix C; §5.2).
- **Serving scope:** Measurements use H200 hardware. The paging integration cannot safely handle compressed-request preemption, requiring concurrency caps; full-attention throughput ratios include preemption overhead. The unpaged TriAttention comparison uses an unfused scorer, which the authors explicitly caution against treating as its attainable performance (Appendix G).
- **Analyst inference—claim strength:** Nonsignificant differences do not prove equivalence. Moreover, both TriAttention and Random Attention already protect prompts, so attributing their code gap to prompt length requires additional intervention (Appendix C).
- **Reporting inconsistencies:** Appendix A describes the Qwen3-14B code margin as 2.6 points, whereas Table 5 implies 2.3. Section 5.3 describes R-KV as accumulating attention over history, differing from its recent-window-plus-redundancy formulation in §§2 and 7.

## Open research questions
- **How should long prompts share the cache budget with reasoning?** Whole-prompt protection limits code settings. Compare selective prompt compression against full protection at fixed total budgets, stratified by prompt length.
- **Can inexpensive protection recover isolated facts?** Random retention fails the delayed-passcode test. Test a small content-selected reserve alongside random retention on both delayed retrieval and reasoning accuracy.
- **When does cross-head diversity matter in real reasoning?** The reported natural-trace ablation shows negligible benefit. Repeat it across tasks and model families while measuring or manipulating fact restatement.
- **How much of the serving advantage survives asynchronous scoring?** The authors attribute much overhead to synchronized compression. Compare asynchronous and synchronous selectors under identical paging, workload, and capacity settings.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text provides extensive methods, controls, and measurements, but broad claims exceed some demonstrated results.

Material missing information includes the complete per-comparison confidence intervals and p-values, a stated equivalence criterion, prompt-over-budget handling, direct measurements of rare-fact frequency in natural traces, and a protected Qwen3-14B grid. Public code and logs are author-reported; their contents were not supplied or independently checked.
