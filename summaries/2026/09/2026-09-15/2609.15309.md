# When Agents Slow Down: Understanding LLM Agents&#x27; Test-Time Strategies via Elo-per-token Analysis

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.15309) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15309)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across open-ended optimization tasks, agents initially improve faster per token than independent sampling but later fall below its 400-Elo-per-decade reference, and splitting compute near this crossover can outperform both one long session and many short sessions.

## Problem and motivation

LLM agents choose how to spend test-time compute through revision, tool use, exploration, and stopping decisions, yet existing evaluations do not reveal the marginal value of extending an agent trajectory. Binary terminal benchmarks hide intermediate progress, while raw scores from open-ended tasks are difficult to compare across tasks and score ranges. The authors seek a common, compute-aware measure that can distinguish useful within-session adaptation from gains obtainable through independent restarts (Sections 1 and 3).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Elo-per-token:** At each token checkpoint, the method takes every session’s best score so far, converts within-task score orderings into pairwise outcomes, and fits Bradley–Terry ratings. Self-Elo measures within-system scaling; joint-Elo permits cross-system comparisons (Sections 3.1–3.2; Figure 2).

2. **Sampling reference:** The authors prove that best-of-\(n\) independent attempts gains exactly 400 Elo per tenfold compute increase, assuming continuous i.i.d. scores and equal attempt cost (Theorem 3.1; Appendix A.1).

3. **Long-horizon empirical comparison:** Four general agents are evaluated on 14 problems from four open-ended benchmarks, using five sessions per agent–task pair and targets of 100M cache-inclusive tokens. Historical human contestants and three specialized optimization approaches are also studied (Sections 4–5; Appendix C.1).

4. **Compute allocation rule:** The scaling inflection point is defined as the last budget where the agent’s marginal slope exceeds 400 Elo per decade. The authors use it to choose how many independent sessions should share a fixed budget (Section 6; Theorems 6.1–6.2).

The evidence supports diminishing marginal Elo gains over the measured budgets and shows that an estimated crossover can guide allocation on two tasks. It does not establish that every agent, task, or deployment setting follows the same curve, that Elo gains correspond to equal practical utility, or that human wall-clock time is equivalent to agent token compute. Claims about continual learning and “sticky basins” are interpretations consistent with the curves, rather than direct measurements of internal learning mechanisms.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** The framework requires open-ended tasks with continuously scored intermediate submissions; standard pass/fail benchmarks do not supply the needed trajectories (Sections 1 and 3.1).

- **Finite and selected task set:** The headline evaluation covers 14 selected problems across four benchmarks. Bootstrap intervals resample sessions while holding tasks fixed, so they do not measure uncertainty over a wider task population (Section 4.1; Appendix B.2).

- **Allocation evidence is narrow:** The allocation rule is tested with Kimi K2.7 on two dedicated tasks. On MLS-Bench, the strongest one-, two-, and three-session allocations are statistically indistinguishable (Appendix E).

- **Metric assumptions:** The sampling and allocation theorems require i.i.d. continuous outcomes and equal-cost attempts or sessions; Theorem 6.1 additionally requires a common Bradley–Terry scale and sustained post-inflection behavior (Theorems 3.1 and 6.1–6.2).

- **Human–agent comparability:** Human curves use wall-clock contest time, whereas most agent curves use tokens. Figure 5 explicitly compares scaling behavior rather than absolute speed, and AHC014 is the only joint time-aligned human–agent comparison (Section 4.3).

- **Score-data filtering:** Twelve relative-scored long AHC contests were excluded because stored scores were not comparable over time. Rejudged AHC031 failed the authors’ standings check and was excluded from pooled human self-Elo fits (Appendix D).

- **Token accounting:** Headline curves count cached input tokens, while specialized methods use uncached or generated tokens. The authors justify each axis, but comparisons across strategy classes remain sensitive to accounting conventions (Sections 4.1 and 5; Appendix F).

- **Analyst inference:** The reported inflection point is estimated from a discretized, noisy curve and requires substantial profiling. The paper shows that domain-level curves may not predict task-level crossings, limiting immediate use on unseen tasks (Section 7; Appendix E).

- **Analyst inference:** The sticky-basin model provides a possible explanation but assumes persistent session-specific basins and, in its formal version, Gaussian candidate scores; the experiments do not directly identify basins or causal mechanisms (Section 7; Theorem 7.1).

## Open research questions

1. **Can an inflection point be predicted early?** Full task-level profiling is expensive, and domain-level crossings failed to align with the two task-level estimates. A next experiment could train a predictor from early trajectory statistics and test it prospectively on held-out tasks (Section 7).

2. **Can deliberate strategy resets sustain slopes above 400 Elo per decade?** The sticky-basin hypothesis suggests that accumulated context may impede reconsideration, but this mechanism is not directly tested. Compare matched agents with periodic summarization, branching, or forced high-level replanning under equal token budgets.

3. **How broadly does the allocation rule generalize?** Evidence comes from one agent system and two tasks, with uncertainty among leading allocations on MLS-Bench. Run preregistered allocation studies across agents, domains, and total budgets, estimating each inflection point only from earlier runs.

4. **What causes the human scaling advantage?** Historical trajectories show persistent improvement but do not isolate memory, collaboration, offline computation, or strategy change. A controlled contest could record interventions and artifacts, then compare humans and agents under matched feedback and resource rules.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the framework, experimental setup, and reported comparisons because the supplied text includes the main paper and methodological appendices.

Material missing or unresolved:

- Exact per-checkpoint numerical Elo values and confidence intervals for most curves are not tabulated.
- The full list of all 14 task-level results and per-task inflection points is absent.
- Detailed agent prompts are shown for some task families, but complete prompts and configurations for every headline task are not supplied.
- No direct ablation isolates context compaction, feedback use, strategy resetting, or other causes of slope decline.
- The supplied text reports a code URL but does not establish artifact completeness or independent reproducibility.
