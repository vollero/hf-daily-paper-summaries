# HazardAuditor: From Executable Threats to Safer Computer-Use Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.15134) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15134)


## One-sentence takeaway
HazardAuditor combines normalized execution traces with decision-level guard optimization, improving accuracy over BraveGuard by 4.0–16.5 percentage points across four balanced computer-use framework subsets, while showing mixed gains on external benchmarks (Table I; Appendix B).

## Problem and motivation
Agent safety depends on attempted actions, authorization, and interaction history: encountering malicious content is different from acting on it. The authors identify two gaps: executable safety platforms do not provide normalized training supervision across frameworks, and token-level guard training gives longer explanations disproportionate optimization weight (§§I–II).

## Approach and contributions
- **Execution-grounded supervision:** Adapters normalize Claude Code, Codex, Hermes, and OpenClaw logs into user messages, agent responses and optional reasoning, tool calls, and observations. Labels concern unauthorized or harmful attempts, including unsuccessful attempts, rather than risky content alone (§III-A).
- **Guard Policy Optimization (GuardPO):** After rationale-supervised fine-tuning, deterministic verdict rewards produce batch-centered sequence advantages. Clipped token updates separately average rationale and verdict losses, limiting direct dependence on explanation length (§III-C).
- **Guard and evaluation infrastructure:** An adapted Qwen3Guard-Gen-8B generates an explanation and binary verdict without an external inference-time judge. CUA-EXEC provides balanced, safety-case-held-out evaluation across the four frameworks (§§III-B, IV-A). Code, models, and artifacts are promised; their release is not established by the supplied text.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strongest gains occur on balanced execution traces | Each framework has 100 safe and 100 unsafe trajectories. HazardAuditor versus BraveGuard accuracy: Claude Code **94.0% vs 81.5%**; Codex **95.5% vs 91.5%**; Hermes **86.5% vs 77.0%**; OpenClaw **87.5% vs 71.0%**. | Table I; §IV-A |
| GuardPO improves the SFT initialization most on CUA-EXEC | Equal-framework mean accuracy rises **80.50%→90.88%**; mean macro-F1 **80.16%→90.85%**. | Table IV |
| AgentHazard transfer includes a trade-off | On GPT-5.5-generated trajectories, HazardAuditor accuracy is **84.03% vs BraveGuard’s 83.65%**, but unsafe-class F1 is **84.31% vs 89.22%**. | Table II; §IV-B |
| External benchmark gains are not uniform | ASSE-Safety accuracy/F1: **91.5%/91.5%**, versus SFT **90.9%/90.7%**. R-Judge accuracy/F1 decline to **89.6%/89.5%**, from **90.4%/90.4%**. ATBench accuracy improves **86.2%→88.4%**. | Table III |
| A smaller fixed execution test also shows heterogeneous effects | Across **228 trajectories**, pooled accuracy improves **76.75%→88.16%**; Codex macro-F1 declines **90.78%→88.86%**. | Appendix F; Table VI |
| Query transfer leaves coverage gaps | On a balanced **6,400-query** NSFA subset, accuracy is **88.89%**, unsafe recall **81.28%**, and resource-abuse recall **42.95%**. | Appendix D; Fig. 6 |
| Faster classification sacrifices quality | At batch size one on four A100 GPUs, MLP-64 reduces mean inference latency **3,138.10→303.99 ms**, while macro-F1 falls **88.12%→69.63%** versus the generative guard. | Appendix G; Table VII |

The results support improved classification of recorded trajectories, especially within the execution infrastructure. They do not establish that deploying the guard prevents harmful actions: intervention is delegated to an external controller, and no closed-loop prevention experiment is reported.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-acknowledged:** Improvements vary by dataset and framework; the small, imbalanced fixed test does not support precise framework-level population estimates. Query-domain coverage and fast-head calibration remain weaknesses (Appendices B, D, F–G).
- **Analyst inference—causal attribution:** SFT-versus-GuardPO comparisons test the complete optimization stage. No reported ablation isolates region normalization, verdict weighting, or reward design; execution-data benefits are also not isolated through matched data-removal experiments.
- **Analyst inference—scope of proof:** Proposition 1 establishes invariance when existing token losses are replicated. It does not prove invariance when newly generated longer rationales change token probabilities or reasoning.
- **Analyst inference—context loss:** Prefix-preserving truncation can omit later safety-relevant actions; performance by trajectory length or truncation status is not reported (Appendix A).
- **Reporting concerns:** §IV-B gives **80.7%** as the best prior guard’s worst-benchmark F1, whereas Appendix B/Table III show **82.5%** for AgentDoG-Llama3.1-8B. Some reported F1 values also appear inconsistent with the stated precision/recall convention—for example, HazardAuditor’s ATBench row warrants clarification (Table III).

## Open research questions
- **Which GuardPO component drives improvement?** The bundled comparison leaves attribution unresolved. Next experiment: matched-compute ablations of region normalization, verdict weighting, and reward penalties.
- **Does trajectory classification translate into prevention?** Runtime intervention outcomes are unreported. Next experiment: evaluate pre-action gating with harm completion, benign-task completion, false blocks, and latency.
- **How robust is the guard to unseen frameworks and long traces?** Framework-wide holdouts and truncation-stratified results are absent. Next experiment: leave-one-framework-out training plus evaluation by trace length and risk-event position.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The method and principal comparisons are clearly described, but reporting inconsistencies limit confidence in some metrics.

Material missing information includes training-set sizes and source proportions, annotation and rationale-generation quality controls, checkpoint-selection criteria, repeated-run uncertainty, the efficiency evaluation’s dataset composition, and confirmation that promised artifacts are available.
