# RSIAgent: Autonomous Exploration for Recursive Self-improvement in New Environments

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.15364) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15364)


## One-sentence takeaway

RSIAgent uses curriculum, actor, and verifier agents to build and freeze environment-specific memory through broad and targeted exploration, improving open-source digital agents without parameter updates, though several headline comparisons use unmatched protocols and partly retained baseline scores.

## Problem and motivation

Digital agents must operate in unfamiliar software whose interfaces, tools, conventions, and failures may be absent from pretrained knowledge. Existing adaptation often requires additional training data and human assistance, which the authors argue is costly and unsuitable for private or changing environments.

The identified gap is training-free adaptation that goes beyond storing successful trajectories: agents should autonomously discover, verify, and retain reusable relationships among actions, conditions, and consequences (Introduction, pp. 2–3).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Three-agent recursive loop:** A curriculum agent selects practice tasks, an actor executes code-based actions and maintains persistent memory, and an isolated verifier judges outcomes from environment evidence (Section 3.1, pp. 4–5).

2. **Broad-then-deep exploration:** Broad Recursive Self-exploration runs diverse projects in parallel; Deep Recursive Self-exploration sequentially targets hard cases, uncertainties, hidden constraints, and boundary conditions (Section 3.2, pp. 5–6).

3. **Actor-owned memory consolidation:** After verification, the originating actor distills and reconciles experience, including failures, into an unstructured memory bank. Parallel experiences are committed sequentially, and the final memory is frozen for evaluation (Appendix A.2–A.3, pp. 21–22).

4. **Evaluation across software and game tasks:** The authors test the framework on OSWorld 2.0, Agents’ Last Exam, and a 40-task GameCraft-Bench sample, including a four-task stage ablation and selected execution case studies (Sections 4.1–4.5, pp. 6–10).

The evidence supports the authors’ claim that environment exploration and reusable memory can improve their shared agent harness, with the four-task ablation suggesting that broad and deep stages are complementary. The artifact studies also connect some gains to concrete execution changes.

It does not establish a controlled superiority over frontier systems: external systems use different harnesses and budgets, aggregate RSI rows retain many baseline scores, and some records include retries, corrected grades, or protocol variants. It also does not isolate the effect of every agent role, memory operation, or exploration decision.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations**

- Exploration adds potentially substantial inference-time computation; outcomes depend on budgets, stopping policies, and memory quality (Section 7, p. 12).
- Model-based verifier errors can propagate into memory and later behavior (Sections 4.6 and 7, pp. 9–12).
- Environments may require different tools, verification signals, and exploration strategies, while the experiments do not isolate every component (Section 7, p. 12).
- Autonomous program execution and persistent memory introduce unintended-action, unauthorized-access, and privacy risks; evaluation used controlled environments with permitted access (Section 7, p. 12).

**Analyst inferences grounded in the text**

- **Headline comparison validity is limited:** published systems retain their own harnesses and execution budgets, so Table 1 is not a matched-protocol comparison (Appendix C.4, p. 28).
- **Aggregate gains are partly imputed:** only 41 of 82 OSWorld entries and 19 of 67 ALE entries are reported RSI results; remaining entries retain baseline scores (Appendix C.4, pp. 28–29).
- **The stage ablation is narrow and selected:** it uses four tasks chosen from a cohort showing improvement over recorded baselines, and its conditions have differing historical evaluation and stopping procedures (Appendix C.5, p. 29).
- **Compute-normalized efficiency is unresolved:** the paper specifies large per-run iteration and watchdog limits but reports no aggregate exploration cost or accuracy-versus-compute comparison (Table A3 and Appendix C.2, p. 27).
- **Some case-study attribution is confounded:** T065 received a current-date clarification, while T049/T044 comparisons use historical runs rather than matched seeds (Section E.2, p. 36; Table A6, p. 43).

## Open research questions

1. **Which component produces each gain?**  
   The paper does not fully isolate curriculum quality, verifier independence, reconciliation, or target conditioning. A factorial ablation could disable each component under identical seeds, models, task sets, and exploration budgets.

2. **How does performance scale with exploration cost?**  
   Exploration can exceed the nominal BRS budget and DRS has adaptive stopping, but aggregate compute is not reported. A next experiment could plot partial and binary scores against projects, tool calls, tokens, and wall time.

3. **Can memory remain reliable under noisy verification?**  
   The audits show accepted mistakes becoming reusable rules. An experiment could inject controlled verifier error rates and compare uncertainty-aware memory, multi-verifier consensus, and rollback mechanisms.

4. **Does learned memory transfer beyond a known target neighborhood?**  
   Exploration is guided by the target query, and several case studies revisit known targets. A stronger test would freeze memory before revealing held-out tasks and evaluate transfer across unseen applications or task families.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the method description, reported scores, and stated qualifications because the supplied text includes the main paper and detailed appendices.

- Complete per-task results and run-level variance for the main benchmark aggregates.
- Total token, tool-call, wall-time, and monetary cost of exploration and evaluation.
- Fully matched budgets, harnesses, and evaluator protocols for external model comparisons.
- Broad ablations of individual roles, reconciliation, verifier isolation, and target conditioning.
