# Thought without systematicity? Evaluating reasoning models on rule induction tasks

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.13948) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13948)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across four synthetic rule-induction families, the evaluated reasoning models often solve one task variant but fail structurally equivalent variants, with Boolean concept learning a notable exception and Gemini experiments showing that greater reasoning effort or greedy decoding does not consistently remove the gap (§5).

## Problem and motivation
A successful reasoning test is useful only insofar as it predicts performance beyond its particular presentation. The authors ask whether models preserve their ability across structurally equivalent tasks—a behavioral notion of **systematicity**. They identify a gap between demonstrating task success and establishing consistent competence across substitutions, permutations, and other transformations that preserve underlying structure (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Formal evaluation framework:** Define task isomorphisms and measure whether a model solves any, the fraction, or all sampled variants. An attempt succeeds only if the entire query set is correct; a variant passes when a majority of five attempts succeed (§3).
- **Procedural task adaptations:** Extend four established paradigms: grammar-based instruction learning, symbolic Raven’s matrices, integer-list program induction, and Boolean concept learning. Contributions include automatically constructed informative grammar demonstrations, a program-generating grammar, and Boolean support sets checked using an ideal observer (§4).
- **Empirical diagnosis:** Evaluate five proprietary models using five variants per task isomorphism, then examine reasoning effort, instructional hints, additional demonstrations, task difficulty, and sampling variance. A code repository is linked on page 1 (§5; Appendices A–B).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Large program-induction gap | Claude Opus 4.7 solves at least one variant in **91.67%** of task-variation sets, but all variants in **50.00%**: a **41.67-percentage-point** gap. Main evaluation uses high reasoning effort, five variants per isomorphism, and majority success over five attempts per variant. | §5.1; Fig. 1 |
| Systematicity depends on task and model | Almost all models solve all tested Boolean tasks and variants. For grammar induction, GPT-5.5 reportedly solves either every variant in a set or none, despite modest overall performance. | §5.1; Fig. 1 |
| More reasoning effort is not a consistent remedy | Gemini low/medium/high comparisons show modest performance improvements on program induction and symbolic Raven tasks while the systematicity gap largely persists; grammar results depend on the model. | §5.2; Fig. 6 |
| Sampling noise is only part of the explanation | Within-variant variance often dominates, but Gemini evaluations at temperature zero retain broadly comparable performance and systematicity gaps. | §5.3; Table 1; Fig. 7 |
| Clearer task information can help substantially | Program-component hints improve Gemini Flash and Pro performance and **almost eliminate** Pro’s gap. Increasing extra grammar demonstrations from **4 to 8**, beyond curriculum examples, improves any-variant success without resolving the gap. | Figs. 8–9; §5.1 |

These results support presentation-sensitive success on the tested synthetic tasks. They do not establish universal model incapacity, identify an internal reasoning mechanism, or demonstrate a human–model difference. Conversely, solving all sampled variants establishes consistency only over those tested transformations; failure on every variant does not demonstrate useful systematic competence (§3.5).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Controlled isomorphisms require synthetic tasks, and evaluation cost limits coverage to five variants with five attempts each (§6).
- **Author-stated:** Greedy decoding can retain numerical stochasticity. GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.7 do not support the tested zero-temperature reasoning setting, so this control covers only Gemini (§5.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Structural equivalence does not itself establish equal difficulty for a particular model. The framework explicitly assumes equally informative input semantics; familiarity effects remain relevant to interpreting the measured gaps (§3.5).
- **Analyst inference:** Instructional disclosure affects conclusions: Boolean tasks receive their generating grammar, while program induction receives operation descriptions only in the hint condition, where Pro’s gap nearly disappears (§§4.3–4.4; Fig. 9).
- **Analyst inference:** No human comparison or training intervention is reported. The findings diagnose behavior but do not establish its cause or a remedy. Variance estimates are also noisy, with negative between-variant estimates clipped to zero (Table 1).

## Open research questions
- **Does apparent systematicity survive broader coverage?** Five variants cannot exhaust the transformation space (§6). Next experiment: increase variant counts on fixed base tasks and report uncertainty in any/all success.
- **How much does explicit knowledge of the hypothesis space explain the gaps?** Hints nearly eliminate Gemini Pro’s program-induction gap, but their effects are not tested uniformly across families (Fig. 9). Next experiment: match rule disclosure across task families and model providers.
- **Which failures persist after more reliable estimation?** Sampling noise often dominates and greedy controls cover only Gemini (§5.3). Next experiment: collect more repeated attempts per variant and compare estimated success probabilities across transformations.
