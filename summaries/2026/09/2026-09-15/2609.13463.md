# Root-Cause Attribution Is a Search Problem: Continual Search for Long-Horizon Agent Failures

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.13463) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13463)


## One-sentence takeaway
Continual Search improves failure attribution on large agent execution records by prompting judges to inspect further evidence, but benefits are inconsistent on short traces and the experiments do not fully isolate search from other prompt changes.

## Problem and motivation
Root-cause attribution identifies where an agent execution first goes wrong and which component warrants intervention. Long execution records make this difficult: relevant evidence can be sparse, distant from the final failure, or located outside the main trajectory. The authors argue that single-turn judges stop at plausible diagnoses before adequately searching these records, while existing benchmarks underrepresent massive logs (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Continual Search:** Start with an agentic judge’s initial attribution, then continue the same session through turns 2–4 using benchmark-specific prompts to inspect unread artifacts or reconsider unresolved candidate failures. Passive Continuation starts from the same initial session but emphasizes reconsideration (§3.1; Appendix B.2).
- **MegaRCA-Mix:** Introduce 50 human-annotated failed Harbor Index trials, with a median evidence size of 286K tokens and access to trajectories, configurations, verifier outputs, logs, and sandbox artifacts (§3.2; Table 1).
- **Empirical analysis:** Compare continuation strategies, independent resampling, model tiers, reasoning effort, and evidence acquisition; additionally evaluate benchmark-defect detection (§§4–6). The interaction-centric taxonomy and four existing benchmarks are reused assets.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Large-record attribution improves | MegaRCA-Mix, 50 trials: GPT-5.5 set-based F1 rises from 0.349 single-turn to 0.498 at T4, versus 0.401 passive; Opus-4.8 rises from 0.478 to 0.620, versus 0.559 passive. | Tables 1–2; Appendix B.3 |
| Gains extend to other long-record settings | GPT-5.5 on TRAIL: Weighted F1 0.426 → 0.500, versus 0.451 passive. TELBench first-error accuracy: 0.121 → 0.212, versus 0.152 passive. | Table 2; Appendix B.3 |
| Independent resampling underperforms | TRAIL, Opus-4.8: Continual Search Weighted F1 0.539 at $2.58 mean cost per trajectory; four-sample self-consistency 0.430 at $6.52; judge panel 0.431 at $4.73. | Table 4 |
| Short traces show inconsistent benefits | GPT-5.5 exact step accuracy falls from 0.345 to 0.241 on AgentRx and 0.548 to 0.452 on Who&When. Opus-4.8 improves on AgentRx but declines on Who&When. | Table 3; Appendix B.3 |
| Search gains accompany greater coverage | Opus-4.8 on MegaRCA-Mix: artifact coverage increases from 70.8% at T1 to 97.4% at T4; passive plateaus at 77.0%. | Appendix A.1; Figure 9 |
| Advantage persists across repeated runs | Four Opus-4.8 runs on TRAIL: T4 Weighted F1 exceeds passive by 0.021–0.064; joint-accuracy gains are 0.037–0.055. | Appendix A.2; Table 5 |
| Benchmark auditing improves, with a recall tradeoff | Opus-4.8 on the same 50 MegaRCA-Mix trials: T3 F1 0.74 versus ABA’s 0.63; precision 0.93 versus 0.51, but recall 0.61 versus 0.83. Continual Search costs more. | §6; Figure 8 |

These results support the usefulness of the tested continuation procedures on larger evidence spaces. They do not establish that evidence acquisition alone causes the gains or that search universally supersedes model capability. Metrics also differ materially: TRAIL’s “Joint Accuracy” measures gold-pair recall without penalizing extra predictions, while its Weighted F1 evaluates categories rather than exact locations (Appendix B.3).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Hidden model reasoning limits attribution to observable records. Overlapping taxonomy categories and difficult human annotation may introduce scoring ambiguity and label noise (§7).
- **Analyst inference—confounded mechanism:** TELBench continuation broadens the error criteria beyond the initial baseline. MegaRCA-Mix’s second turn requests a subagent and independent rederivation, while later turns supply unread-artifact lists. These changes prevent cleanly attributing improvements to additional search alone (Appendix B.2).
- **Analyst inference—limited uncertainty assessment:** Repeated-run validation covers Opus-4.8 on TRAIL; equivalent checks across the other datasets and models, confidence intervals, and significance tests are not supplied (Appendix A.2).
- **Analyst inference—limited generalization:** AgentRx uses retail trajectories, and Who&When uses its Algorithm-Generated subset. Cross-benchmark differences therefore do not isolate execution length from task and prompt differences (§3.2; Table 1).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Table captions describe “four continuation turns,” whereas Appendix B.2 specifies one initial turn followed by turns 2–4. AgentRx’s initial prompt enumerates candidates without selecting a root cause, leaving its single-turn scoring conversion unclear.

## Open research questions
- **What independently drives the gains?** Search, rubric changes, stronger challenge language, and subagent use are bundled. Next experiment: factorial ablations holding the rubric and inference budget fixed.
- **When should continuation stop?** Short-trace regressions suggest that additional turns can become harmful (§4.3). Next experiment: compare fixed turns with stopping rules based on new evidence acquisition and attribution stability.
- **How reliable is the massive-log ground truth?** The authors acknowledge possible label noise but provide no agreement statistics (§7). Next experiment: independent annotation and adjudication, reporting agreement and performance under alternative acceptable labels.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly documents score improvements and prompts, but supports the proposed causal explanation less strongly.

Material missing information includes dataset/code release access, annotation and adjudication procedures, inter-annotator agreement, MegaRCA-Mix sampling and domain counts, phase-1 vote generation, taxonomy-to-cluster mapping, and uncertainty estimates beyond the TRAIL repeated-run study.
