# ModaLens: Measuring Image Sensitivity in Report-Conditioned Medical VLMs

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.15635) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15635)


## One-sentence takeaway

ModaLens finds that providing radiology reports substantially reduces medical VLMs’ answer sensitivity to image substitutions, but its report-derived labels cannot establish whether that reduced sensitivity causes visual errors (§4.1, §5).

## Problem and motivation

A report may already answer a clinical question, making ordinary accuracy insufficient to show whether a model uses the image. This matters when reports and images disagree, although trusting an accurate report may be appropriate. The authors identify a gap in prior image-dependence audits: explicitly manipulating report availability while holding the question and image substitution fixed (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Paired report-availability audit:** Compare answers for an original image and a substitute while fixing the question and report, then repeat without the report. The primary cohort contains 3,199 image pairs from 293 MIMIC-CXR patients, mostly paired within patient (§2, Table 1).
- **Complementary sensitivity measurements:** Measure answer flips and continuous yes/no margin changes; compare the original lowercase-token proxy with token-family and generated-answer scoring under an explicit answer instruction (§4.1, Table 3).
- **Robustness and mechanism tests:** Evaluate text-content controls, question and input ordering, additional model families, and report-token attention interventions. The distinctive contribution is the controlled report-availability comparison; the interventions support a limited pathway claim rather than identifying where report information resides (§§4.1, 4.5; Appendices F–N).
- **Reported reproducibility assets:** The authors state that code, exact prompts, and numerical run records are available in the ModaLens repository (abstract).

## Evidence and main results

All confidence intervals below are reported 95% patient-clustered bootstrap intervals.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Reports reduce generated-answer sensitivity | MedGemma-27B, explicit Yes/No instruction, 44,786 trials: flip rate **4.26% with report versus 20.94% without**; paired increase **16.7 percentage points [15.6, 17.7]** when removing the report. | §4.1, Table 3 |
| Excluding the mismatched composite question preserves the effect | On 41,587 finding-specific trials, instructed generated-answer flips are **4.42% versus 19.93%**; increase **15.5 points [14.5, 16.5]**. | §4.1, Table 22 |
| Scores move even without answer flips | Original lowercase-token protocol, 44,786 trials: mean absolute yes/no margin change **0.690 with report versus 2.307 without**; paired difference **1.62 [1.53, 1.70]** logit units. | Figure 2, Table 17 |
| The pooled effect is not universal across findings | Instructed generated answers, 3,199 cases per finding: atelectasis flips **6.81% with report versus 4.63% without**; support devices **11.03% versus 1.44%**, reversing the pooled direction. | Table 22 |
| Additional lineages reproduce the direction | Six finding-specific questions asked of every case, 19,194 trials/model, instructed generated answers: removing reports increases flips by **13.1 points [11.2, 15.1]** for Qwen3.5-9B, **17.5 [15.8, 19.3]** for Qwen3.5-27B, and **20.9 [19.9, 21.9]** for LLaVA-NeXT-Mistral-7B. | Appendix F, Table 13 |
| Report access affects sensitivity under intervention | MedGemma-27B, 400 one-question cases: cumulative report-token attention blocking from layer 0 raises flips from **1.50% to 7.75%**, versus **12.00%** with no report. Report-specific differences against both controls occur at tested starting layers within 0–20. | §4.5, Appendix N.2, Figure 7 |
| Report-label accuracy and image sensitivity measure different things | Original all-question modality ablation: pooled balanced accuracy is **0.770 report-only**, **0.717 full input**, **0.665 image-only**, and **0.500 question-only**, against report-derived labels. | §4.2, Table 20 |

The evidence establishes a protocol-dependent reduction in image sensitivity when reports are available. It does **not** establish that unchanged answers ignore all visual information, that additional flips improve correctness, or that report reliance causes clinical harm. The authors’ cross-model results support replication of report dependence, with image competence varying by question design.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Ground truth:** No independent expert image annotations were obtained. Manual labels also annotate reports; classifier-derived image labels are only a surrogate (§5, Appendices E, K).
- **Intervention specificity:** Whole-image swaps change more than the queried finding. Pairing does not match acquisition time, positioning, or view, and substitutes may preserve the relevant finding (§§2, 5).
- **Prompt and readout dependence:** The original lowercase tokens carry negligible probability mass. Explicit instructions strengthen the result but change the protocol; input ordering also changes effect magnitude (§5, Appendix I).
- **Narrow scope:** Every report-availability estimate uses one institution’s frontal chest radiographs and English reports. Other datasets lack paired reports (§5).
- **Mechanistic limits:** Early layer projections are poorly calibrated; attention blocking does not remove report information already copied elsewhere. Residual patching, steering, and probe transfer do not provide reliable localisation or correction (§§4.5–4.6, Appendix N).
- **Analyst assessment—reporting ambiguity:** §4.1 calls the finding-specific subset the “analysis of record,” while Appendix G retains all questions as the primary headline. Both estimates should therefore remain distinguishable. Bootstrap draw counts for the instructed pooled results also differ between Tables 3 and 22.

## Open research questions

1. **When does report reliance produce visually incorrect answers?** Report-derived labels cannot answer this. A next experiment could obtain blinded radiologist annotations of both images and distinguish appropriate report reliance from harmful disagreement (§5).
2. **Does the effect generalise beyond this setting?** Report availability is tested only on MIMIC-CXR. Repeat the paired audit across institutions, imaging modalities, and report languages with matched acquisition characteristics (§5).
3. **Can an intervention improve correctness rather than merely increase flips?** Attention blocking increases sensitivity, while steering and probe transfer are negative. Test interventions against independent image labels, measuring both recovered correct answers and newly introduced errors (§§4.5–4.6).
4. **Can models use genuinely historical reports appropriately?** Existing framing interventions do not enforce temporal consistency. Evaluate verified longitudinal pairs with explicit dates and independently annotated finding changes (Appendix D).

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** in the reported direction of the sensitivity effect within the evaluated protocols; substantially lower for clinical correctness and generalisation.

Material information unavailable includes independent image ground truth, clinical harm outcomes, report-conditioned evaluation outside MIMIC-CXR, and a question-only baseline for the cross-model six-question sweep. Repository artifacts were not inspected; some primary-analysis and bootstrap reporting details remain inconsistent.
