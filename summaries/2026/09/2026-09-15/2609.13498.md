# Building a Production Greek-English Speech Recognizer

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.13498) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13498)


## One-sentence takeaway
The authors report that specialist routing, recognizer combination, and decode-time safeguards improve production Greek-English ASR beyond their tested compact-model training mixtures, although proprietary evaluation and inconsistent gate reporting limit the strongest claims.

## Problem and motivation
The system targets Greek-English transcription in noisy calls, business meetings, and code-switched speech. The authors identify a production gap: improving Greek noisy-speech recognition can degrade English language identification, while clean-speech benchmarks miss overlap, silence hallucinations, and domain-specific failures (§§1, 3).

Production readiness combines transcription, language-identification, and non-speech-output requirements rather than optimizing WER alone (§3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Measured bilingual trade-off:** Controlled exposure experiments characterize incompatible Greek WER and English LID requirements for the tested compact model. The contribution is an empirical training-budget analysis, not a general impossibility theorem (§3).
- **Data and measurement engineering:** A six-stage filtering pipeline uses in-domain quality-score calibration, teacher agreement, alignment checks, language balancing, and Greek-aware normalization. Controlled ablations and independent measurement checks diagnose training-data and evaluation failures (§§4, 7).
- **Complementary recognizers:** ROVER combines hypotheses through word voting; the separate K1 system uses a gradient-boosted classifier to select one of two complete transcripts per clip. The authors explicitly avoid claiming architectural novelty for that classifier (§6).
- **Deployment findings:** Language/domain routing and decode-time silence, boilerplate, and code-switching fixes succeed where several retraining attempts fail. These are engineering findings from one program, not validated universal rules (§§5, 8–9).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Exposure budgets do not overlap in tested runs | Greek noisy-speech WER ≤25.25 requires approximately 1,500 dense-exposure steps; English LID ≥95% tolerates approximately 250, or 1,250 with rebalancing that leaves Greek WER near 26. Two runs, no repeated seeds. | §3; Figure 1; Appendix B |
| Calibration retains substantially more scored Greek data | Changing UTMOS threshold from 3.0 to 1.30 reduces discarded rows from 98.7% to 10.6% on one training-pool build. This measures retention, not downstream ASR improvement. | §4.2; Appendix B |
| Better labels improve compact-model gate coverage | Replacing labels with approximately 230 hours of consensus-labeled Greek call audio raises best coverage from six to seven of nine gates; Greek meeting and noisy-environment gates remain unmet. | §5.1; Figure 3 |
| Voting improves Greek noisy-speech and code-switch WER | Production bilingual model versus three-model vote: Greek noisy environment 26.63% → 21.56%; code-switched speech 59.74% → 23.84%. Unmodified vote still reports 1.0% non-speech hallucinations. | Table 3 |
| K1 improves the reported English composite | Cleaned seven-set average WER: K1 4.35%, far-field Qwen member 4.85%, Canary member 5.53%; same sets and forced-English leaderboard harness. No confidence intervals. | Table 4 |
| Live English routing improves WER point estimate | On 719 noisy English clips, Qwen alone scores 15.14% versus K1’s 14.21% (95% CI 13.01–15.57), with 24 concurrent clients on one B200. | §6.1 |
| Live Greek result has threshold uncertainty | Forced-Greek Qwen leg scores 25.88% WER (95% CI 24.44–27.19; 1,325 clips), nominally passing a ≤26 gate; L4 scores 24.63% on the same set. | §6.1 |
| Overlap voting yields a larger measured gain | On overlap-only spans from 18 AMI meetings, two-model voting reduces WER from 53.35% to 37.87%; bootstrap 95% CI for absolute reduction: 11.0–19.5 points. | §6.2; Figure 6; Appendix B |
| Audited decode-time filter suppresses boilerplate | Removing legitimate “ok”/“okay” from the suppression list reduces boilerplate hits from 26 to zero, with other metrics unchanged to two decimal places. | §5.2 |

The evidence supports complementary recognizer errors and useful data/decoding interventions in the tested settings. It does not establish universal training infeasibility or Greek overlap gains. The headline nine-gate success requires qualification: Table 3 shows nonzero hallucinations for both voting combinations, while §6 attributes full coverage to an additional, post-hoc silence rule.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** The trade-off uses two unrepeated runs at one scale/configuration. Capacity, auxiliary objectives, and alternative schedules remain unresolved (§§3, 11).
- **Author-stated:** Tables 3–4 lack confidence intervals; differences below roughly one WER point are not treated as established. This limits interpretation of K1’s small composite advantage over its Qwen member (§11).
- **Author-stated:** AMI pretraining exposure affects the Canary member; overlap tests cover English only, and streaming fusion latency was never measured (§6.3).
- **Author-stated:** No weights, code, training data, or proprietary evaluation sets are released (§10).
- **Analyst inference—reporting ambiguity:** The Greek noisy-speech gate changes from ≤25.25 to ≤26 without a clear reconciliation. The claim of a first single-model pass also sits uneasily with the lower L4 result reported alongside it (§§3, 6.1).
- **Analyst inference—system identity:** §11 describes a Whisper checkpoint inside K1, whereas §6.1 specifies Qwen plus Canary. Exact K1 composition therefore needs clarification.
- **Analyst inference—ablation interpretation:** Calling 20 boilerplate hits “within range” against a stated healthy baseline of 17–18 lacks an explicit tolerance or uncertainty estimate (§4.4).

## Open research questions
1. **Can training changes shift the bilingual frontier?** Only exposure quantity and mixture were controlled. Compare repeated-seed runs varying capacity, auxiliary LID loss, and schedule under fixed gates (§3).
2. **Does overlap fusion generalize to unseen Greek meetings?** Existing evidence is English-only and affected by AMI exposure. Evaluate on independently collected, annotated Greek overlap with audited training provenance (§6.3).
3. **What independently drives K1’s routing gains?** Feature-level ablations are absent. Compare the full arbiter with confidence-only selection, hard rules alone, and ROVER on held-out domains (§6.1).
4. **Does calibrated filtering improve transcription accuracy?** Retention changes are measured, but their isolated WER effect is not. Train matched-budget models using alternative filtering thresholds (§4.2).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports a detailed engineering account, but several headline interpretations remain uncertain.

Material missing information includes complete numerical gate thresholds, proprietary test composition, repeated-seed variability, exact model/checkpoint identities, full training and arbiter configurations, calibrated decoding thresholds, and interactive latency measurements. The conflicting ensemble, gate, and K1 descriptions also require clarification.
