# Pick Your Poison: Learning to Select Poison Sets for Stronger LLM Backdoor Attacks

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.15029) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15029)


## One-sentence takeaway
SAILS learns to rank entire poison sets and improves held-out backdoor attack success by approximately 21 percentage points over TRAK + representer at matched oracle budgets across three LLaMA mini benchmarks (Table 4).

## Problem and motivation
Backdoor evaluations commonly fix the trigger, target behavior, and poison count while sampling poisoned examples randomly. The authors argue that this overlooks substantial variation caused by **which examples are selected together**, potentially understating vulnerability (§1).

Selecting the strongest set requires combinatorial search, with each direct evaluation requiring model finetuning. Pointwise influence scores offer cheaper selection but can misestimate individual effects and miss redundancy or complementarity between examples (§2.2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Budgeted formulation:** The paper separates the number of injected examples from the number of permitted finetune-and-evaluate runs, formalizing poison selection as oracle-budgeted set optimization (§2.1).
- **SAILS:** A text-based scorer learns from evaluated poison sets, ranks many candidate sets, and sends a shortlist for direct evaluation. Audited results refine the scorer. The default DistilBERT scorer predicts validation triggered loss; final ASR is generally measured separately (§2.3; §3.1).
- **Theory:** Bounds separate proposal quality from shortlist retrieval error under exact oracle feedback. A counterexample shows that accurate singleton scores do not guarantee effective set selection; these are conditional bounds, not guarantees that practical search finds the global optimum (Appendix C).
- **Empirical evaluation:** Experiments compare influence methods, random search, and oracle-guided search, with additional tests of scale transfer, different model families, code generation, shopping agents, and API-only finetuning (§3).

The strongest evidence supports better selection within the tested finetuning setups, including matched-budget gains. It does not establish global worst-case vulnerability or effectiveness against active defenses. Cross-model evidence concerns loss, while some behavioral metrics only detect target text.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** No pretraining or RLHF poisoning evaluation; triggers and targets are fixed. Stealth constraints and active defenses are not tested (§5).
- **Author-stated cost:** Hundreds to thousands of finetuning runs remain expensive; transfer reduces but does not remove this requirement (§5).
- **Analyst inference—behavioral validity:** COMPLIANCE counts an affirmative target phrase, which does not establish substantive harmful assistance. WebShop counts a product-name substring anywhere in the conversation, which does not establish completed purchase (§3.1; Appendix F.5).
- **Analyst inference—selection and accounting:** Kimi’s detailed protocol describes additional evaluations after exhausting its stated budget and reports the best held-out ASR among final picks, conflicting with the general validation-only selection rule (Appendices E, E.2).
- **Analyst inference—attribution of gains:** Competitive mean-pooled Ridge results suggest that learning from oracle labels explains substantial gains; the evidence does not isolate nonlinear interaction modeling as the sole cause (Table 5; Appendix F.10).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Table 2 uses the strongest evaluated SAILS configuration per setting. Some pool sizes, clean-data counts, and compute-frontier budgets differ across sections, complicating exact reproduction (§3.5.3; Table 6 versus Table 1; Appendix F.9 versus E.2).
- **Training sensitivity:** Primary LLaMA experiments use deterministic full-batch training. SGD checks preserve method ordering but show substantial seed variability (Appendix F.9).

## Open research questions
1. **Do gains survive active defenses?** These are explicitly unevaluated (§5). A next experiment could compare optimized and random poisoning under identical filtering defenses and matched total compute.
2. **Do reported successes translate into completed behavior?** Current compliance and agent metrics rely on text matches. Re-evaluate with substantive-response assessment and confirmed purchase outcomes, using validation-only selection.
3. **How reliable is selection under stochastic training?** Existing SGD tests reuse sets selected under full-batch training (Appendix F.9). Compare repeated-seed oracle estimates with single-run selection at equal total compute.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** Matched-budget results support the central claim, but protocol inconsistencies weaken precise efficiency and generalization claims.

Material information unavailable or unresolved includes reconciled oracle accounting, uncertainty intervals for the main comparisons, exact validation/test construction where split descriptions conflict, verified behavioral outcomes beyond substring metrics, and performance against active defenses. Code release is reported by the paper; its contents were not independently inspected.
