# Learning to Solve Hard Problems in RL for LLMs by Never Giving Up

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-15.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.13443) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13443)


## One-sentence takeaway
Never Give Up adaptively resamples difficult prompts during asynchronous RL, improving compute-matched math performance and enabling complete solutions on a coding task where per-test GRPO stagnates.

## Problem and motivation
The authors identify a “Matthew Effect”: RL often improves problems that a model already handles more than initially difficult problems (§2). They argue that fixed-size sampling groups contribute by spending excessive compute on easy prompts, while hard prompts struggle to produce useful learning signals. Increasing samples per prompt can worsen this allocation under a fixed batch budget (§3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Empirical diagnosis:** Analyze difficulty-dependent RL gains across math, code completion, and agentic coding, then investigate training-batch composition as a possible mechanism (§§2–3).
- **Adaptive sampling:** NGU generates a small group of completions, filters fully correct groups, and probabilistically requeues unsuccessful prompts. Asynchronous execution allows freed capacity to serve other prompts (§4).
- **Stale-sample handling:** Exclude old completions from gradient updates while retaining their reward counts for the baseline; “anchoring the positives” rescales remaining negative advantages to preserve a centered update (§4).
- **Coding extension:** Examine difficulty within test suites and propose measuring difficulty after early prompt/harness adaptation. NGU then supports continued improvement under per-test rewards (§6).

Sequential sampling until success has prior precedent; the claimed distinction is its combination with probabilistic stopping, asynchronous compute allocation, and advantage handling (Appendix C.1).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| RL gains vary substantially with difficulty | Olmo math evaluation on AIME reports pass@1 gains of **0.03 on hard**, **0.36 on medium**, and **0.63 on easy** problems over its initial model. | Figure 1; §2 |
| Larger groups are not automatically better | On Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct/GSM8k Platinum, **K=4** outperforms **K=8,16,32** with total update batch size fixed at **256 completions**, including on the hardest subset. Three seeds. | §3; Figure 2 |
| NGU improves compute-matched contest math | Qwen3-4B-Base trained on a Deepscaler subset: NGU **p=0.875** achieves average pass@1 **26.5±0.6%**, versus **25.3±0.5%** for the strongest average GRPO setting, **K=32**. AIME/BRUMO evaluation; approximately **120 H100-hours per run**, three seeds. | §5; Table 2 |
| Hard-subset gains increase | In the same math setting, NGU **p=0.875** improves hard-subset pass@1 by **4.3±1.2 percentage points** over initialization, versus **2.5±1.1** for GRPO **K=32**. The subset contains **28 prompts** with no initial success in the difficulty-estimation samples. | §5; Figure 7; Table 3 |
| Implementation choices materially affect results | GSM8k extra-hard pass@1: GRPO **47.1±6.4%**; NGU **p=0.75**, asynchronous execution, positive anchoring **87.9±2.2%**; synchronous NGU **14.8±5.2%**. | Appendix C.1; Table 1 |
| NGU enables complete coding solutions | On Manufactoria, NGU improves hard-test and all-tests-pass performance while per-test GRPO plateaus. The caption reports no completely solved examples for the GRPO baseline; exact NGU endpoints are not tabulated. | §6; Figure 9 |
| NGU can recover from a plateaued checkpoint | Starting from the GRPO checkpoint after **3,000 steps**, per-test NGU approximately matches switching to all-tests reward when compared by compute time. | §6; Figure 10 |

These results support improved compute allocation in the tested settings, especially the explicitly compute-matched math experiment. They do not establish a universal causal explanation for uneven RL gains. NGU also leaves much smaller gains on hard than easy math problems (Table 3), and the reported seed variability does not establish statistical significance.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** NGU requires enough easy problems to free sampling capacity. Predominantly difficult datasets may favor larger initial groups because iterative sampling increases rollout staleness (Appendix B).
- **Author-stated:** Current training settings are contextual bandits; extension to multistep agentic environments remains future work (§§7–8).
- **Analyst inference:** Controlled NGU experiments cover math and one specialized coding benchmark. The agentic coding analysis demonstrates the motivation, not NGU’s effectiveness in that domain (§§2–6).
- **Analyst inference:** Difficulty estimates depend on sampling settings and adaptation stage. Initial and trained math models use different evaluation sampling parameters; Manufactoria difficulty is reassigned after adaptation (Appendix D.2; §6).
- **Reporting ambiguities:** GSM8k’s hyperparameter table conflicts with the main-text batch settings; Manufactoria’s table lists **1,500 steps** while the narrative reports **3,000**. Figure 8 also shifts between prompt- and test-difficulty descriptions. These complicate reproduction (Tables 4, 6; §6).
- **Analyst inference:** Recovery in Manufactoria supports retained learning ability in that experiment, but does not establish the authors’ broader suggestion that LLM RL avoids plasticity loss (§6).

## Open research questions
1. **When does NGU lose its compute advantage?** Appendix B predicts failure on predominantly hard data without measuring the boundary. Sweep easy/hard mixture proportions at fixed compute.
2. **Can an adaptive curriculum match NGU?** The curriculum comparison fixes difficulty estimates at initialization. Compare NGU with periodically refreshed difficulty estimates under equal total compute, including estimation costs.
3. **Does NGU transfer to multistep agents?** The paper leaves this setting untested. Evaluate adaptive resampling in an agentic environment while measuring task success, rollout cost, and staleness.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** Tables support the main math findings, but exact coding endpoints, coding seed variability, an unambiguous partial-reward stopping rule, consistent hyperparameter specifications, and quantified evaluation overlap are unavailable or unclear. The linked repository’s contents and any released checkpoints are not established by the supplied text.
