# From Production Traffic to Post-Training: Building a Self-Hosted LLM That Covers the Corporate Request Mix

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.01572) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01572)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors consolidate half of one enterprise’s self-hosted LLM traffic onto a 32B non-reasoning model by training separate GRPO experts for general dialogue, instruction following, and function calling, then combining them with sequential SLERP.

## Problem and motivation

Data-residency requirements can force enterprises to self-host models, while application teams’ continued use of different model generations fragments a finite GPU pool and raises serving costs. From a human-reviewed sample of 2,500 production responses, the authors identify instruction-following violations as 37.9% of failures; tool-equipped requests constitute about 12% of traffic, and public training distributions do not match the organization’s task mix (Section 1; Appendix A.1, Tables 9–10).

The claimed gap is a reproducible Russian-focused post-training method that simultaneously improves instruction execution, structured tool use, and production-distribution alignment without sacrificing broad capabilities or relying on fragile joint optimization (Sections 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Production-derived evaluation:** A template-aware sampler groups normalized prompts with locality-sensitive hashing, diversifies within templates, and allocates sampling budget proportional to the square root of template frequency. A task router then selects reference-based or rubric-guided LLM judging, calibrated against human annotations (Section 3).

2. **Modular post-training:** Starting from Qwen3-32B with a Cyrillic-dense tokenizer, the authors run one shared multi-domain SFT stage, fork three GRPO experts—general, instruction-following, and function-calling—and merge them using two-stage sequential SLERP (Section 4).

3. **Reward-specific failure corrections:** The general expert uses length and KL regularization; the instruction-following expert adds a prompt-specific reward-model threshold to prevent semantically empty, verifier-satisfying answers; the function-calling expert injects irrelevant-tool cases to counter over-calling (Section 4; Appendices C–E).

4. **Russian-native synthetic data:** The work constructs 43K verified instruction constraints and a function-calling corpus containing 1.2M English and 300K Russian turn-level samples, generated through schema-aware planning and multi-agent simulation (Section 4; Appendices D.1 and E.1).

5. **Released checkpoint and deployment evidence:** The authors release T-pro-it-2.1, trained with the same recipe but without internal data, and report deployment of the internal model across more than 200 services (Sections 1 and 5).

These results support the authors’ claim that modular specialization plus merging works well for this Qwen3-based Russian/English deployment and that its target-domain quality can exceed a substantially larger comparator. They do not establish that merging is universally superior to joint RL, that similar gains transfer to other model families or organizations, or that the internal Arena scores represent general public-world quality. Most reported model comparisons lack uncertainty estimates; Table 7 is a notable exception.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Evaluation covers only Russian and English, while the internal benchmarks derive from Russian-language traffic at one organization. Generalization to other languages and domains is unverified (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** Every experiment uses the Qwen3 family, so backbone independence is asserted as a methodological possibility rather than demonstrated (Limitations).
- **Author-stated:** Open-ended results depend on LLM judges. Although calibrated against annotators and checked across four judges, a new deployment would require recalibration (Limitations; Appendix H, Table 27).
- **Author-stated:** The model is restricted to non-reasoning operation; some rollbacks involved frontier-scale agentic needs beyond a dense 32B model (Sections 4–5).
- **Author-stated:** Function-calling tools used for synthetic dialogue generation are not executable; correctness depends on planner-generated references, and one Qwen model family generates and validates the dialogues (Appendix E.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Internal datasets and production cost accounting are not shown as independently reproducible assets; only the checkpoint is explicitly said to be released.
- **Analyst inference:** SLERP order and layer-wise coefficients are selected by validation grid search, and the authors report no rule for transferring them to a different expert set, creating selection and portability risks (Appendix G).
- **Analyst inference:** Some mixture settings are based on single runs or small subsets—for example, BFCL relevance has \(N=18\)—so adjacent-setting differences should not be interpreted as precise optima (Appendix E.2, Table 16).
- **Source limitation:** The supplied paper text is truncated, with 15,650 characters omitted from the middle. This analysis therefore cannot claim complete coverage of the benchmark construction, training details, or all ablations.

## Open research questions

1. **Does modular GRPO plus merging transfer beyond Qwen3?** The paper tests only one model family. A next experiment could repeat shared SFT, expert GRPO, joint GRPO, and sequential merging on two unrelated backbones under matched compute.

2. **Can loose semantic constraints improve without weakening strict verification?** The model remains below the larger baseline on loose in-house IF (0.58 vs. 0.66; Appendix H, Table 26). A factorial experiment could add rubric- or judge-based semantic rewards to the IF expert and measure strict accuracy, loose accuracy, usefulness, and reward hacking separately.

3. **Can function-calling rewards optimize full-session outcomes?** The authors note that per-turn exact-match gains translate only modestly to τ²’s end-to-end session success. A next experiment could compare per-turn Tool-N1 rewards with trajectory-level success rewards on matched dialogues (Appendix E.2).

4. **How stable are merge choices as new capabilities are added?** Merge order and coefficients are empirically selected for three experts. Adding a fourth expert and comparing fixed, retuned, and learned merging strategies would test extensibility and quantify validation-search cost (Appendix G).
