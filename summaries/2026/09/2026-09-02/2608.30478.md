# Agents in the Large: Perception-Centered Architecture for Persistent Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.30478) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30478)


## One-sentence takeaway

The paper proposes Pera, a conceptual architecture that extends task-bounded language agents with continual perception and lifecycle-level control so they can detect cross-task changes and adapt persistent service procedures.

## Problem and motivation

Most language-agent architectures organize memory, tools, reasoning, and actions around a bounded user task. The authors argue that this leaves an observability and control gap in long-lived settings: user needs, artifacts, constraints, environments, and reusable procedures can change between tasks, making stored context stale or previously successful procedures unsuitable (Sections 1 and 3.1–3.2).

Persistent assistance therefore requires more than longer execution or long-term memory. An agent must identify service-relevant changes across task boundaries, decide whether intervention is warranted, and maintain conditions that affect future tasks (Sections 2.2 and 3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Pera architecture:** A persistent-agent core couples lifecycle perception with a control plane. Sensors and signal processors interpret external and internal changes; the controller converts selected changes into executable lifecycle-task packages (Sections 4–4.2; Figures 3–4).
- **Two levels of work:** The framework distinguishes episodic tasks, which produce bounded user-facing outcomes, from lifecycle tasks, which establish, restore, or prepare persistent conditions for future service (Sections 3.3 and 4).
- **Lifecycle execution loop:** Pera organizes lifecycle decisions into processing, formulation, execution, and review, including acceptance, further revision, and rollback of persistent changes (Section 4.5).
- **Unified action taxonomy:** It identifies sensing, dispatching, instantiation, reasoning, retrieval, updating, and grounding as the relevant action space spanning lifecycle- and task-level control (Section 4.3; Figure 4).
- **Analytical synthesis:** The authors use Pera to classify prior architectures and illustrate its intended operation through a model-development reliability scenario (Section 5; Table 1 and Figure 5), then derive research directions for sensing, context learning, proactivity, lifecycle control, self-improvement, and evaluation (Section 6).

The evidence establishes a detailed conceptual vocabulary and shows how it can organize selected prior systems and reason about an illustrative scenario. It does **not** establish that implementing Pera improves task success, reliability, cost, adaptation speed, or safety: the paper reports no experiment, quantitative metric, ablation, or deployed implementation.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors’ stated gaps:** Adaptive sensing policies, structured signal representation, cross-signal aggregation, temporal validity of context, calibrated proactivity, lifecycle-aware dispatch, coordinated and safe self-improvement, and realistic persistent-service benchmarks remain underdeveloped (Section 6).
- **Authors’ stated operational risks:** Continuous signals may be noisy and computationally expensive; signal causes may be difficult to infer; persistent updates can affect many future tasks and therefore require validation, recovery, authorization, or rollback (Sections 4.1–4.2 and 6).
- **Analyst inference — no empirical validation:** Pera is supported by conceptual analysis and a constructed case study, so its feasibility and benefits relative to existing architectures remain unmeasured.
- **Analyst inference — comparison validity:** Table 1 compresses heterogeneous systems into a small set of categorical dimensions without an explicit selection protocol or sensitivity analysis; consequently, the claimed architectural distinctions may depend on the authors’ interpretation.
- **Analyst inference — reproducibility:** No implementation, executable specification, benchmark, dataset, or evaluation protocol for Pera is described in the supplied text.
- **Analyst inference — governance remains abstract:** Although authorization, review, regression testing, and rollback are discussed, the paper does not provide concrete policies or guarantees for deciding when autonomous lifecycle changes are acceptable.

## Open research questions

1. **Does lifecycle control measurably improve long-term service?** The paper offers no quantitative evaluation. A next experiment could compare a Pera-style agent with task-only and memory-only agents over an evolving multi-task environment, measuring task success, adaptation delay, maintenance cost, and regressions.

2. **How should sensing allocate limited resources?** Section 6 identifies frequency, granularity, source selection, and noisy signals as unresolved. An experiment could compare fixed, event-driven, and uncertainty-adaptive sensing under controlled signal rates and compute budgets.

3. **How can persistent updates be accepted safely?** Pera proposes completion criteria, review, recovery, and rollback but does not validate a policy. A next study could inject beneficial and harmful procedure updates and measure detection, false acceptance, rollback success, and downstream failures.

4. **How should persistent-agent benchmarks model evolving goals and context?** The authors state that realistic scalable evaluation is underdeveloped. A plausible experiment would construct multi-session task sequences with timed environment changes, stale information, delayed feedback, and emerging needs, then test cross-task observability and adaptation.
