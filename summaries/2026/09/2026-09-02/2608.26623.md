# AgentJudgeBench: A Multi-Difficulty Benchmark for Evaluating LLM Judges on Agentic Tool-Calling

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.26623) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26623)


## One-sentence takeaway

AgentJudgeBench finds that LLM judges become systematically less aligned with a programmatic reference as tool-calling workflows grow harder, with hard reference-free evaluation compressing all six judges into a 77–82% band that model scale alone does not overcome.

## Problem and motivation

LLM judges increasingly score agentic tool-calling, but their reliability in dependency-driven workflows has not been calibrated as carefully as in open-ended text evaluation. Here correctness requires selecting appropriate tools, supplying structurally valid arguments, respecting execution order, and covering the query—dimensions that can fail independently.

The authors identify three gaps in prior tool-calling evaluations: judge reliability is generally reduced to one aggregate agreement figure; task difficulty and workflow topology are not systematically varied; and the effect of providing versus withholding a ground-truth execution trace is not isolated (§1, §2.3, Appendix L).

## Approach and contributions

- **Controlled benchmark:** 3,808 synthetic BFCL-style records across 15 enterprise domains, six DAG topologies, and easy/medium/hard query variants, with programmatically verified traces (§3.1).
- **Paired reliability protocol:** Five generator models are evaluated by six main LLM judges both with and without ground truth, producing 321,648 unique generator–judge–difficulty–record tuples, each observed under both conditions (§4.1; Appendix D).
- **Decomposed scoring:** Judges and the deterministic reference independently score tool selection, parameter structure, sequence accuracy, and query coverage; alignment is based on distance between their four-component score vectors (§3.2, Eqs. 1–5).
- **Reliability analysis:** The study examines difficulty, topology, inter-judge agreement, ground-truth anchoring, temperature, chain-of-thought, prompt format, and a judge ensemble (§4.2).
- **Released assets:** The authors report releasing the dataset, generator outputs, judge verdicts, prompts, scoring pipeline, and a reproduction script (Abstract; Supplementary Material).

| Hard reference-free performance compresses across judges | On hard queries without GT, six judges fall in a 77–82% band across four of five generators. The six-judge ensemble reaches 79.5%, versus 79.8% for the best individual judge, GPT-5.4. | §4.2, RQ3; Tables 2 and 4 |

These results establish systematic alignment patterns relative primarily to the paper’s deterministic scoring rules, not an unrestricted measure of real-world correctness. The human study provides partial validation, but its single annotator, hard-only sample, and scorer disagreements mean that “best judge” conclusions depend materially on whether programmatic or human judgments are treated as authoritative.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:**

- The records are synthetic, single-shot, and stateless; domain drift and interactive multi-turn execution remain untested (Limitations; Appendix B).
- The human validation covers 120 hard records with one annotator, so it cannot estimate inter-annotator agreement or establish consensus correctness (Appendix G).
- The programmatic scorer penalizes some schema-valid extra keys, equivalent tools, defensible extra calls, and alternative orderings of independent branches. Absolute alignment therefore inherits a strict structural notion of correctness (Limitations; Appendices B, G, I).
- Only 58.1% of easy-to-medium rewrites received unanimous validation as strictly harder, versus 93.9% for medium-to-hard; medium is therefore not a fully calibrated difficulty tier (§3.1; Appendix N).
- Prompt-format and temperature ablations cover only two judge–generator pairings each; the format result already reverses on hard examples in the second pairing (Limitations).
- GPT-5.4 is a non-reproducible Azure snapshot and occupies generator, judge, and rewrite-meta-judge roles. Its +0.172 self-overcredit on sequence accuracy is correlational rather than causally isolated (Appendices B, O, P).
- The six main judges are general-purpose. Prometheus-2 is 20–30 pp lower and forms a separate agreement bloc, so broader claims about specialized judges are unsupported (Limitations; Appendix B).
- The study evaluates judges only as reporting mechanisms, not as reward or model-selection signals during training (Limitations; Appendix B).
- Generator parse failures and persistent null judge outputs are excluded. Overall generator-output success is 93.8%, with failures concentrated in smaller generators and long outputs near some judge context limits (Appendix D).

**Analyst inference:** Because the corpus intentionally overrepresents some DAG topologies and headline values use unweighted topology averages, the reported absolute alignment reflects the benchmark’s aggregation choice. The authors report that count weighting changes figures by at most 1.5 pp without changing rankings, which reduces but does not remove this external-validity concern (Appendices B, S).

## Open research questions

1. **Do the observed ceilings persist on real, interactive workflows?** Synthetic single-shot records cannot establish this. A next experiment could repeat the paired GT/no-GT protocol on replayable multi-turn enterprise traces with execution-based outcomes.

2. **How should correctness represent equivalent tools and valid alternative schedules?** The scorer disagrees with humans on redundant tools, extra schema-valid arguments, and interchangeable sibling branches. A schema-aware, partial-order scorer could be compared with the current exact-trace reference and multi-annotator judgments.

3. **Can judge training or calibration break reference-free verdict compression?** Scale, chain-of-thought, temperature, and a six-model ensemble do not overcome the hard-query ceiling here. A follow-up could train or calibrate judges specifically on difficult DAG errors and test them on held-out topologies and domains.

4. **What happens when judge scores become training rewards?** The paper only studies evaluation-time scoring. Controlled agent-training runs could compare programmatic, single-judge, ensemble, and human-corrected rewards while measuring whether anchoring and compressed feedback alter learned tool-use behavior.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the paper’s reported design, results, and stated qualifications because the supplied text includes the main paper and extensive appendices.

Material missing or unresolved:

- The numerical contents of several referenced tables are absent; only their surrounding summaries and captions are supplied.
- Full prompt bodies are not visible despite their appendix headings.
- The identities of every open-weight generator/judge configuration are not completely tabulated in the retrieved rendering.
- There is no multi-annotator human reference, real-trace evaluation, interactive execution study, or training-time experiment.
- Per-record repeated-run judge stochasticity is not directly measured.
- The precise causal mechanism behind GPT-5.4’s sequence-specific self-overcredit is not isolated.
