# UI-Venus-2 Technical Report

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.00028) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00028)


## One-sentence takeaway

UI-Venus-2 is a 9B/27B multimodal GUI-agent family that unifies mobile, web, and desktop control through large-scale task generation, offline reinforcement learning, action-aware multi-teacher distillation, and fine-grained verification, achieving strong results across most reported benchmarks but remaining weak on OSWorld 2.0 and vulnerable in safety-blind-spot settings.

## Problem and motivation

The paper addresses dependable automation of digital interfaces from natural-language instructions. Existing GUI agents can perform grounding and navigation, but the authors argue that practical deployment is limited by narrow environment coverage, generated tasks that may not reflect executable application functions, and coarse reward verification that can mistake partial progress for success or permit reward hacking (§1, pp. 2–3).

The targeted gap is therefore broader than next-action accuracy: agents must work across multilingual mobile apps, live websites, and desktop operating systems; construct valid tasks at scale; verify both trajectories and individual actions; recover across long workflows; and control consequential actions safely (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Unified cross-platform agent:** UI-Venus-2 observes screenshots, reasons about task state, executes structured GUI actions, and incorporates environmental feedback in a closed loop across mobile, web, and desktop environments (§2.1, p. 3; Table 7).

- **Three-stage training:** Qwen3.5-9B and Qwen3.6-27B initialize the two variants. Training combines large-scale multimodal mid-training, domain-specific offline RL, and Multi-teacher On-policy Distillation (MOPD) over grounding, CAPTCHA, mobile, web, and computer tasks (§§2.1–2.4; Figure 3).

- **Action-aware MOPD:** Distillation weights depend on whether the student’s action type and parameters are correct. Incorrect action types receive type-focused supervision with dependent parameters masked; teachers also receive the correct action type as a training-only hint (§2.4, pp. 5–6; Equation 1).

- **Function-grounded data pipeline:** A dynamically updated capability catalog drives task construction, validity filtering, real-environment rollout, and feedback from successful and failed executions (§3.1). The reported collection scope includes more than 100 Chinese and 70 English mobile apps, over 4,000 web domains in 19 categories, and dedicated desktop TaskSpecs (§1, p. 3; §3.2, pp. 7–8).

- **Synthetic grounding and CAPTCHA data:** Executable browser interfaces provide pixel-aligned grounding targets, while rule engines for 70 CAPTCHA types generate deterministic answers, geometry, valid actions, and solution traces (§3.2, p. 8).

- **Two-level verification:** Semantic Guided Verification classifies complete trajectories as completed, partial, infeasible, or failed using visual completion keypoints and VLM judgments. Sample-level verification labels prospective actions as correct, exploratory, ineffective, or incorrect (§3.3, pp. 8–10).

- **Released assets:** The authors claim to release full-parameter model weights, code, and evaluation infrastructure (§1, p. 3). The supplied text does not specify licenses or enumerate all released training data.

The evidence establishes broad benchmark competitiveness under the paper’s stated protocols, with particularly strong web, CAPTCHA, and standard mobile results. It also reveals important counterexamples to a blanket state-of-the-art claim: the 27B model trails another system on MobileWorld, OSWorld-Verified, ScreenSpot-Pro, and UI-Vision, while OSWorld 2.0 exact completion remains very low.

The results do not isolate which training component causes the gains. Several comparisons are not strictly controlled: baseline action scaffolds and tool settings may differ; some values were reproduced by the authors; live-site states vary; and DeskCraft uses an author-evaluated aggregate rather than official split-level reporting (Figure 1; Tables 1–3).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-acknowledged weaknesses:** Spatial-CAPTCHA-Bench remains below 50% even for the 27B model, with reference-frame changes, perspective taking, and multi-step spatial transformations identified as error sources (§4.2.5, p. 16). Open CaptchaWorld shows near parity with a general-purpose baseline (§4.2.5, p. 17).

- **Long-horizon desktop limitation:** OSWorld 2.0 Binary Accuracy is only 2.8% for the 27B model and 0.0% for the 9B model, substantially below GPT-5.5’s reported 13.0% (Table 2). This directly limits claims of dependable general-purpose computer use.

- **Residual safety exposure:** OSBlind ASR remains 47.9–48.8%, so approximately half of these benign-looking hazardous tasks still produce harmful behavior under the benchmark protocol (Table 6). The paper’s characterization of reliable blind-spot recognition is stronger than these residual rates alone support.

- **Analyst inference — missing causal ablations:** Although the introduction promises detailed ablations, none appear in the supplied complete text. There is no controlled decomposition of environment scaling, task construction, offline RL, structured action weighting, teacher hints, verification, or safety mechanisms.

- **Analyst inference — comparison validity:** OSWorld-Verified baselines may use different action scaffolds; OSWorld 2.0 baselines may use different tool settings; live-web evaluation depends on changing sites and automatic judges; and the DeskCraft aggregation differs from official reporting (Tables 2–3). These issues weaken strict model-to-model attribution.

- **Analyst inference — reproducibility gaps:** The text does not provide training-set sizes, compute budget, optimization hyperparameters, teacher identities/routing details, verification error rates, human-review volume, or contamination analyses. Licensing and availability of generated training data are also unclear.

- **Analyst inference — benchmark-selection risk:** MCA-Bench uses only 1,000 of 4,000 test examples, while some NextGen-CAPTCHAs and Open CaptchaWorld types are excluded because of annotation problems (Appendix C.2). The authors state that fixed selections were made before model evaluation, but results do not cover the full original benchmark distributions.

## Open research questions

1. **Which training component produces each capability gain?** The paper reports no component ablations. A next experiment could factorially remove action-aware weighting, teacher hints, synthetic grounding/CAPTCHA data, and each RL domain while holding compute constant.

2. **Can strong partial progress become reliable completion on long desktop workflows?** OSWorld 2.0 shows 13.2% Partial Score but only 2.8% Binary Accuracy for the 27B model. Error-stratified rollouts could test whether failures arise from planning, state tracking, grounding, recovery, or exhausting the 150-step budget.

3. **How accurate and robust are the proposed verifiers?** The paper describes SGV and sample-level labels but reports no verifier precision, recall, agreement, or reward-hacking stress test. A blinded human audit across completed, partial, infeasible, and failed trajectories would measure calibration and compare keypoint voting against simpler final-state judges.

4. **Can safety improve without suppressing legitimate task completion?** The paper reports ASR but no paired utility or over-refusal metric. A matched benign/hazardous task study could evaluate harmful-action prevention, false refusals, and task success under identical interface contexts.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the described architecture, benchmark values, and stated evaluation settings because the supplied text contains the complete report, tables, and appendix protocols.

Material missing information includes training-data counts and composition, compute and optimization details, teacher models and routing, verifier validation results, human-annotation procedures, contamination checks, component ablations, statistical uncertainty or multi-run variance for most results, safety–utility trade-offs, release licenses, and the exact scope of publicly available training data.
