# Does Imitation Learning Preserve Temporal Robustness in Dexterous Manipulation? An Expert-Learner Comparison Across Task Execution Speeds

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.01453) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01453)


## One-sentence takeaway
In the ParcelStow simulation benchmark, ACT-A matched the scripted expert’s 100% nominal success but fell to 53% versus the expert’s 84% at the fastest demonstrated speed, showing that nominal imitation success did not preserve the expert’s robustness to execution-speed changes (Table 1; Sec. 5.1).

## Problem and motivation
The paper asks whether an imitation learner preserves its expert’s task-success profile when execution speed changes. This matters in contact-rich manipulation because shorter schedules alter velocities, tracking demands, and contact transients even when geometry and goals remain fixed. The authors identify a gap in benchmarks that mainly test variation in scenes, objects, tasks, or instructions rather than comparing expert and learner at matched speeds—including speeds represented in demonstrations (Introduction, pp. 1–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Matched-speed evaluation:** The authors define expert and learner success curves and their signed difference using identical task conditions and matched initial-condition draws at each speed (Secs. 2–3).
- **ParcelStow benchmark:** They introduce a simulated parcel acquisition, reorientation, transfer, insertion, and release task whose post-acquisition phase durations scale with speed while geometry and success criteria remain fixed (Sec. 4.1; Fig. 2).
- **Expert–learner study:** A scripted expert is compared with three ACT initializations, Diffusion Policy, and DAgger over demonstrated and extrapolation speeds (Secs. 4.1.4–4.2).
- **Failure diagnostics:** Stage outcomes, joint-velocity utilization, hand–parcel motion, force closure, and a shared relative-motion handoff are used to localize failures (Sec. 4.3).
- **Released assets:** The authors report releasing the task, expert, baselines, demonstrations, episode records, evaluation tools, and reproduction scripts (Appendix J; Table 9).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Nominal parity does not persist at faster demonstrated execution. | Over 100 matched episodes per speed, expert and ACT-A both score 1.00 at \(r=1\), but score 0.84 and 0.53 at \(r=2\); the 0.31 paired difference has a 95% bootstrap interval of [0.18, 0.44]. | Sec. 5.1; Table 1; Fig. 4 |
| ACT-A degrades more than the expert. | From \(r=1\) to \(r=2\), success decreases by 47 percentage points for ACT-A and 16 for the expert. | Sec. 5.1; Table 1 |
| The pattern appears across ACT initializations, though only ACT-A has nominal parity. | ACT-B falls from 0.70 to 0.36 and ACT-C from 0.62 to 0.14 between \(r=1\) and \(r=2\), decreases of 34 and 48 points. | Sec. 5.1; Table 1 |
| ACT-A failures concentrate around insertion. | At \(r=2\), its 47 failures comprise 35 insertion misalignments, 10 jams, one excessive-slip failure, and one timeout; 94 episodes reach the pre-insertion pose, but only 53 finish successfully. | Sec. 5.2; Fig. 6a |
| Free-space parcel loss does not explain ACT-A’s gap under the imposed shared motion. | All 300 ACT-A acquisitions across \(r\in\{1,1.5,2\}\) retain the parcel through reorientation and transfer under the expert-relative hand motion. | Sec. 5.3; Fig. 5b |
| Acquisition state or later contact still matters under shared downstream motion. | At \(r=2\), continuing the same relative-motion controller through task completion succeeds after 95% of expert acquisitions but 64% of ACT-A acquisitions. | Sec. 5.3; Fig. 5b |
| Lack of force closure is associated with universal failure in the tested sample. | None of 414 acquisitions with \(\epsilon\leq0\) succeeds; the 95% Wilson upper bound is 0.009. The authors explicitly treat force closure as necessary but not sufficient in this setting. | Secs. 5.4–5.5 |
| More demonstrations improve nominal ACT-A performance but not comparably at \(r=2\). | With 50, 100, and 297 demonstrations, success is 0.27, 0.44, and 1.00 at \(r=1\), versus 0.46, 0.44, and 0.53 at \(r=2\), with 100 trials per cell. | Sec. 5.1; Appendix F, Table 7 |

The evidence establishes a substantial temporal-robustness gap between one nominally matched ACT policy and its expert in this controlled simulation, with consistent degradation across two other ACT initializations. It does not establish that action chunking causes the gap, that the same effect holds for other tasks or hardware, or that the handoff-state offsets alone cause insertion failures. Diffusion Policy and DAgger do not provide nominally matched comparisons.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Evaluation covers one simulated, contact-rich task, one fixed-base humanoid embodiment, state observations, and fixed friction; generalization to other architectures, tasks, sensing modalities, or hardware is untested (Sec. 5.5).
- **Author-stated:** Chunk horizon, temporal ensembling, and replanning frequency are not varied, so the gap cannot be attributed to ACT’s action chunking (Sec. 5.5).
- **Author-stated:** Recorded diagnostics omit joint torques, acceleration-dependent tracking error, hand-joint limits, and detailed contact dynamics (Sec. 5.2).
- **Author-stated:** The handoff experiment cannot separate initial handoff-state differences from later receptacle interactions or assess the learner’s replaced downstream controller (Secs. 4.3.2, 5.3).
- **Author-stated:** Demonstration scaling stops at 297 episodes, so the effect of larger datasets remains unknown (Sec. 5.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Only ACT-A provides a clean nominal-parity comparison; ACT-B, ACT-C, Diffusion Policy, and DAgger begin below expert performance, confounding their higher-speed expert gaps with baseline quality (Table 1).
- **Analyst inference:** Because execution speed is supplied directly in the learner observation, the results demonstrate failure despite access to speed information, but do not isolate whether representation, optimization, coverage, or control dynamics is responsible (Secs. 4.1.2, 5.5).

## Open research questions

1. **Which ACT design choice drives the speed sensitivity?** The paper does not vary chunk length, temporal ensembling, or replanning frequency. A factorial experiment over these parameters with matched nominal success could isolate their effects.
2. **Can broader demonstration coverage recover temporal robustness?** Only up to 297 demonstrations are tested, with no clear gain at \(r=2\). A learning-curve study using larger datasets and deliberately balanced speed/contact regimes could test whether the gap is data-limited.
3. **Do the findings transfer to physical robots and richer sensing?** All trials use fixed-friction simulation and state observations. Repeating matched-speed evaluations on hardware with camera or tactile inputs would test external validity.
4. **What causes the insertion failures?** Existing diagnostics cannot distinguish handoff error, tracking error, hand actuation, and receptacle contact. Instrumented experiments varying insertion clearance and measuring torques, tracking error, and contact impulses could separate these mechanisms.
