# SMELT: Scaling Laws for Compute-Matched MoE Looped Transformers

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.01343) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01343)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors report that repeating the middle half of a sparse MoE Transformer twice improves language-model scaling under closely matched FLOPs, non-embedding parameters, and KV cache, yielding fitted training-compute savings of 6.8–18.0% within the supported compute range.

## Problem and motivation

Prior looped-Transformer comparisons often hold parameter count fixed while allowing repeated layers to consume additional FLOPs and KV cache, making it unclear whether gains come from weight-sharing architecture or larger execution budgets. The authors seek a cleaner comparison that simultaneously matches per-token FLOPs, total non-embedding parameters, and KV-cache size, then tests whether looping remains advantageous across model scales rather than at one fixed size (Sections 1–2; Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Compute-matched architecture:** SMELT repeats the middle 50% of layers twice, scales residual updates within that block by \(1/2\), narrows hidden width to offset repeated computation, adds experts to restore parameter capacity, and adjusts attention-head geometry/GQA to approximately preserve KV cache (Sections 3.1–3.2; Figure 1; Table 2).
- **Empirical recipe search:** Ablations at the 200M active-parameter scale select middle-half rather than full-stack looping, greater effective depth without greater physical depth, and two rather than three or four visits (Sections 3.3–3.5; Tables 3–5).
- **Architecture-specific scaling laws:** Separate Chinchilla-style surfaces are fitted for SMELT and an unlooped MoE baseline across four scales and three sparse levels, enabling compute-optimal frontier comparisons (Sections 4.1–4.3).
- **Downstream and mechanistic analysis:** The paper evaluates DCLM Core, DCLM Completion, MMLU, domain and length effects, and in-context learning; probes suggest the second visit preserves retrieval coordinates while changing values, enlarging aligned residual updates, and reducing attention-sink mass (Sections 5–6).

The experiments establish consistent empirical advantages for this particular MoE looping recipe under close—but not exact—budget matching. The scaling-law evidence supports interpolation across the measured \(1.3\times10^{19}\)–\(2.2\times10^{21}\)-FLOP window; it does not validate distant extrapolation, dense-model generalization, wall-clock savings, or a causal link between attention-sink reduction and performance.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Design ablations were conducted only at the 200M active-parameter scale, so the optimal span and loop count may change at larger scales (Section 7).
- **Author-stated:** Only contiguous, fully weight-shared block recurrence was studied; adaptive depth, learned halting, adapters, and other sharing patterns remain untested under the same matching protocol (Section 7).
- **Author-stated:** Matching covers arithmetic FLOPs, parameters, and KV-cache size—not latency or wall-clock efficiency. Serial execution and sparse routing may introduce hardware overhead (Section 7).
- **Author-stated:** The mechanistic probes are descriptive rather than causal (Section 7).
- **Author-stated:** The complete proprietary architecture and training stack cannot be released, limiting reproducibility; the pretraining corpus is also described only as internal (Appendix A; Section 3.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Each ablation configuration was trained once, and reported DCLM standard errors reflect evaluation seeds rather than training-run variability, weakening certainty about small ablation differences (Section 3.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Budget parity is approximate: among sparse fit cells, maximum mismatches were 3.9% for FLOPs, 1.0% for parameters, and 3.6% for KV cache, although measured FLOPs were used in the fit (Appendix A).
- **Analyst inference:** Domain CE Gains share aggregate scaling exponents and fit only subset-specific intercepts, so the reported domain ranking depends on that structural assumption (Section 5.2).

## Open research questions

1. **Does the optimal loop span or count change with scale?** The search was confined to 200M active parameters. A next experiment could repeat the span/count factorial sweep at 600M and 1.6B under the same three matched budgets.
2. **Do fitted FLOP savings translate into real serving and training savings?** Wall-clock and hardware utilization were not matched. Benchmarking latency, throughput, energy, and memory traffic across multiple accelerator systems would test practical efficiency.
3. **Is attention-sink reduction causally responsible for the ICL gain?** The probes show correlation only. A controlled intervention that restores or suppresses sink attention on the second visit could measure changes in Dyck and broader few-shot performance.
4. **Can adaptive or partially untied recurrence improve the frontier further?** The paper evaluates only fixed two-pass, fully shared recurrence. Comparing learned halting, token-dependent depth, or per-visit adapters under identical FLOP/parameter/KV constraints would test this.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported experiments and their stated scope because the supplied text includes detailed methods, tables, figures, and appendices.

Material unavailable or incomplete includes the proprietary model implementation and training stack, the composition and release status of the internal corpus and validation sources, training-run variance across independent pretraining seeds, wall-clock and hardware-efficiency measurements, checkpoint/code availability, and evidence supporting scaling far beyond the fitted compute window.
