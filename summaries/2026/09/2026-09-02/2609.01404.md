# Evaluating Multimodal LLMs as Generalist Vision-Language-Action Agents for Drone Control: Commanding, Approaching, Tracking and Searching

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.01404) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01404)


## One-sentence takeaway

In simulated closed-loop drone control, small open MLLMs often navigate near targets but fail to follow the prompt-defined arrival protocol, while frontier-model performance remains task-dependent and well below saturation.

## Problem and motivation

The paper asks whether an off-the-shelf MLLM can serve as the decision-making component of a drone agent when its complete high-level action space is specified only through prompting. Existing systems often reduce the model’s responsibility to grounding or planning and delegate trajectory generation, verification, or termination to external modules; this obscures which embodied-control capabilities actually depend on the model (Introduction; Sections 2.1–2.2).

The authors target four gaps: evaluating approach, tracking, and out-of-view search under one closed-loop protocol; treating the MLLM backbone as the experimental variable; testing one model commanding multiple drones; and measuring performance down to potentially edge-deployable 2B models.

## Approach and contributions

- **DroneCATS-Agent:** A model-agnostic loop in which an MLLM observes an egocentric RGB frame, instruction, and five-action history, then emits exactly one prompt-defined action: `go`, `rotate`, `think`, or `finished`. A fixed geometric controller and flight stack execute the result (Sections 3.1–3.2; Figure 2).
- **Unified benchmark:** DroneCATS crosses initial target visibility with target motion to form approaching, searching, tracking, and search-and-track tasks, all scored using the model’s own arrival declarations (Sections 4.2–4.3; Figure 3).
- **Fleet-command evaluation:** One context receives four ordered camera views and emits one command per drone; success pools the fleet’s declarations while requiring identification among close-range look-alikes (Section 4.4).
- **Model-centered analysis:** Nine model configurations are compared across scale and specialization, with a three-run variance audit and a failure taxonomy (Sections 4.6 and 5.4–5.5).
- **Empirical declaration finding:** The authors argue that small-model failures arise substantially from premature or absent termination declarations rather than navigation alone (Section 5.2; Tables 3–4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| The easiest task remains unsolved. | Best approaching SR was 65%: 13/20 episodes for Gemini 3.7 Flash, using declaration-based success with \(\delta=5\) m. | Table 3; Sections 5.1 and 6 |
| Initial invisibility materially reduced performance. | For Gemini 3.7 Flash, approaching fell from 65% to 40% when the stationary target began out of view; no searching score exceeded 40%. | Table 3; Section 5.1 |
| Performance did not transfer consistently across tasks. | GPT-5 scored 60% on approaching but 15% on tracking; Gemini 3.7 Flash scored 65% and 80%, respectively, over 20 episodes per cell. | Table 3; Section 5.1 |
| Small-model navigation and declared success diverged sharply. | Qwen3.5-9B reached within 5 m in 90% of 20 approaching episodes but achieved 35% SR; its decisive declarations averaged 0.63 of starting distance. | Tables 3–4; Section 5.2; Figure 7 |
| Two 2B models exhibited opposite termination failures. | Qwen3.5-2B declared in 25% of approaching episodes at a mean declared/start-distance ratio of 1.28 and scored 0% SR; Cosmos3-Edge-2B reached within 5 m in 25% but never declared and scored 0%. | Table 4; Section 5.2 |
| Fleet commanding strongly separated models. | At \(N=4\), Gemini 3.7 Flash reached 80%, declared 90%, and succeeded 80%; GPT-5 reached 45%, declared 35%, and succeeded 20%, across 20 commanding episodes. | Table 5; Section 5.3 |
| Small Qwen models frequently copied commands across distinct views. | Among steps issuing `go` for all four drones, Qwen3.5-9B repeated an identical point in 70% and Qwen3.5-27B in 58%; selected frontier models were at 0–1%. | Section 5.3; Figure 11 |
| Observational evidence did not show a benefit from `think`. | Across 739 matched occurrences, the next three steps improved closing by only +0.03 m/step; three-step reacquisition was 13.5% after `think` versus 13.4% after ordinary actions. This was explicitly not an ablation. | Section 5.6 |
| Per-cell model differences have substantial sampling uncertainty. | Three Gemini 3.7 Flash runs produced per-cell standard deviations of 9–13 percentage points and totals of \(43.7\pm7.8\) successes out of 80. | Section 5.4; Table 6 |

The evidence establishes that, under this particular prompted action interface and simulator configuration, successful physical proximity does not guarantee declared task completion, especially for small open models. It also shows meaningful variation across tasks and fleet commanding. It does **not** isolate model size as a causal factor, prove that protocol adherence is the sole failure source, establish real-world flight performance, or provide a controlled estimate of the benefit of `think`.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** All experiments are in simulation; transfer to real hardware remains unmeasured (Section 6).
- **Author-stated:** Hierarchical commanding with separate per-drone agents was not evaluated (Sections 4.4 and 6).
- **Author-stated:** Each result cell has only 20 binary episodes, so scores change in five-point increments and neighboring model rankings can fall within 9–13 points of run-to-run variation (Sections 5.3–5.4).
- **Author-stated:** The one-drone and commanding columns are not matched controls: commanding also introduces close-range inscription disambiguation and replaces the campus map with Blocks (Section 5.3; Table 5).
- **Author-stated:** Throughput affects performance, particularly for moving targets; control rate must therefore be pinned and reported (Section 5.4).
- **Author-stated:** The `think` analysis is observational rather than an intervention or ablation (Section 5.6).
- **Author-stated:** Some episodes suffered simulator spawn faults; affected episodes were retried once, and repeated failures remained in the measurements (Appendix D).
- **Analyst inference:** Comparing API and locally served models may retain provider- and serving-path confounds, despite common prompting; decoding constraints also differed because open models used a JSON schema while API models decoded freely (Appendix A.3–A.4).
- **Analyst inference:** The protocol-adherence interpretation is plausible but not causally isolated because the study does not compare prompt-only termination against trained termination heads or otherwise selectively repair declaration behavior.
- **Analyst inference:** The five-action history mixes pixel coordinates with the model’s normalized coordinate convention, causing rescaling when coordinates are copied; this implementation detail could affect repeated-action behavior (Appendix A.2).

## Open research questions

1. **Can targeted training repair termination without harming navigation?** The paper observes a large SR–OSR gap but does not train the action protocol. A next experiment could fine-tune only action selection or arrival prediction and compare SR, OSR, and premature-declaration rates against the prompt-only agent.

2. **Does the ranking survive real flight and variable control rates?** Evaluation is simulated, and the authors observed sensitivity to host-induced slowing. Hardware trials could sweep fixed and jittered control frequencies while holding scenes, models, and high-level commands constant.

3. **What deliberation mechanism helps under a physical latency budget?** The observational `think` analysis showed no measurable advantage, but it was not randomized. A controlled ablation could compare no deliberation, prompted one-step deliberation, and fixed token budgets at matched wall-clock control rates.

4. **Does hierarchical fleet control outperform one-context commanding?** The benchmark evaluates one MLLM directly issuing commands to four drones but leaves commander-to-sub-agent delegation untested. A matched experiment could compare centralized multi-view control with a commander plus per-drone agents on identical episodes.
