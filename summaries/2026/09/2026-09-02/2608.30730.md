# E-Commerce Bench: Evaluating LLM Agents on Long-Horizon Autonomous Business Operation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-02.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.30730) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30730)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors introduce a deterministic, real-data-grounded benchmark for year-long e-commerce agents and find large, uneven capability gaps: profitability does not reliably predict negotiation, fraud avoidance, execution, efficiency, solvency, or learning.

## Problem and motivation

Existing long-horizon agent benchmarks mostly assess terminal task completion or simplify continuing business operation. The authors argue that prior business benchmarks either use stochastic LLM-generated counterparts, weakening reproducibility, or omit supplier negotiation and adversarial dynamics. E-Commerce Bench targets sustained planning and adaptation across thousands of consequential actions in a changing market, where procurement, inventory, fulfillment, returns, and delayed cash flow interact over time (§1; Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Continuing business benchmark:** Agents operate up to four stores over a simulated 365-day year using 18 tools, starting with ¥100,000 and maximizing final assets. The environment contains 6,886 products, 576 suppliers, 12 store types, dynamic events, promotions, and hidden market parameters (§3.1–§3.6; Figure 3).

2. **Controlled market simulation:** Customer demand and returns follow a fixed, seeded economy model. Supplier prices, concessions, and decisions come from a deterministic negotiation kernel; an LLM only renders those decisions as dialogue (§3.4–§3.5).

3. **Repeated and adversarial negotiation:** The benchmark supports multi-round, cross-supplier, repeated bargaining and includes 152 fraudulent suppliers with five scam overlays (§3.5.3–§3.5.4).

4. **Multidimensional evaluation:** Eighteen models are evaluated over five episodes each using final assets plus six capability dimensions: negotiation, fraud avoidance, solvency, operational efficiency, execution, and learning (§3.7; Table 2).

These results establish behavior within the benchmark’s fixed simulated economy, not general real-world business competence. Deterministic economics strengthen attribution to agent actions, but policy sampling, supplier-renderer wording, and delivery-day variation remain. Five episodes per model provide limited precision, especially for rare failures such as fraud and bankruptcy (§3.5.2; §4.1).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Five episodes per model are a small sample; means are therefore accompanied by standard deviations (§4.1).
- **Author-stated:** Supplier prose is sampled, and advertised delivery days are drawn outside the episode seed. Fraud evaluation consequently contains renderer and policy sampling noise (§3.5.2).
- **Author-stated:** CSE+ and related negotiation metrics omit abandoned sessions, weight sessions rather than quantities, and are most comparable when bargaining-range portfolios are similar (§3.5.5).
- **Author-stated:** AnchorRatio assumes comparable cycle difficulty, ignores order size, excludes fraudulent suppliers and early-ended episodes, and measures an outcome without identifying its cause (§3.5.5).
- **Author-stated validity issue:** Supplier email identifiers leak the fraud label; the text does not establish whether evaluated agents exploited that signal (Appendix B.5).
- **Author-stated validity issue:** Qualitative return labels understate numeric return bands for 20 of 60 categories, potentially misleading agents (Appendix B.5).
- **Analyst inference:** Results from one fixed calendar, marketplace design, tool interface, and economy may not transfer to other regions, business models, or real counterparties.
- **Analyst inference:** The study compares complete agent-model systems under provider defaults; the supplied text reports no controlled ablation isolating base-model ability, memory strategy, renderer choice, or context eviction.
- **Coverage limitation:** The supplied source is truncated, with 65,034 characters omitted from the middle. Appendices containing some formulas, per-dimension tables, implementation details, and prompts are therefore only partially available.

## Open research questions

1. **How much do context eviction and memory behavior cause declining negotiation performance?** The paper identifies eviction, sparse memory use, and lack of within-episode gradients as possible mechanisms but does not separate them (§4.3.6). A next experiment could compare full-history, fixed-summary, mandatory-memory, and current-eviction conditions on identical trajectories.

2. **How robust are rankings to renderer and delivery-delay variation?** These are the remaining non-deterministic components (§3.5.2). A factorial experiment could replay fixed agent actions across multiple renderers, phrasings, and delivery draws.

3. **Would models generalize to unseen economies?** Evaluation uses one fixed world and supplier roster (§4.1). A next experiment could create held-out calendars, supplier assignments, elasticity regimes, and store economics while preserving the benchmark’s deterministic structure.

4. **Can explicit fraud attribution improve screening without reducing profitable exploration?** Fraud evidence is delayed and sometimes pooled across stock (§3.5.4). A controlled intervention could vary traceability and feedback delay, measuring BadSpend%, profit, and honest-supplier coverage together.
