# PARSER: Read in Parallel, Reason in Depth for Long-Context LLM Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.06702) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.06702)


## One-sentence takeaway
PARSER combines parallel chunk readers with an RL-trained lead agent to improve long-context multi-hop QA accuracy and evidence-order robustness, with measured speedups that depend on hardware and request concurrency (Tables 1–2; Figure 2).

## Problem and motivation
Sequential memory agents read documents chunk by chunk, repeatedly compressing earlier evidence before its downstream relevance is known. The authors identify two consequences: sensitivity to evidence placement and a sequential processing chain that grows with document length. Existing parallel readers often use fixed queries or hand-specified coordination, motivating learned, iterative query refinement (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Iterative parallel reading:** Every chunk has a frozen subagent. A lead agent broadcasts focused queries, gathers findings while dropping abstentions, and formulates follow-up queries. It receives extracted evidence but never raw chunks; cross-chunk reasoning happens across rounds (§3.2).
- **Lead-only learning:** GRPO optimizes the lead agent using a binary answer reward; reader models remain frozen and observation tokens are excluded from policy gradients. The default readers use Qwen3.5-4B with either a 4B or 9B lead (§§3.3–4.1).
- **Mechanism-focused evaluation:** Controlled evidence-position, order, and distance tests supplement accuracy, timing, RL, reader-size, and chunk-size experiments (§5; Appendix B).

The authors position the contribution as a long-context instantiation of existing orchestrator–worker and lead-only training designs, rather than inventing those general patterns (§2.2).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Stronger HotpotQA results | Across 7K–896K contexts, 4B PARSER averages **84.57% Sub_EM**, versus **78.91%** for ReMemR1; at 896K, **85.42% versus 73.44%**. | Table 1(a) |
| Larger lead improves average performance | On the same HotpotQA grid, 9B PARSER averages **86.79%**, versus **80.05%** for MemAgent and **80.47%** for DeepSeek-V4-Pro think-max. | Table 1(a) |
| Transfer to another QA benchmark | On 2WikiMultiHopQA, average Sub_EM is **87.04%/88.48%** for 4B/9B PARSER, versus **77.41%/79.27%** for corresponding ReMemR1 models; DeepSeek think-max scores **88.18%**. | Table 1(b) |
| Robustness to reversed evidence order | On 512 selected 2WikiMultiHopQA questions in 894K-token documents, logical→reversed accuracy is **96.4→97.1%** for PARSER, **66.5→23.3%** for MemAgent, and **75.5→61.7%** for ReMemR1. | §5.1; Figure 2(b) |
| Lower amortized inference time | At 896K on HotpotQA, PARSER versus MemAgent takes **78.22 versus 876.20 seconds/sample** at concurrency 1, and **58.86 versus 101.94** at concurrency 16. PARSER uses an H100 plus RTX3090; MemAgent uses one H100. | §5.2; Table 2 |
| RL contributes substantially | For 4B PARSER, average Sub_EM without→with RL rises **74.42→84.57%** on HotpotQA and **82.85→87.04%** on 2WikiMultiHopQA across the length grid. | Appendix B.1; Table 7 |
| Chunking materially helps | With Qwen3.5-4B, HotpotQA average Sub_EM is **84.57%** using 4,096-token chunks versus **73.76%** with one full-document reader; at 896K, **85.42% versus 53.13%**. | §5.3; Table 4 |

The evidence supports improved accuracy and reduced sensitivity under the tested perturbations. It does not establish universal position invariance, constant wall-clock latency, or superiority on every setting: some shorter 2WikiMultiHopQA settings favor full-context models. The headline average gains against sequential agents refer to HotpotQA, despite broader wording in the conclusion.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-identified failure:** Isolated readers can confuse similarly named entities, and the lead may accept a misleading finding despite contradictory evidence. It cannot directly inspect the underlying source context (Appendix F.2; Figure 9).
- **Author-identified resource constraint:** Repeated reading can require repeated prefill when chunk caches are evicted, reducing the speed advantage at higher concurrency (Appendix A).
- **Analyst inference—limited generalization:** Main evaluation reuses only 128 questions per benchmark across eight synthetic context lengths. Longer contexts primarily add distractors; they do not establish performance on increasingly complex reasoning or natural long documents (Appendix C; Table 8).
- **Analyst inference—comparison limits:** Timing uses different hardware allocations. Training also differs in rollout groups, steps, and resources, so results are not a matched-compute comparison (§5.2; Appendices D.3, E.4).
- **Analyst inference—measurement uncertainty:** Results average three runs without reported uncertainty intervals. The bidirectional substring metric can accept partial answers, and checkpoint selection uses “best in-distribution overall performance” without clearly identifying a separate selection split (§4.2; Appendix E.6).
- **Analyst inference—causal attribution:** No matched iterative-versus-single-round ablation or frozen-versus-trained reader comparison isolates those design choices. Thus, the proposed explanation for improved transfer remains an author interpretation (§4.3; Tables 3–7).

## Open research questions
1. **Can source verification prevent entity confusion?** Appendix F.2 shows a failure but does not quantify its frequency. Compare default aggregation with source-context inspection on an entity-ambiguity test set.
2. **Does stability survive greater reasoning depth or dense evidence?** Current length scaling reuses questions and adds distractors. Independently vary hop count and evidence density, measuring accuracy, rounds, and gathered-context size.
3. **What speed advantage remains under equal resources?** Hardware differs and cache retention affects cost. Compare methods under matched GPU budgets while measuring request latency, throughput, and cache misses.
4. **How much does adaptive querying contribute?** Existing RL and chunking ablations do not isolate query adaptation. Compare fixed-query, single-round, and adaptive workflows with matched reader-call budgets.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text provides detailed methods, results, prompts, and failure analysis, but broader claims exceed the demonstrated scope.
