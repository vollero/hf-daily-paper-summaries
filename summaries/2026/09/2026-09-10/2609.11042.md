# T1: Terminal Agent Reinforcement Learning for Long-Horizon Tasks

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.11042) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11042)


## One-sentence takeaway
T1’s combined terminal-agent training recipe raises Terminal-Bench 2.1 resolution from 49.4% after supervised fine-tuning to 64.0%, but the experiments do not isolate the effects of reward design, data filtering, and training stabilization (Table 1; §10).

## Problem and motivation
Long-horizon terminal agents must maintain state, recover from failed commands, and produce machine states that pass executable checks. The authors identify two training obstacles: sparse success rewards hide partial progress, while token reconstruction and inconsistent MoE expert selection make training diverge from the behavior that generated a trajectory (§§1, 4–5).

Their stated gap is stable reinforcement learning for large sparse terminal agents, beyond the smaller policies and binary-outcome training described in their related-work survey (§8).

## Approach and contributions
- **Integrated agent training:** T1 post-trains Qwen3.5-122B-A10B from an SFT checkpoint using asynchronous PPO, a separate full-size critic, critic warm-starting, and executable sandbox feedback. Despite “purely RL” wording elsewhere, the final pipeline explicitly includes SFT (§§4.5, 6.2).
- **Multi-turn alignment:** TITO preserves sampled token identifiers during trajectory assembly; routing replay preserves sampled expert selections while retaining trainable gating weights. These principles have prior sources; this paper contributes their terminal-agent integration, alignment checks, capture path, and failure handling (§§4.2–4.3, 8).
- **Graded execution reward and audited data:** T1-15k selects 15,000 recursively synthesized tasks through quality auditing. Reward is passing-assertion count divided by a fixed 20, with binary fallback when assertion reports are unavailable. “Dense” means finer score resolution: feedback remains a terminal scalar, propagated through the critic (§§3, 5.2–5.3).
- **Operational recipe:** Memory modeling, recurrent context parallelism, sandbox admission control, and weight-publication barriers support the large actor–critic training loop (§7).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

| Alignment diagnostics improve | Joint TITO and routing replay reduce mean absolute log-probability discrepancy from 0.021 to 0.013 on the same model stack; a 1,402-sample audit finds zero token drift within loss-bearing positions. | §§4.2–4.4; Fig. 6 |
| Early GRPO comparison stalls | Under the stated same harness, reward, and device budget, GRPO resolves 51.7% of Terminal-Bench 2.1 tasks at both evaluated checkpoints, steps 10 and 20. | §9.4; Fig. 16 |

These results support improvement from the **complete recipe** across three terminal suites. They do not establish independent causal effects for its components, statistical superiority for close leaderboard differences, or generalization beyond terminal-agent settings. The alignment diagnostic also includes legitimate policy-version movement, so it is not a pure measurement of implementation error (Appendix B.2).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated limitations:** Binary-versus-dense comparisons simultaneously change data, initialization, and routing replay. Verifiers lack runtime tamper protection. Completion-based rollout selection drops difficult long trials, and training coverage is uneven (§10).
- **Residual conditioning mismatch:** Loss tokens are preserved, but later-turn training history can differ from inference history; the authors report affected retokenized history covering 2.6% of audited tokens (§10).
- **Analyst assessment—protocol ambiguity:** §6.1 specifies a 60-turn evaluation ceiling, whereas §6.4 reports 94.4 average turns and a 500-turn ceiling. Appendix C additionally uses unequal model budgets. These settings are not fully reconciled.
- **Analyst assessment—reporting inconsistencies:** Relative-gain annotations disagree across sections; stated production learning rates imply a different critic/actor ratio from the introductory claim. The scope statement says one benchmark despite reporting three suites (§§1, 4.5, 6.4, 10).
- **Analyst assessment—limited attribution:** Small category subsets, repeated checkpoint evaluation, and missing uncertainty estimates limit strong ranking and behavioral claims. Claimed training/evaluation disjointness supports the intended transfer setting, but detailed overlap-audit evidence is unavailable (§§3, 6).

## Open research questions
1. **How much does assertion-count reward contribute independently?** Campaigns change several factors together. Run matched binary, pass-ratio, and assertion-count PPO experiments with identical data, initialization, routing, and compute (§10).
2. **Can long-task retention improve performance efficiently?** Oversampling cancels slow trajectories. Compare cancellation with partial-rollout continuation under equal compute, measuring task-family retention and held-out performance (§§9.3, 10).
3. **Do gains survive strict verifier integrity?** Auditing is currently the only task-exploitability defense. Repeat training with protected verifiers and independent final-state checks (§10).
4. **Does stronger task completion require more interaction?** Reported evaluation turns increase, but their productivity is unresolved. Evaluate base, SFT, and T1 across identical turn/context budgets and report success-versus-cost curves (§6.4).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The method and principal scores are explicit, but causal and comparative interpretation remains limited.

Missing information includes reconciled per-row evaluation budgets and attempt aggregation, confidence intervals, matched component ablations, detailed contamination checks, SFT construction details, total training compute, confirmed artifact availability, and the production configuration items the authors list as outstanding (§10).
