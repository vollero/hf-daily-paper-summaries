# Show-Harness: Just a VLM Agent Can Play Robots

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.10522) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10522)


## One-sentence takeaway
Show-Harness connects VLMs to bounded robot motions through discrete action commands, reporting stronger manipulation performance than tested baselines while leaving transfer to unseen complex embodiments unestablished (Table 2; §6).

## Problem and motivation
The authors address the gap between VLMs’ semantic reasoning and executable robot control. They argue that learning embodiment-specific motor outputs demands substantial adaptation, while delegating whole skills to downstream controllers limits the VLM’s involvement in physical decisions. Their proposed middle ground is an interpretable interface for incremental movement with execution feedback (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Semantic control interface:** View-relative translation, axis-specific rotation, gripper commands, and completion signals map deterministically to bounded Cartesian setpoint updates. Embodiment-specific interpreters handle calibration, workspace limits, and low-level controllers (§3.2).
- **Configurable agent harness:** Multi-view perception, textual robot state, planning, action history, recovery, adaptive step sizes, and selective action chunking support closed-loop execution (§3.3).
- **Two deployment modes:** Frontier VLMs operate without task fine-tuning; smaller VLMs learn action names through native vocabulary using LoRA and demonstration supervision, without dedicated action heads (§3.4).
- **GUMI and demonstration corpus:** A shared keyboard/browser interface records observations, semantic actions, and corresponding robot trajectories for human, agent, or mixed collection (§4; Appendix §7.2).

The distinctive contribution is the integrated interface and supporting evidence. The action-representation ablation suggests that explicit motion conventions contribute more than meaningful action names alone (§5.4.4).

## Evidence and main results
ZS denotes Gemini-3.1 Pro with medium thinking effort; FT denotes adapted Qwen3.5-2B.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Higher average task success | Across ten object–receptacle tasks, ten trials each: ZS **89%**, FT **86%**, versus RATS **57%**, H-VLA **50%**, π0.5 **39%**, and GR00T **35%**. | Table 2 |
| Robustness to tested visual changes | For background, lighting, viewpoint, and distractor shifts, each tested on twenty trials: ZS **20/20** in every condition; FT **18/20, 19/20, 19/20, 19/20**, respectively. | Table 2 |
| Simulation-only training transfers in a narrow setting | On real Franka Block/Banana→Plate tasks, FT achieves **13/20**, versus **0/20** for both π0.5 and GR00T; trainable methods use the shared simulated demonstration corpus. | §5.2; Table 2 |
| Same interface works on both tested arms | Five Plate tasks per arm: ZS **48/50** on Franka and **45/50** on AgileX; FT **45/50** and **42/50**. FT was trained on demonstrations from both embodiments. | §5.2; Table 2 |
| Explicit conventions largely explain action grounding | Twenty Block/Banana→Plate trials: semantic names plus conventions **20/20**; names alone **18/20**; arbitrary symbols plus conventions **19/20**; symbols alone **1/20**. | §5.4.4; Fig. 10 |
| Planning and recovery materially affect ZS performance | Five Plate tasks: default success **96%**, versus **60%** without subtask planning and **72%** without failure recovery. | §5.4.3; Fig. 9 |
| Small-model adaptation has a reported modest training cost | Qwen3.5-2B fine-tuning takes **less than two hours on one H200**, using forty epochs; authors also state training fits 24GB-class GPUs, without reporting equivalent runtime there. | Appendix §7.1 |

These results support the usefulness of the complete harness in the tested settings. They do not isolate semantic action prediction as the sole cause of superiority over other architectures. Matched demonstrations improve the VLA comparison, but do not establish matched model capacity, tuning effort, or inference resources.

Table 2’s environmental averages cover different conditions: FT and the standalone VLAs include sim-to-real, whereas ZS and agentic baselines do not. Those averages should not be compared as identical test sets.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Evaluation primarily covers single- and dual-arm manipulation with parallel-jaw grippers. Humanoids, dexterous hands, and richer tactile/force inputs remain future work (§6).
- **Analyst inference—transfer scope:** FT performance on Franka and AgileX demonstrates multi-embodiment training and evaluation, not transfer to an unseen robot (§5.2).
- **Analyst inference—statistical strength:** Main tasks use ten trials each, and the supplied text reports no confidence intervals or repeated-training variability (§5.1; Table 2).
- **Analyst inference—engineering dependence:** Calibration, controller implementation, explicit directional conventions, and detailed prompt guidance remain required components; zero-shot deployment does not mean zero robot integration (§3.2; Appendix §7.4).
- **Internal reporting discrepancies:** Fine-control prose reports ZS improvement from **60% to 82%**, while Fig. 6 displays **60% to 80%**; the same prose/figure disagree on π0.5 results. Video-conditioning prose reports FT **95%**, while Fig. 6 displays **90%** for both demonstration sources (§5.3). These exact results remain unresolved.
- **Reproducibility gaps:** Canonical prompts and FT settings are supplied, but full specialized-plugin implementations are deferred to the repository; detailed baseline tuning and comprehensive deployment costs are unavailable in the supplied text.

## Open research questions
- **Does FT transfer to an unseen embodiment?** Both evaluated arms appear in training. Train on one arm and evaluate on another using only a new interpreter, with calibration effort recorded.
- **How much advantage comes from action representation versus surrounding support?** Existing ablations examine plugins and naming, but do not fully match competing control architectures. Compare semantic and continuous-action policies with shared backbones, observations, demonstrations, planning, and recovery.
- **Can the interface support contact-rich manipulation?** The authors identify richer sensing and embodiments as future work. Evaluate force-sensitive insertion or dexterous manipulation with and without tactile/force feedback, measuring success and contact failures.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The method, main counts, prompts, and training setup are well described, but some secondary results conflict internally.

Material missing information includes statistical uncertainty, complete baseline configurations, specialized-plugin implementations, comprehensive runtime/API costs, and tests of FT transfer to unseen embodiments. The text advertises code/model/data assets and explicitly states a corpus release; their availability was not independently verified.
