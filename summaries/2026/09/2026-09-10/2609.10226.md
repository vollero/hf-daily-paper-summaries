# Φ-Bench: Can Large Language Models Engineer the Infrastructure That Powers Them?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-10.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.10226) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10226)


## One-sentence takeaway
Φ-Bench extends infrastructure-agent evaluation from isolated kernels to repository implementation and open-ended optimization, with the strongest evaluated model scoring 36.53% under its mixed, correctness-gated reward scheme (Tables 2–3).

## Problem and motivation
LLM infrastructure engineering requires navigating repositories, identifying bottlenecks, and repeatedly implementing, profiling, and debugging changes. The authors argue that existing benchmarks mostly specify individual operators or optimization targets, leaving these broader engineering capabilities insufficiently evaluated (Introduction; Related Works).

## Approach and contributions
- **Benchmark:** 85 repository-grounded tasks: 55 Kernel Function Completion (KFC), 20 Long-Horizon Implementation (LHI), and 10 End-to-End Optimization (E2EO) tasks. Their scopes expand from single-file implementation to repository-wide optimization (Task Formats; Task Distribution).
- **Construction pipeline:** A taxonomy derived from 2,260 papers and 1,852 repository artifacts guides PR/issue reconstruction, agent-assisted implementation removal and branch-coverage-driven test generation, and expert curation. The taxonomy contains nine broad categories and 62 middle-level topics (Task Synthesis).
- **Evaluation framework and analysis:** Correctness and integrity checks precede binary implementation or continuous performance rewards. Experiments compare eight models and examine reasoning effort, iterative refinement, and solution trajectories (Evaluation Metrics; Evaluation; Analysis).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Best overall score remains modest | Claude Opus 5: **36.53%**; Kimi K3: **28.12%**; Qwen3.8 Max: **27.73%**, across the full 85-task benchmark | Table 2 |
| Performance differs across formats | Opus 5 leads with **37.16% KFC**, **21.60% LHI**, and **62.94% E2EO**; every evaluated model scores lower on LHI than KFC | Table 3 |
| Hardware & Edge has particularly low scores | Highest category score is **5.40%**, achieved by Qwen3.7 Max | Table 2; Main Results |
| Maximum reasoning effort helps, but gains are nonmonotonic | Authors report that Opus 5, Kimi K3, and GPT-5.6 Sol each perform best at maximum effort on the **20 LHI tasks**; intermediate settings do not consistently improve scores | Figure 5; Ablation Studies |
| Iteration can improve optimization outcomes | On one nanoGPT E2EO task minimizing validation bits per byte under wall-clock and parameter-count constraints, authors report progressive improvement for several models and extended plateaus for others | Figure 6; Ablation Studies |
| Controlled experimentation appears useful in the case study | An Opus learning-rate-schedule change worsens BPB from **1.3646 to 1.3932** under matched seed and step count; a subsequent warmup–stable–decay schedule achieves **1.3248** | Case Study, p. 8 |

The main evaluation uses eight CPU cores, 32 GiB memory, and one H20 GPU per task; KFC permits one submission, while LHI/E2EO permit up to 16, with the best submission scored (Experimental Setup). These percentages are **benchmark rewards, not task-solving rates**: implementation rewards are binary, while performance rewards are zero at or below reference performance and reach full credit at the square of the reference improvement ratio (Evaluation Metrics).

The evidence establishes differences within this setup. It does not isolate model capability from scaffold, submission budget, task composition, or scoring effects, nor establish that observed trajectory behaviors cause higher performance.

## Limitations and validity risks
The authors emphasize uneven capabilities and nonmonotonic reasoning gains but provide no dedicated limitations section. Additional **analyst inferences** are:

- **Comparison confounds:** GPT-5.6 Sol uses Codex; other models use Claude Code. Maximum reasoning settings and context capacities are model-specific, without matched compute or scaffold controls (Experimental Setup).
- **Scope imbalance:** Kernel completion dominates the task collection, and evaluation uses a single GPU configuration. Broader distributed-system and hardware generalization remains untested here (Task Distribution; Experimental Setup).
- **Limited causal evidence:** Iteration and detailed behavioral conclusions center on one E2EO task. Error counts are not normalized by attempts or trajectory length, limiting the persistence interpretation (Ablation Studies; Error Modes Analysis; Case Study).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Figures 6 and 8 extend to 24 iterations despite the default 16-candidate limit, without explicitly reconciling the exception. Figure 5’s extracted labels do not reliably map scores to settings or clarify their relationship to Table 3’s LHI results.
- **Integrity uncertainty:** Visible tests and public-source implementations create potential overfitting or prior-exposure risks. No confirmed hacking was found, but that observation alone does not establish comprehensive prevention (Task Formats; Hacking Prevention & Detection).

## Open research questions
- **Do rankings survive scaffold and compute controls?** The setup varies both across models. Next experiment: cross models with both scaffolds under matched token, time, and submission budgets.
- **Do controlled experiments causally improve agent performance?** The case study provides observational evidence from one task. Next experiment: ablate local validation, noise estimation, and cache controls across multiple E2EO tasks.
- **How robust are scores to unseen workloads and hardware?** Tests are visible and execution uses one GPU type. Next experiment: evaluate submitted solutions on held-out workloads and additional hardware configurations.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** Benchmark structure and tabulated rankings are clear; broader interpretations are less secure.

Material missing information includes per-task specifications and repository revisions, task-by-task metric assignments, complete aggregation details, repeated-run uncertainty, exact compute and time budgets, synthesis-model configurations, contamination analysis, and confirmation of which assets are publicly released. Some figure details are not recoverable from the supplied text.
