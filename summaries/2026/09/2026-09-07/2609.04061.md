# When Models Edit Too Much: On the Fidelity of Minimal Code Edits

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-07.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.04061) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04061)


## One-sentence takeaway
On controlled local code repairs, preservation prompting reduces unnecessary edits, while execution-and-edit-reward RL gives Qwen3 a stronger out-of-domain fidelity–correctness trade-off than the tested supervised methods, with limited evidence of transfer to real Java bugs.

## Problem and motivation
Passing tests does not establish that a repair preserves surrounding implementation choices or remains easy to review. The authors study **over-editing**: passing repairs that change more than the intended local fix requires. They identify a gap in correctness-centered coding evaluation and target function-level maintenance, where unnecessary rewrites can obscure the actual repair (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled evaluation:** Corrupt reference solutions from 400 BigCodeBench Python tasks using localized AST transformations, retaining programs that fail existing tests. Reversing the transformations supplies an intended small repair (§3.1).
- **Fidelity measurement and validation:** Measure excess normalized token Levenshtein distance against the intended repair and added cognitive complexity, alongside Pass@1. Validate these proxies through developer comparisons and a manual audit (§3.1; Appendix B).
- **Behavioral study:** Compare generic and preservation prompts, reasoning variants, model sizes, and corruption types; introduce a multi-label taxonomy of unnecessary edits (§3.2).
- **Post-training comparison:** Compare SFT, rejection-sampled SFT, DPO, and GRPO-style RL on corrupted DeepCoder examples. RL combines execution feedback with an excess-edit penalty; experiments include LoRA, reward and rollout ablations, coding retention, and Java transfer (§4; Appendix D).

The distinctive contribution is this combined controlled evaluation and comparative study; the paper acknowledges earlier minimal-repair and edit-aware training methods (§2).

Unless otherwise stated, fidelity metrics average only passing repairs. The evidence supports fidelity as a useful additional evaluation axis and shows improvements within the tested settings. It does not establish semantic correctness beyond the tests, measured reductions in review time or regressions, or universal RL superiority.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Short synthetic Python repairs and Qwen training dominate the study; repository-wide changes are outside its main evaluation. Human studies are small, and real Java repair rates remain low (Limitations).
- **Analyst inference—reference minimality:** The injected reversal is a known intended repair, but not demonstrably the globally smallest valid repair. Valid alternatives in the audit and negative excess distances in Table 3 qualify the authors’ “known minimal” framing.
- **Analyst inference—OOD separation:** Some held-out operators overlap with training families: min/max swaps and range-endpoint changes appear in both lists. This weakens interpretation as wholly unseen corruption semantics (Appendices A.1, D.2).
- **Analyst inference—uneven evidence:** Slice-bound conclusions rest on only three benchmark tasks; category-level rankings therefore warrant caution (Tables 1, 9).
- **Comparison limits:** AdaPatcher comparison covers its preference-learning stage without its runtime-trace locator. Rollout-count matching addresses one budget difference, but comprehensive hyperparameter search was omitted (§4.2; Appendices D.5–D.6).

## Open research questions
- **Does smaller editing reduce actual maintenance cost?** Human judgments assess perceived reviewability, not timed review or regression discovery. Next experiment: randomized developer reviews measuring time, missed defects, and acceptance.
- **Does fidelity training generalize to clearly disjoint bug semantics?** Held-out corruption lists partly overlap training families. Next experiment: audit operator overlap and evaluate semantically disjoint synthetic families alongside human-written bugs.
- **Can preservation remain useful when broad changes are necessary?** The benchmark deliberately favors local fixes. Next experiment: mix local repairs with multi-method bugs and necessary refactorings, measuring both excess edits and failures caused by insufficient changes.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** Detailed tables, human checks, and ablations support the central findings, but generalization and minimality claims require qualification.

Material missing or unclear information includes Defects4J sample counts and selection details; training-seed variability; uncertainty for retention and transfer differences; exact definitions of alternative reward ablations; and consistent post-training evaluation provenance—§4.1 and Appendix D.2 describe DeepCoder evaluation, while Appendix D.4 uses BigCodeBench.
