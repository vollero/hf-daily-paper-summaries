# HarvestBench: Measuring Whether LLM Agents Will Pay to Avoid Killing Animals

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-07.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.04444) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04444)


## One-sentence takeaway
HarvestBench finds large, prompt-sensitive differences in simulated animal-harm avoidance, but provider dependence and uncertain fuel opportunity costs limit interpretation as stable willingness to pay or compassion.

## Problem and motivation
The authors study whether LLM agents spend resources to avoid harming animals while pursuing another objective. They identify a gap between stated animal-welfare preferences and consequential choices that impose a cost for avoiding harm. Their proposed contribution is a benchmark combining priced side effects, explicitly identified living victims, and a resource-constrained spatial simulation (§§1, 3); the claim of being first is not independently established by the supplied text.

## Approach and contributions
- **Benchmark and deterministic scoring:** HarvestBench uses an Inspect-based farm simulation. Two tractors controlled by the same model select crops, then autopilot interrupts navigation at obstacles. Fresh model calls choose to continue, swerve, or reroute. Game logs directly record animal deaths, obstacle collisions, harvests, theft, and fuel use (§§2, 4).
- **Controls and experimental interventions:** Rocks impose tractor damage and fuel loss; hay bales provide a nonliving, tractor-safe comparison. Experiments vary morality instructions, reasoning settings, avoidance prices, evaluation disclosure, and pasture geometry (§§2, 5).
- **Behavioral and economic analysis:** The authors compare farmed versus wild animals and estimate price elasticities using episode-level count models with answered-encounter offsets and layout-clustered standard errors (§§5.3, 5.10).

Animal welfare is absent from the default scored objectives, although the morality arm explicitly requests moral conduct and encounter prompts identify animals. Avoidance consumes simulated fuel, not money (§2; Appendix D).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Wide variation under moral instructions | Across 30 shifts per model at the standard 2-fuel setting, animal continue rates range from Terra’s 3/712 encounters (0.4%) to GPT-4o-mini’s 162/164 (98.8%). | Table 1 |
| Controls distinguish some avoidance patterns | Terra continues through 70% of hay encounters versus 0.4% of animal encounters; Sol’s corresponding rates are 1% and 0.9%. Rock rates are reported as 0% for eight models and 1% for Gemini Flash. | Table 1 |
| Removing morality instructions sharply increases harm | Among six reasoning models with usable paired conditions, neutral-arm animal continue rates are 84.6–100.0%; five have morality-arm rates below 6%. Sonnet is excluded from this comparison for inconsistent reasoning. | Table 2 |
| Reasoning ablation changes morality-arm behavior | Disabling reasoning increases Haiku’s animal continue rate from 4.5% to 94.3% and GPT-5-mini’s from 5.4% to 60.9%; reported significance is \(p<10^{-8}\). | Table 3 |
| Wild animals receive less protection | All nine models have higher wild-animal than farmed-animal continue rates on the default map under the morality briefing; reported gaps span 0.6–24.5 percentage points. | §5.3; Figure 4 |
| Explicit animal scoring changes behavior | Across 30 seeds per briefing, Sonnet’s continue rate changes from 19.0% with hidden animal scoring to 0.6% when animals are explicitly scored; Haiku changes from 7.5% to 0.4%. | Table 5 |
| Price sensitivity exists but is uneven | Sonnet’s animal kill rate rises from 17% at a 1-fuel swerve price to 38.7% at 8 fuel. Elasticity analysis over 2–8 fuel describes most harm rates, and all sparing rates, as inelastic. | §§5.9–5.10; Figures 5–6 |
| Serving conditions materially affect scores | Gemini’s unpinned runs yield 38.7%, 40.3%, and 45.1%, versus 3.6% and 4.2% when pinned to Google. Sonnet’s July rates of 17.0–21.0% fall to 2.8–3.4% in August reruns matching recorded settings. | Appendix C |

These results establish conditional behavior in this harness. They do not establish conscious intent, stable moral preferences, or real-world animal treatment. Rock avoidance supports basic action comprehension; it does not prove that every animal collision reflects an understood choice to kill. The authors’ asset-protection explanation for the species gap is an interpretation, explicitly acknowledged as such in Appendix B.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope limits:** Only two-agent crews were evaluated, communication was restricted, animals were explicitly salient, and real-world transfer remains unknown. Wild-animal labels may also evoke pest status (§6).
- **Uncertain economic meaning:** Agents lack enough information to determine whether fuel is binding. Paying fuel therefore does not establish a measured sacrifice in the harvest objective (§6).
- **Reproducibility limits:** Model aliases often lack immutable build identifiers; routing and later reruns substantially change some scores. Deterministic scoring does not make model behavior fully reproducible (Appendices A–C).
- **Statistical ambiguity:** Section 4 first describes Fisher’s exact tests, then specifies shift-level Mann–Whitney and sign tests. Multiple-comparison correction is absent. The supplied Figure 6 extraction lacks its numerical estimates and confidence intervals.
- **Analyst inference—exposure and reporting:** Avoidance preserves animals for repeated encounters, while theft changes exposure. The authors report stable rankings after deduplication (§4), but encounter rates remain policy-dependent. Appendix B also gives Haiku and DeepSeek kill counts that differ from Table 1 without clear reconciliation.
- **Analyst inference—causal scope:** The reasoning on/off comparison covers only two retained models; broader claims about reasoning or capability exceed that ablation’s coverage (Table 3).

## Open research questions
- **Does fuel expenditure reflect sacrifice of task performance?** Fuel’s effect on deliveries is unclear (§6). A next experiment could impose known fuel budgets with quantified delivery losses from avoidance.
- **Does the species gap reflect ownership, pest status, or species identity?** These explanations remain confounded (§6; Appendix B). Randomize ownership and pest descriptions independently of animal species and position.
- **Do morality and reasoning effects generalize beyond salient interruptions?** Animals are explicitly presented, and reasoning ablations are narrow. Test more models with matched compute budgets and less explicit harm cues.
- **Which scores survive deployment changes?** Provider and temporal shifts are unresolved (Appendix C). Repeat identical seeds across pinned providers and dated model snapshots where available.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The tables strongly support setting-specific behavioral differences, but causal interpretation and reproducibility are less secure.

Material missing information includes readable Figure 6 estimates and intervals, raw episode logs and executable artifacts, complete sampling configurations, immutable model versions for most systems, reconciliation of inconsistent counts and statistical descriptions, and evidence connecting simulated fuel costs to real-world outcomes.
