# CosmoH2G: A Hand-to-Gripper Transfer Dataset and Baseline Method for Object Manipulation with Complex Spatial Movements

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-09.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.07498) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07498)


## One-sentence takeaway
CosmoH2G combines paired human–gripper demonstrations with two-stage diffusion and geometric optimization, outperforming evaluated baselines on rotational pick-and-place transfer while remaining limited by open-loop execution and hand-scale generalization.

## Problem and motivation
Transferring human demonstrations to robot grippers requires bridging differences in hand shape, contact, and motion. The authors argue that hand-crafted retargeting struggles with natural grasps, while object-trajectory methods lose reliable tracking under rotations and occlusion and omit useful hand-pose information (§1, Figs. 3–4). They identify a shortage of paired demonstrations emphasizing spatial rotation and flipping.

## Approach and contributions
- **Dataset and collection pipeline:** CosmoH2G contains 6,189 paired episodes across 1,254 objects. Collectors perform varied human grasps and spatial movements, then imitate them with a handheld UMI gripper. RGB-D processing produces registered object points, hand meshes, and gripper poses; alignment filtering retains closely matched pairs (§§3.1–3.3).
- **Two-stage transfer model:** Point Transformer encoders condition diffusion Transformers on hand/object geometry and contact maps. Stage I predicts initial and terminal gripper poses; Stage II predicts the intervening orientation sequence using these endpoints (§§4.1–4.3).
- **Geometric translation refinement:** Translation starts from aligned hand–gripper contact centers and is refined for contact, penetration, smoothness, and inverse-kinematics feasibility. Thus, the complete system combines learned generation with geometric assumptions and optimization (§4.3, Appendix A.4).

The authors claim the first hand–gripper dataset focused on complex spatial movement and promise public dataset, pipeline, and algorithm releases; neither priority nor completed release is established by the supplied text (§1).

## Evidence and main results

GOA and TOPA measure angular error, so lower is better. SR counts final positions within 10% of desktop width; it does **not** require orientation success. TS compares contact-center position trajectories using normalized FastDTW, with tolerance set to 20% of table width (§5.1, Appendix D).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Large, closely aligned paired dataset | 6,189 episodes, 1,254 objects; retained-pair similarity above 0.9, mean 0.957 and median 0.958. These describe filtered pairs. | §§3.1–3.3; Fig. 13 |
| Best reported simulation results | On 186 cases across 40 training-unseen objects: CosmoH2G versus Track2Act achieves SR 83.87% vs 77.42%, TS 0.9672 vs 0.9015, GOA 7.53° vs 10.67°, TOPA 10.27° vs 12.48°. Track2Act is the strongest listed baseline on these metrics. | §5.1; Table 2 |
| Best reported real-robot results | Same test tasks on Galaxea R1 Lite: CosmoH2G versus Track2Act achieves SR 70.43% vs 60.22%, TS 0.9413 vs 0.8732, GOA 7.53° vs 10.67°, TOPA 19.34° vs 20.58°. | §5.1; Table 2 |
| Two-stage design beats tested single-stage alternatives | Simulation SR is 83.87% versus 62.90% for a denoising Transformer, 58.60% for Diffusion Policy, and 64.52% for ACT. | Table 3 |
| Endpoint conditioning and representation matter | In simulation, removing the ending action raises TOPA from 10.27° to 21.79°. Keypoints yield SR 83.87%, versus 69.89% for matrices and 72.58% for quaternions. | Tables 4–5 |

These results support improved transfer within the evaluated pick-and-place setting. They do not establish broad manipulation competence or statistical significance: uncertainty estimates are absent. The no-contact ablation supports gains beyond that input, but does not by itself establish fully matched baseline comparisons.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Execution lacks feedback correction and explicit collision avoidance. Real-world failures include slipping during rotation and movement before sufficient lifting (§5.2, §7).
- **Author-stated:** Evaluation focuses on pick-and-place; children’s hands fall outside the training scale distribution and perform substantially worse (§5.1, Appendix E).
- **Analyst inference:** Filtered, closely aligned demonstrations and the aligned-contact prior leave robustness to mismatched pairs or changing grasp regions uncertain (§3.2, §4.3).
- **Analyst inference:** Track2Act is modified to use 3D hand sequences and rule-based initialization. Its comparison therefore concerns this adaptation; the text also inconsistently describes its training as retraining and fine-tuning (§§5.1–5.2, Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference:** Real-object orientation metrics depend on FoundationPose++ estimates without reported measurement uncertainty. Table 2 repeats identical simulation and real GOA values for every method without explanation (Appendix A.5, Table 2).
- **Analyst inference:** Simulation ablations do not separately isolate every optimization term or quantify sensitivity to depth and reconstruction errors (§5.3).

## Open research questions
1. **Can feedback reduce rotational failures?** Open-loop slipping remains unresolved (§5.2, §7). Compare the current system with visual feedback and replanning on matched rotation and obstacle trials.
2. **Does more paired data close the single-stage gap?** The authors suggest this possibility but provide no scaling study (§7). Train matched single- and two-stage models across increasing dataset sizes.
3. **What limits transfer across hand scales and perception quality?** Children’s-hand performance drops, while reconstruction-error sensitivity is untested (Appendix E). Evaluate scale normalization and controlled depth/hand-mesh perturbations separately.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text gives detailed methods, quantitative comparisons, and appendices, but lacks confidence intervals, repeated-run statistics, demonstrator-test episode counts, baseline training details, inference latency, several optimization/contact hyperparameters, and confirmation of artifact availability. Exact cross-dataset rotation statistics underlying Fig. 2 are unavailable in the retrieved text.
