# EVOHARNESSBENCH: Can Your Agents Keep Pace with an Evolving Harness?

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-09.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.04280) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04280)


## One-sentence takeaway
EVOHARNESSBENCH shows that expanding an agent’s tools, skills, or specialist pool can impair previously successful behavior without changing model weights, while persistent adaptation delivers uneven benefits across environments and capability types.

## Problem and motivation
Deployed agents encounter growing collections of tools, procedures, and specialists. Earlier capabilities remain available, yet agents may become less reliable at using them; accumulated memories or instructions may also become less useful.

The authors identify a benchmark gap: existing evaluations generally keep the harness fixed, change the task stream, or study agent-generated capability growth. Their focus is externally supplied harness growth and its separate effects on retention and adaptation (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled harness streams.** A deterministic pipeline ranks annotated capabilities by task frequency, releases common capabilities first, and assigns tasks to their earliest annotation-feasible stage. The benchmark reports 17 streams with 3–6 stages, 802 unique tasks, and 1,510 axis-specific task examples derived from EOG and ALE (§§3.1–3.2; Appendix B).
- **Three capability axes.** Tools use source annotations; skills use rule-based procedural extraction and heuristic matching; specialists receive disjoint entity or software-family tool bundles. The reported capability inventory contains 520 tools, 42 reference skills, and 62 specialists (§3.3; Appendices D–E).
- **Two evaluation modes.** Fresh deployment instances isolate expansion effects; adaptation-enabled systems carry memory, prompts, skills, or harness code across stages while model weights remain fixed. Held-out cohorts measure new-task adaptation and earlier-task retention separately (§3.4; Appendix C).
- **Comparative diagnosis.** Experiments evaluate existing adaptation methods and exposure controls, identifying distinct tool-search, skill-engagement, and delegation problems. The contribution is the benchmark and analysis, rather than a new adaptation algorithm (§4).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Broader tool access improves aggregate deployment accuracy but increases tokens | EOG ReAct: task-specific versus cumulative tools gives **26.0% → 30.2% pass**, with **27.2M → 75.8M tokens**. ALE Codex: **11.6% → 12.2% pass**, with **82.2M → 100.8M tokens**. | Table 1 |
| Tool adaptation gains vary by environment | Under cumulative tools, MemToolAgent versus deployment reaches **38.6% versus 30.2% pass on EOG**, but **10.1% versus 12.2% on ALE**. | Table 1 |
| Retention and adaptation can diverge | ALE tools: GEPA and Meta-Harness have positive reported BWT, but reported FWT of **−28.5%** and **−11.1%**, respectively. Transfer normalization is ambiguous; see limitations. | §4.1, Fig. 3 |
| Skill expansion has little aggregate deployment effect | EOG Codex remains at **18.9% pass** with either task-specific or cumulative skills; cumulative-skill GEPA reaches **24.1%**. | Table 3 |
| Specialist adaptation helps EOG more than ALE | Meta-Harness versus cumulative deployment: **18.5% versus 8.8% pass on EOG**, but **3.2% versus 4.2% on ALE**. | Table 5 |
| Agent expansion can substantially impair retention | ALE deployment Codex has reported **−34.7% BWT** without persistent adaptation. This is a transfer statistic, not a final pass rate. | §4.3, Fig. 6 |
| Stable adaptation exposure can help coordination | EOG Meta-Harness, always evaluated with cumulative specialists: adaptation with the final pool throughout reaches **20.3% pass**, versus **18.5%** with staged cumulative exposure. | Table 6 |

These results support harness-dependent retention failures and environment-dependent adaptation benefits in the tested systems. Improved aggregate accuracy can coexist with deterioration on earlier cohorts. Delegation and skill-use diagnostics suggest mechanisms, but do not establish that those mechanisms alone cause the observed failures or that the findings generalize to arbitrary deployments.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** capabilities are only added, never modified or removed; replacement and retirement remain future work (§§3.2, 5).
- **Author-stated annotation limitation:** skill matching does not establish that annotated procedures are necessary, sufficient, or unique. Consequently, skill feasibility and “required” coverage depend on a heuristic proxy (§3.3).
- **Analyst inference—generalization:** two source benchmarks, selected enterprise domains, and one frequency-based release policy limit conclusions about other environments or release orders. Some ALE multi-agent runs exceeded comparable computational budgets (Table 5; Appendices B, D).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** main text describes retrievable reference skills, while Appendix B says evaluated systems do not receive those skills. Appendix D supports the library-access description, leaving the exact protocol inconsistent.
- **Metric ambiguity:** figures describe relative FWT/BWT, but Appendix C gives weighted, unnormalized accuracy differences. Reported percentages should not automatically be interpreted as percentage-point changes.
- **Evaluation-subset ambiguity:** ALE construction tables list larger test sets than the main result tables, without a clear account of selection. Skill-memory results also differ substantially between Tables 3 and I.1 without fully specified comparable settings.
- **Reproducibility gaps:** the supplied text does not clearly establish repetition counts, uncertainty definitions for the main tables, complete execution configurations, or exact released artifacts.

## Open research questions
- **Would different release orders produce the same failures?** Only frequency-ranked growth is evaluated. Repeat matched tasks under randomized and dependency-aware releases while controlling pool sizes.
- **Can adaptation improve both retention and new-task performance?** Transfer trade-offs remain unresolved. Compare ordinary adaptation against updates explicitly scored on both earlier and current held-out cohorts.
- **Does increased skill engagement cause better outcomes?** Invocation varies across models and methods, while skill labels are heuristic. Test controlled retrieval interventions with manually validated task–skill annotations.
- **Can preserving successful delegation prevent forgetting?** Delegation drift is associated with forgotten tasks, but causality is unproven. Test reuse of prior successful delegation plans against unrestricted replanning under expanded pools.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The main tables support the central qualitative findings, but protocol and metric inconsistencies weaken precise interpretation.

Material missing information includes reconciled skill exposure rules, transfer normalization, ALE evaluation-subset selection, main-table uncertainty definitions and repetition counts, complete adaptation settings, and confirmed release contents.
