# Exploring Collaboration between a language and a non-language agent

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-03.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.00474) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00474)


## One-sentence takeaway
The authors propose projecting a frozen specialist agent’s hidden state into an LLM as learned tokens, reporting substantially better chess collaboration than matched text-only integration, especially for signals and multi-step tasks that are difficult to verbalize.

## Problem and motivation
LLMs typically communicate with specialized agents through text, but non-language agents encode expertise in continuous policy, value, and feature representations that textual summaries may discard. The authors call the resulting performance loss “verbalization debt” and identify two gaps: the absence of a direct interface between LLMs and pretrained non-language specialists, and the lack of a diverse benchmark for measuring such collaboration (Introduction, pp. 1–3).

Chess is the primary testbed because engines provide positional expertise but cannot generate contextual explanations, while LLMs can produce language but lack reliable chess-state assessment. The benchmark therefore targets tasks the authors argue neither component handles well alone (Sections 1 and 3.1, pp. 2–3, 6).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Latent state internalization:** A three-layer MLP, `LatentBridge`, projects a frozen agent’s penultimate-layer activation into 32 continuous embeddings inserted into the LLM context. Language, environment actions, and dynamically refreshed state tokens coexist in one interaction trace (Sections 2.1–2.2; Figure 2, pp. 4–5).

2. **Two-stage LLAMIA training:** The projector is first aligned on agent-generated state–policy pairs while the LLM remains frozen; the projector and Qwen3 backbone are then jointly optimized with DAPO reinforcement learning. The specialist remains frozen, and the LLM learns when to query it (Section 2.3, pp. 5–6).

3. **Controlled verbalization comparison:** LLAMIA-Verb uses the same backbone, data, rewards, and RL recipe but receives only textual engine outputs, allowing the authors to attribute matched-system differences to the interface (Sections 3.2 and C.2, pp. 6, 24).

4. **LLAMIA-Bench and data assets:** The authors assemble six chess tasks covering behavior imitation, puzzle assessment, move rationale, and full-game commentary; additions include three Wild behavior-cloning splits, puzzle-interest prediction, and the 1,900-game Agadmator-2K commentary dataset (Sections 3.1 and B.1, pp. 6, 20–21).

5. **Cross-domain pilot:** The same broad recipe is applied to rank-conditioned Go behavior cloning using a frozen KataGo specialist (Section F, pp. 35–36).

These results support the authors’ claim that the tested latent interface preserves useful specialist information better than their particular textual serialization, under matched chess experiments and one Go task. They do not establish that every possible verbal interface is intrinsically inferior, nor that the method generalizes to robotics, autonomous driving, closed agents, or broadly different specialist architectures.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Most evidence comes from chess; the Go study covers only rank-conditioned behavior cloning rather than the full task suite (Limitations, p. 46).
- **Author-stated:** Internalization requires access to specialist activations, excluding direct use with closed-source agents unless an intermediary with such access is available (Limitations, p. 46).
- **Author-stated:** Agent architecture, parameter count, strength, and projector compatibility are confounded in the backend comparison, preventing a causal conclusion about architectural compatibility (Section E.2.1, p. 33).
- **Author-stated:** Agadmator-2K’s low-view-count split only heuristically reduces base-model contamination; closed-model pretraining overlap cannot be verified (Section B.3, p. 23).
- **Analyst inference:** The text baseline exposes selected moves, evaluations, probabilities, and variations. The experiments therefore establish a gap against this serialization, not against all possible higher-bandwidth, adaptive, or learned textual interfaces.
- **Analyst inference:** G-eval is both the training reward and primary metric for language tasks, which may favor optimization toward the judge. The paper reports human validation, but it uses only 12 participants and acknowledges a length association for G-eval (\(ρ=0.31\); Section H.5, p. 40).
- **Analyst inference:** Some reported quantities are internally inconsistent: Table 1 lists LLAMIA rationale as 45.8, while Table 15 reports 50.3 for its stated Planning/Comparative average; puzzle evaluation is described as 5,000 held-out examples in Section B.2.3 but approximately 1,000 in Table 17; commentary evaluation is described as 100 held-out games in Table 10 but approximately 300 games in Table 16. These discrepancies weaken confidence in exact result interpretation.
- **Analyst inference:** “Below chance” bot detection does not by itself prove statistically reliable human indistinguishability; the supplied text gives standard errors graphically but no hypothesis test or confidence interval for the 39% rate.

## Open research questions

1. **Can a stronger verbal interface close the reported debt?**  
   The paper compares latent tokens with a fixed engine serialization. A next experiment could match bandwidth and train a learned adaptive textual summarizer, then compare it with latent integration under equal context and compute.

2. **Does the method transfer to less structured real-world agents?**  
   Only chess and one Go behavior-cloning task are tested. A next experiment could internalize a frozen robotics policy and evaluate multi-step instruction following, explanation quality, and state-tracking under distribution shift.

3. **Which specialist representations are most compatible with an LLM?**  
   Architecture, strength, scale, and projector suitability are confounded. A factorial experiment could compare convolutional and Transformer specialists at matched parameter count, playing strength, layer depth, and projector capacity.

4. **How robust are the language-quality gains to independent evaluation?**  
   G-eval serves as both reward and primary automated metric. A preregistered study with more raters, blinded systems, independent expert rubrics, and judges not used during training could test whether gains persist.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text is extensive and includes methods, ablations, costs, per-task results, and human-study protocols. Confidence is reduced by inconsistent task counts and evaluation-set descriptions, conflicting rationale scores, and the absence of statistical significance tests for several human-study comparisons. The text also does not establish performance beyond chess and a single Go task, robustness across unrelated agent families, or comparison with learned high-bandwidth verbal interfaces.
