# Language Models Can Control Their Own Attention

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-03.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.02737) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02737)


## One-sentence takeaway

Declarative Attention lets off-the-shelf language models explicitly select global, focused, or local attention scopes during generation, reducing attended tokens by 52.0% on Gemma-4-31B and 31.1% on Qwen-3.6-27B across 15 long-context tasks, with accuracy drops of 1.27 and 2.75 percentage points.

## Problem and motivation

Long-context language models repeatedly read the full KV cache during decoding even though useful attention is concentrated on relatively few tokens. Existing dynamic sparse-attention methods avoid full attention computation but still scan a proxy representation of the entire context at every step, retaining \(O(N)\) selection overhead. The authors seek a query-dependent mechanism that avoids this external selection scan by having the model state its intended attention scope directly (Sections 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Declarative Attention (DA):** The model emits parseable reasoning tags for `<global>` attention over all context segments, `<focus>` attention over named segments, and `<local>` attention over the scaffold and generated response only. A state machine converts these declarations into decode-time masks (Section 2; Figure 1).
- **Zero-shot, task-agnostic control:** One fixed prompt elicits DA from unmodified Gemma and Qwen models across 15 long-context tasks, without fine-tuning or an auxiliary relevance scorer (Sections 1, 4).
- **Hardware-aligned implementation:** A custom vLLM integration rewrites KV-cache block tables at mode transitions, with outward-rounded block boundaries and no attention-kernel or scheduler modifications (Section 2.3; Appendix B).
- **Empirical characterization:** The paper evaluates accuracy, attended tokens, model-size scaling, context-length scaling, protocol adherence, and a roofline estimate of decode time (Sections 5–6).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| DA lowers average accuracy modestly. | Across 15 tasks, Gemma-4-31B falls from 87.01% to 85.74% (−1.27pp); Qwen-3.6-27B falls from 85.31% to 82.56% (−2.75pp). | Section 5.1; Table 2 |
| DA substantially reduces attended tokens. | Gemma falls from 13.43M to 6.45M per response (−52.0%); Qwen falls from 22.54M to 15.52M (−31.1%), macro-averaged across 15 tasks. | Section 5.1; Table 2 |
| Masking, rather than prompt formatting, drives the savings. | DA-no-mask uses 66.2% more attended tokens than vanilla on Gemma and 28.8% more on Qwen; adding the mask cuts tokens by 71.1% and 46.5% relative to DA-no-mask. | Section 5.1; Table 2; Figure 2 |
| DA generates more tokens despite lowering total attention. | Relative decode steps are 135% of vanilla for Gemma and 131% for Qwen, while attended-token totals are 48% and 69%. | Figure 2 |
| Accuracy retention improves with model size. | Relative accuracy rises from 29% to 99% across Gemma E4B–31B and from 64% to 97% across Qwen 4B–27B. | Section 5.2; Figure 3 |
| Absolute savings grow with context length on Gemma. | DA saves about 1M attended tokens in the shortest context bin and about 21M in the 64K–256K bin; relative accuracy declines to roughly 96% of vanilla in the longest bin. | Section 5.3; Figure 4 |
| Cheap modes account for most generated tokens. | On Gemma-4-31B, focus and local modes comprise about 73% of generated tokens and save 76–99% attention per token; global mode saves none by construction. | Section 6.1; Figure 5 |
| Larger models follow the focus syntax more reliably. | Focus success increases from 58% to 99% across Gemma E4B–31B and from 89% to 99% across Qwen 4B–27B. | Section 6.2; Figure 6 |
| Projected decode time decreases under specified assumptions. | On one B200 in bf16 at 40% MFU and 70% MBU, roofline totals fall from 269.1 to 192.3 ms for Gemma (0.71×) and 306.2 to 237.3 ms for Qwen (0.77×). | Section 5.4; Table 3 |
| The local evaluation judge closely matches a frontier judge. | On 2,993 responses, verdict agreement is 98.53%, \(\kappa=0.940\), and per-cell Pearson \(r=0.992\). | Appendix D.2; Table 9 |

These results establish that model-generated scope declarations can reduce logged attention reads while largely preserving aggregate judged accuracy on the selected tasks and larger backbones. They do not establish measured end-to-end latency gains: wall-clock results are roofline projections, exclude prefill, and assume an optimized large-batch deployment. They also do not show that DA consistently helps every task or model; several tasks incur higher total attention, and the smallest model exhibits severe protocol failure.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** All experiments use non-thinking mode because models failed to follow DA inside thinking traces (Section 4; Section 8.2).
- **Author-stated:** Zero-shot DA adds approximately one-third more decode steps, and its chosen mode decomposition may not fit a task well (Section 8.2).
- **Author-stated:** Artificial segmentation can split tables or destroy information needed for global aggregation. In the reported failure cases, mean accuracy falls from 84.21% to 58.84% across two such sources (Appendix D.4; Table 10).
- **Author-stated:** Tasks whose output length grows with the document can erase total savings even when per-step attention decreases; four failure cases rise from 17.84M to 21.20M attended tokens per sample on average (Appendix D.4; Table 10).
- **Author-stated:** Global steps remain fully expensive and account for more than 80% of DA’s attended tokens (Section 8.2).
- **Author-stated:** Some responses fail to terminate within the 8K generation budget, reaching about 6% for Gemma-4-12B and inflating token totals (Table 2 caption; Figure 3).
- **Author-stated:** Timing results are theoretical ceiling estimates, not measurements; they assume saturated hardware, omit prefill and minor operations, and count token-granular rather than slightly larger block-aligned reads (Section 5.4; Appendix C.8).
- **Analyst inference:** Generalization to natural agentic histories remains unverified because evaluation uses static benchmarks and manufactured segments; the paper presents agentic use as future potential (Section 8.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Reproducibility is limited by the absence of an explicit code or artifact-release statement in the supplied text, despite detailed prompts and implementation descriptions.
- **Analyst inference:** Accuracy depends on an LLM judge and partly synthetic QA, although the strong judge-agreement study reduces—without eliminating—this validity risk (Section 4; Appendix D.2).

## Open research questions

1. **Can post-training jointly optimize answer quality, tag validity, and attention cost?** Zero-shot use adds decode steps and fails on smaller models. A next experiment could fine-tune or apply reinforcement learning with a combined accuracy–attended-token reward, then compare against zero-shot DA and DA-no-mask.

2. **Would structure-aware segmentation prevent information loss?** Artificial chunks can split tables and undermine global counting. A controlled experiment could compare fixed-size, document-structure-aware, and learned segmentation on the six failure-case tasks in Table 10.

3. **Can global navigation avoid full-context reads?** Global mode contributes most of DA’s remaining attention cost. A next experiment could replace full context during global mode with segment summaries or an index, measuring recall, accuracy, and total attended tokens.

4. **Do predicted savings translate into measured production latency?** The paper reports roofline estimates rather than end-to-end timings. A deployment study could benchmark throughput and latency across batch sizes, context lengths, accelerators, and co-located versus disaggregated serving.
