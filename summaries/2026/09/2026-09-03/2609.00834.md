# Replacing Training with Memory: Listwise Selection for Text-to-SQL

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-03.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.00834) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00834)


## One-sentence takeaway

MAP-SQL replaces selector fine-tuning with retrieved, structured question-to-SQL memories and execution-group-aware permutation aggregation, improving SQL-candidate selection accuracy and efficiency across three benchmarks.

## Problem and motivation

Modern Text-to-SQL systems often generate several SQL candidates and then select one. Pointwise selection cannot directly contrast candidates, while pairwise comparison can require quadratic work; listwise selection offers joint comparison but is expensive to fine-tune because its examples contain multiple queries and execution results (Sections 1–3).

The authors identify two roles normally served by fine-tuning: learning selection criteria and reducing position-dependent “lost-in-the-middle” behavior. Their goal is to replace both with inference-time mechanisms while preserving listwise comparison. “Fine-tuning-free” applies only to selector parameters: the method still requires pretrained models and labeled question–SQL pairs for its memory bank (Section 1).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Structured selection memory.** An LLM converts each training question, schema, and gold SQL into a compact specification covering encoding, translation, and decoding criteria—such as schema grounding, joins, filters, aggregation, ordering, output form, and query constraints. Similar memories are retrieved by question embeddings and inserted into the listwise-selection prompt (Section 4.1; Table 1).

2. **Cost-aware permutation aggregation.** Candidates are grouped by identical execution results, groups are ordered using execution-result frequency, and candidates are permuted only within groups. Rankings from multiple runs are averaged, reducing the nominal permutation space from \(O(n!)\) to \(O(g!)\), where \(g\) is the number of execution-result groups (Sections 3 and 4.2).

3. **Confidence-triggered tie-breaking.** A paired-rank heuristic compares the two leading candidates; only uncertain cases invoke an optional pointwise selector (Section 4.2, Equations 4–5).

4. **Empirical evaluation.** The method is tested on three benchmarks, two candidate-pool sizes, and multiple generators, with component ablations, significance tests, repeated candidate pools, and question-perturbation experiments (Sections 5–6; Appendix A).

These results establish stronger selection on the tested candidate pools, with statistically supported fixed-pool gains in both BIRD settings and Spider. They do not establish a significant fixed-pool EHRSQL improvement, superiority to end-to-end proprietary systems, or generalization beyond Text-to-SQL. The main R3-SQL comparisons use a reproduction because its original models were unavailable (Section 5.1).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** The method uses sliding-window coordination; more advanced tournament or setwise ranking was not explored (Limitations, page 9).
- **Author-stated:** Its roughly 70–72% BIRD accuracy trails proprietary-model systems reported at 75–76%, partly because selection cannot recover a correct query absent from the generator’s pool (Limitations, page 9).
- **Author-stated:** Enterprise databases with hundreds of tables require upstream schema linking and pruning; schema-scale context management is outside the method’s scope (Limitations, page 9).
- **Author-stated:** Evaluation is restricted to Text-to-SQL, so transfer to other listwise-selection tasks is unknown (Limitations, page 9).
- **Analyst inference:** “Fine-tuning-free” should not be read as training-data-free or model-free: memory construction uses labeled question–SQL pairs, an LLM, and benchmark training splits. EHRSQL additionally uses memories from BIRD and Spider because it lacks a training split (Sections 1 and 5.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Comparisons with R3-SQL and MCS-SQL depend on reproduced algorithms rather than original released implementations or models, introducing implementation-equivalence uncertainty (Section 5.1; Appendix A.4).
- **Analyst inference:** The position-consistency results remain low in absolute terms—33.17% even with tie-breaking—so positional sensitivity is mitigated rather than eliminated (Table 6).
- **Analyst inference:** Statistical evidence is mixed: the EHRSQL fixed-pool confidence interval includes zero, and the three-seed experiment reports variability but no inferential test across seeds (Tables 9–10).

## Open research questions

1. **Can memory-guided selection generalize beyond Text-to-SQL?** The paper evaluates no other ranking domain. A next experiment could apply the same structured-memory and grouping design to code-solution or document reranking with domain-appropriate grouping signals.

2. **How does memory-bank quality and size affect performance?** Retrieval includes as many memories as fit the context, but the supplied text gives no systematic bank-size, retrieval-depth, or memory-noise ablation. An experiment could vary context budget, top-\(k\), source domain, and corrupted-memory rates.

3. **Can positional consistency improve without a separate pointwise model?** Consistency reaches only 33.17%, while tie-breaking adds model cost. A next experiment could compare adaptive permutation counts, tournament coordination, and setwise ranking at matched token budgets.

4. **How robust is the method on very large schemas?** The paper assumes upstream pruning. An experiment could vary schema size and schema-linking recall, measuring both selection accuracy and memory-retrieval degradation.

## Confidence and missing information
