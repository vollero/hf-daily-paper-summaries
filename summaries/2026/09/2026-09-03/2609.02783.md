# EarlyEval: Cheaper Agent Evaluation via Early Outcome Prediction

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-03.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.02783) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02783)


## One-sentence takeaway

EarlyEval predicts success or failure from partial agent trajectories, reducing execution steps by 13%–26% at selected operating points while keeping average resolve-rate distortion near 1–2 percentage points across three agent benchmarks.

## Problem and motivation

Multi-step agent benchmarks are expensive because every retained task may require dozens of model calls with growing contexts, and evaluation is repeated throughout agent development. Existing benchmark-distillation methods reduce the number of tasks but not the cost of executing each task. The authors address this complementary gap by asking whether a run can be terminated once its eventual benchmark outcome is sufficiently predictable from its intermediate trajectory (Sections I and II-A).

## Approach and contributions

- **Early outcome prediction:** Treat final binary success or failure as a prediction problem over partial trajectories, allowing evaluation to stop before natural completion (Section III-A).
- **EarlyEval framework:** Train separate LightGBM success and failure classifiers on prefixes of completed, outcome-labeled historical runs. At every step, calibrated confidence thresholds determine whether to predict an outcome or continue execution (Sections III-B–III-E; Figure 2).
- **Feature design:** Combine behavioral statistics, TF-IDF/SVD representations of prompts, actions, and feedback, and—where available—reference-solution descriptors and prefix-to-gold overlap (Table II).
- **Unseen-agent evaluation:** Test through leave-one-agent-out folds across SWE-bench Verified, TerminalBench, and Toolathlon, including TerminalBench splits excluding the held-out agent’s model or scaffold (Sections IV-A–IV-B).
- **Empirical characterization:** Measure outcome accuracy, step and token savings, resolve-rate distortion, ranking preservation, feature ablations, and alternative predictor architectures (Sections IV-C and V).

These results establish that historical trajectories can support useful early predictions for held-out agent configurations on the three studied benchmarks, with tunable trade-offs between intervention and fidelity. They do not establish equivalent performance on new benchmarks, benchmarks without historical runs, non-binary evaluations, or wholly new trajectory distributions. They also do not make early-stopped scores interchangeable with canonical full-run results; the authors explicitly retain full execution for final leaderboard claims (Section VIII).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** EarlyEval requires completed, outcome-labeled runs from the target benchmark, so it cannot immediately evaluate a new benchmark without historical trajectories (Section VIII).
- **Author-stated intended use:** Its estimates exhibit roughly 1–2 percentage points of systematic resolve-rate deviation and are intended for iterative development, not canonical benchmark submissions (Section VIII).
- **Author-stated external-validity risk:** Generalization to completely unseen architectures and new benchmarks remains uncertain despite evaluation on three heterogeneous suites (Section VI).
- **Distribution sensitivity:** Withholding scaffolds substantially weakened TerminalBench’s success predictor, suggesting behavioral features may shift with orchestration structure (Section V-A). This interpretation is made by the authors.
- **Analyst inference:** Evaluation remains confined to three benchmarks with binary outcomes and historical pools from 16–37 agent configurations; broader task types and scoring schemes are untested.
- **Analyst inference:** Thresholds are selected using an approximately two-point fidelity criterion per benchmark. The supplied text does not report uncertainty intervals or repeated-seed variability, so the stability of these operating points is unclear.
- **Analyst inference:** Table VI states that the LLM judge incurs a forward pass at each step, but it does not provide measured latency, energy, or dollar-cost comparisons; “negligible” LightGBM overhead is therefore supported qualitatively rather than by an end-to-end cost table.
- **Analyst inference:** Feature-family ablations are reported only on SWE-bench Verified, limiting conclusions about which signals drive performance on TerminalBench and Toolathlon.

## Open research questions

1. **How well does EarlyEval transfer to a newly introduced benchmark with few historical runs?** This remains open because the method assumes a mature labeled trajectory pool. A next experiment could measure accuracy and savings as the number and diversity of training agents are progressively reduced.

2. **Can predictors remain calibrated under genuinely novel scaffolds?** Scaffold exclusion notably weakened success prediction on TerminalBench. A next experiment could test online recalibration or scaffold-invariant representations on held-out scaffold families.

3. **Can the framework handle graded, continuous, or multi-criterion outcomes?** The paper formalizes only binary success and failure. A next experiment could replace the two binary heads with calibrated ordinal or continuous predictors and evaluate score and ranking distortion.

4. **What stopping policy gives statistical guarantees on benchmark-level error?** The reported thresholds empirically trade savings against fidelity but provide no stated formal error guarantee. A next experiment could compare threshold selection with conformal or sequential risk-control procedures under the same leave-one-agent-out protocol.
