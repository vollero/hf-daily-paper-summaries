# Institutional Newspapers Pipeline: Deriving billions of high quality tokens from historical newspapers

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-03.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.18972) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18972)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors release a modular historical-newspaper processing pipeline, supporting models, and a crop-level dataset containing 16.3 billion VLM-OCR tokens from 83.1 million crops across 1,473,635 pre-1931 Boston Public Library scans.

## Problem and motivation

Historical newspaper scans are difficult to process computationally because their layouts are dense and irregular, scan quality varies, and columns, decorative headings, tables, and damaged text challenge general-purpose OCR and layout tools. Existing extracted text may be adequate only for approximate keyword search rather than reliable large-scale analysis. This limits both digital-humanities research and the use of historical newspapers as language-model training data (Introduction, pp. 2–3).

The authors identify a need for an interpretable, customizable, institution-friendly pipeline that extracts atomic newspaper items and enriches them while remaining affordable enough for workstation-class deployment (Introduction, p. 3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Modular 15-step pipeline:** Segments each scan into type-agnostic rectangular crops, applies Tesseract and dots.mocr OCR, and adds text statistics, language, crop type, reading order, entities, subjects, thesaurus matches, and image/text embeddings. Steps remain separately inspectable and replaceable (Figure 1; Sections 3–13).

- **Separated detection and classification:** A YOLO26x detector identifies crops without assigning types. Two small classifiers—YOLO26m-cls for images and a Model2Vec text classifier—then provide complementary crop-type predictions combined by explicit rules (Sections 3.1 and 6.1).

- **Frugal reading-order method:** HDBSCAN column clustering plus content-aware geometric rules approximates crop-level reading order without a transformer (Section 7.1).

- **Released assets:** The authors report releasing the production pipeline, trained detection/classification models, and an open dataset containing scans, bounding boxes, two OCR outputs, annotations, metadata, and precomputed embeddings (Contributions, p. 4; Appendix E).

- **Large-scale institutional dataset:** Processing 1,473,635 public-domain scans dated 1795–1930 yielded 83,147,041 crops and 16,302,004,429 dots.mocr tokens (Abstract; Tables 3 and 5).

The evidence establishes strong in-collection segmentation performance, useful but imperfect reading-order recovery, large output scale, and measured throughput on one hardware configuration. It also shows that dots.mocr emits more text than Tesseract, but it does **not** establish superior OCR accuracy: no manually transcribed OCR benchmark or character/word error rate is reported. Likewise, agreement statistics do not demonstrate that the combined crop classifier is more accurate than either component.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Collection-specific generalization:** The models were developed and tested on a subset of one library’s collection. The authors expect difficulty on materially different scans and collections (Section 14).

- **OCR accuracy is not directly evaluated:** The comparison relies on token volume and coarse text statistics, not transcription ground truth. dots.mocr may hallucinate or enter repetition loops; token-loop removal is rule-based (Sections 4.1, 5.1, and 13).

- **Reading-order assumptions:** The clustering heuristic assumes substantially column-based layouts and cannot reliably handle deeply complex or unusual pages. Visual categories have especially low exact-position accuracy—for example, 0.494 macro for photographs/illustrations (Section 7.2; Table 6).

- **No evaluated combined-classifier gain:** The authors infer the relevance of combining image and text signals from overlap and decision-source statistics, but report no labeled test accuracy for the final rule-based combination (Section 6.2; Table 5). This is an analyst-identified evidentiary gap.

- **Potential label noise:** Crop-type classifiers train largely on VLM-generated labels. A 600-item check found 91% strict accuracy, so errors in supervision remain possible (Section 6.1).

- **Experimental enrichment:** NER may fail on historical names and noisy OCR or mislabel harmful terms; zero-shot subjects have broad confidence distributions; thesaurus matches are explicitly naive and non-interpretive; generic static embeddings may miss semantic ambiguity (Sections 8–11).

- **Language imbalance and metadata substitution:** English dominates the dataset, while low-confidence or short-text language predictions—and unsupported Yiddish detections—are replaced with issue-level metadata (Table 2; Section 13).

- **Cost portability:** Runtime and rental-cost figures depend on one pipeline implementation, one 8×L40S configuration, batching choices, and an assumed hourly price (Section 12; Appendix D).

- **Representational and rights risks:** The collection can contain offensive, biased, or inaccurate historical material and is not a balanced topical corpus. Public-domain determinations are made in good faith for the United States and may not transfer to other jurisdictions (Rights determination and Disclaimers, pp. 23–25).

## Open research questions

1. **How accurate is crop-level VLM OCR relative to Tesseract?** The paper compares output volume and tokenizability but lacks transcription ground truth. A next experiment could measure character and word error rates on a stratified, manually transcribed sample spanning decades, languages, scan quality, and crop types.

2. **Does the combined crop-type rule improve accuracy?** Component classifiers are evaluated separately, while the final fusion is assessed only through agreement. A labeled test could compare image-only, text-only, current heuristic fusion, and a learned fusion model using identical examples.

3. **How well does the pipeline transfer across institutions?** Evaluation is confined to BPL material. The pipeline could be tested without retraining and after limited adaptation on collections from several libraries, reporting performance by layout, language, period, and scan quality.

4. **Can difficult layouts receive better reading order without losing frugality?** The heuristic performs poorly on some visual and atypical layouts. A routed experiment could apply a transformer only to low-confidence or structurally complex pages and compare accuracy, runtime, and cost against the current method.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported pipeline design, dataset scale, in-collection evaluation, and stated limitations because the supplied text is complete and provides detailed tables and settings.

Material missing information includes OCR character/word error rates; a labeled evaluation of final classifier fusion; cross-collection validation; inter-annotator agreement; detailed subject-classification accuracy; language-detection accuracy; downstream retrieval or model-training results; and a complete empirical accounting of energy use or actual monetary cost.
