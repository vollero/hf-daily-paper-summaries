# Post-Training Language Models for Gold-Medal Performance in Coding Competitions

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-03.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.02849) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02849)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors report that synthetic-trace SFT, executable-reward RL, and feedback-driven test-time refinement produce gold-level competitive-programming systems, culminating in an unofficial live IOI 2026 run scoring 535.4/600—above both the 361.12 gold threshold and the top human score of 498.27.

## Problem and motivation

Competitive programming tests algorithm design, constraint reasoning, implementation correctness, and adaptation to hidden-test feedback. The authors argue that although recent systems achieve medal-level results, the effects of training data, post-training, model scale, and inference-time compute are difficult to isolate because many systems are closed or change several components simultaneously (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

The paper therefore studies an end-to-end, comparatively inspectable specialization pipeline and asks how much performance comes from synthetic-data SFT, executable-reward RL, larger model scale, and iterative test-time correction.

## Approach and contributions

- **Curated executable corpus:** The authors collect 22,000 competition problems, validate their environments, and exclude and deduplicate IOI 2025, ICPC 2025, and LiveCodeBench Pro evaluation problems from training (Section 3.1; Appendix A, pp. 3–4, 15–16).
- **Model specialization:** Nano-CC is trained with 1.2 million synthetic reasoning traces followed by GRPO with binary execution rewards; Ultra-CC receives SFT on 477,642 traces but no competition-specific RL (Sections 3.2–3.3, pp. 4–5).
- **GenCorrect:** The new inference procedure repeatedly generates up to 200 candidates, clusters them by code similarity, submits 10 diverse representatives, and conditions later rounds on subtask-level evaluator feedback and complementary prior solutions (Section 3.4; Figure 4, pp. 4–5).
- **Component analysis:** Experiments compare SFT, RL, model scale, parallel sampling, and iterative refinement across IOI 2025, ICPC 2025, and LiveCodeBench Pro (Sections 4.2–4.5, pp. 6–9).
- **Prospective competition system:** A competition-specific Ultra-CC variant combines GLM-5.2-generated SFT data, NVFP4 quantization, and an expanded 1,000-candidate final round for a live IOI 2026 run (Section 5, pp. 10–12).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Nano post-training substantially improves single-sample performance. | IOI 2025 Score@1 rises from 130/600 (21.7%) to 291/600 (48.5%); ICPC 2025 Pass@1 from 16.9% to 51.0%; LCB Pro Pass@1 from 17.6% to 71.6%. | Table 1 and Section 4.2, p. 6 |
| SFT provides most of Nano-CC’s post-training gain. | Across three SFT epochs, normalized IOI Score@1 increases 21.7%→47.3%, ICPC Pass@1 16.9%→46.7%, and LCB Pro Pass@1 17.6%→70.7%. | Figure 6 and Section 4.3, p. 7 |
| RL adds smaller gains after SFT. | From the third-epoch checkpoint, GRPO raises IOI Score@1 46.7%→48.5%, ICPC Pass@1 47.3%→51.0%, and LCB Pro Pass@1 70.7%→71.6%; checkpoint selection used held-out validation. | Figure 7a and Section 4.4, pp. 7–8 |
| RL without SFT helps but does not recover SFT’s gains under the tested budget. | IOI Score@1 improves 21.7%→24.9% after 30 RL steps from the base model, versus 48.7% when RL starts after the third SFT epoch. | Figure 7b and Section 4.4, p. 8 |
| GenCorrect produces gold-level IOI 2025 performance. | Over five rounds, mean score rises 360.6→468.2 for Nano-CC and 343.9→502.0 for Ultra-CC; IOI 2025 gold threshold is 438.3. Results use five runs per round. | Figure 8a and Section 4.5, pp. 8–9 |
| Iterative gains are smaller with binary ICPC feedback. | Nano-CC improves from 8.6 to 9.4 solved problems and Ultra-CC from 9.0 to 9.6; both plateau by early rounds, with nine solves matching the fourth-place gold team. | Figure 8b and Section 4.5, p. 9 |
| The live IOI 2026 system exceeds the reported human benchmarks. | A single prospective run scores 535.4/600, versus a 361.12 gold threshold and 498.27 top-human score, under matched time, internet, platform, and submission constraints. | Table 4 and Section 5.3, pp. 11–12 |
| Standard post-competition GenCorrect also exceeds the top-human score on average. | Five independent IOI 2026 runs average 521.72, with observed range 495.0–545.8. | Table 4 and Section 5.3, p. 12 |
| Quantization trades accuracy for throughput in the live configuration. | Against BF16 at 59.4% Score@1 and 199.1 tokens/s/GPU, selected NVFP4 achieves 52.8% and 736.8 tokens/s/GPU: −6.6 percentage points for approximately 3.7× throughput. | Table 3 and Section 5.2, p. 11 |

The evidence supports the authors’ claim that each tested pipeline stage can improve competitive-programming performance, with SFT dominating single-sample gains and GenCorrect driving the largest submission-budget gains. It also establishes that the reported live system’s score exceeded the official top human score numerically.

It does **not** establish an equal-resource comparison with humans, causal attribution among all IOI 2026 adaptations, or broad generalization beyond competitive programming. The live run was unofficial, unsupervised by IOI, and absent from the official rankings (footnote, p. 2).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Training and inference require substantial compute; the live system used up to 760 GB300 GPUs, so the human comparison matches contest constraints but not resources (Section 5.1, p. 10; Limitations, p. 13).
- **Author-stated:** Compute limits prevented Ultra-scale RL and exhaustive ablations across scales and training stages (Limitations, p. 13).
- **Author-stated:** Results may not generalize outside competitive programming (Limitations, p. 13).
- **Author-stated:** The full training corpus cannot be redistributed because of third-party restrictions, limiting exact reproducibility (Limitations, p. 13).
- **Author-stated:** The IOI 2026 run was unofficial and unsupervised by IOI, despite using the reported contest constraints (footnote, p. 2).
- **Analyst inference:** The single live run cannot isolate whether its advantage came from GLM-5.2 data, expanded final-round selection, quantization-enabled sampling volume, or run-to-run variation. The standard pipeline’s 495.0–545.8 range includes the live score (Section 5.3, pp. 11–12).
- **Analyst inference:** Comparisons between Nano and Ultra do not cleanly identify model-scale effects because their initialization, trace count, epoch count, and RL exposure differ (Sections 3.2–3.3; Table 5).
- **Analyst inference:** The claim of being the first AI to outscore the top IOI contestant is qualified as “to the authors’ knowledge”; the supplied experiments cannot independently prove historical priority.

## Open research questions

1. **Does competition-specific RL improve Ultra-CC?** It was omitted because of compute cost, leaving its marginal value unknown. A next experiment could run a smaller-budget Ultra RL sweep against the same SFT checkpoint and held-out evaluation protocol.

2. **Which IOI 2026 adaptation caused the live-run gain?** The adaptations were deployed together, and the live result remains within the standard pipeline’s five-run range. A factorial evaluation on held-out contests could separately vary teacher data, final-round pool size, selection method, and quantization.

3. **Can GenCorrect retain its gains with much less compute?** Current rounds generate 200 candidates, while the live final round uses 1,000. A compute–performance curve varying candidates, rounds, and submissions could identify the most efficient allocation.

4. **How well does the approach transfer beyond contest programming?** The paper explicitly limits its generalization claim. A plausible next experiment would test the same SFT/RL/refinement pipeline on repository-level repair or software-engineering tasks with executable feedback.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high.** The supplied text contains methods, training configurations, evaluation protocols, principal tables, appendices, and explicit limitations.

Material unavailable or unresolved includes the complete redistributable training corpus, independent supervision of the live IOI run, exhaustive cross-scale ablations, Ultra-CC RL results, confidence intervals for most benchmark comparisons, and evidence that the planned checkpoints and runnable recipes had actually been released at the time of writing.
