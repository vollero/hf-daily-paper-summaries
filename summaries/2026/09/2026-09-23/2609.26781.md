# Agensh: Scaling Organizational Intelligence to 1,024 Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-23.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.26781) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26781)


## One-sentence takeaway
Agensh’s decentralized worker harness improves mean test-pass rate from 19.31% to 28.78% when scaling from 1 to 128 agents on five difficult software-reconstruction tasks under a six-hour deadline, but compute efficiency and superiority over centralized coordination remain untested (§3.1).

## Problem and motivation
Sequential agents face latency and context constraints on complex tasks. The authors argue that centralized orchestrators introduce another bottleneck by managing task allocation, coordination, and integration. Agensh addresses how workers can discover tasks and combine contributions asynchronously without a designated central coordinator (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Decentralized cooperation protocol:** Identically prompted workers repeatedly gather context, claim tasks, act, verify results, and merge contributions. The protocol is implemented through prompts above an existing single-agent harness (§§2.1, 2.3).
- **Shared coordination infrastructure:** Git-based workspaces, asynchronous messaging, and searchable shared context support integration, conflict resolution, and reuse of findings. Shared context explicitly builds on DeLM; the contribution is the combined harness and its implementation, rather than inventing every component (§2.2).
- **Scaling study:** Experiments vary worker count on ProgramBench, with a larger pandoc experiment and qualitative examples of workers developing integration workflows and specialized roles (§3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Higher mean final performance | On five selected ProgramBench tasks, canonical-kept pass rate averages **19.31%, 20.68%, 26.52%, and 28.78%** for **1, 8, 32, and 128 agents**, respectively; same GPT-5.6-sol high model, Copilot harness, and six-hour deadline. The reported gain is **9.47 percentage points**, approximately **49% relative**. | §3.1, Fig. 5; Appendix C |
| Earlier attainment of a performance threshold | On pandoc, **128, 32, and 8 agents** first exceed **30% test-pass rate** at the **30-, 60-, and 90-minute checkpoints**, respectively; the single-agent run remains below it during the first **two hours**. | §3.1, Fig. 6 |
| Further gains at the largest scale | On pandoc under the six-hour deadline, final test-pass rates are **33.89%, 50.94%, and 55.06%** for **1, 128, and 1,024 agents**; the largest configuration adds **4.12 percentage points** over 128 agents. | §3.1, Fig. 1 |
| Qualitative coordination changes | Selected trajectories describe interface agreements, peer correction, reusable integration protocols, and multiple specialized integrators as organization size increases. | §3.2 |

The evidence supports improved deadline-constrained performance for the reported configurations. It does not establish equal-compute efficiency, a causal advantage over centralized orchestration, or general intelligence gains beyond software reconstruction. Coordination examples illustrate behaviors but do not quantify their prevalence or contribution to scores.

## Limitations and validity risks
The paper provides no dedicated limitations section. The following are **analyst assessments** grounded in the reported setup:

- **Narrow generalization:** Only five deliberately difficult ProgramBench tasks are evaluated, with the largest organization tested only on pandoc (§3; Appendix C).
- **Unisolated design contributions:** No centralized-orchestrator comparison or ablation of messaging, shared context, or the cooperation protocol is reported.
- **Unknown resource efficiency:** Token consumption, financial cost, and aggregate compute are absent. A fixed deadline permits substantially different resource use across worker counts.
- **Activation affects interpretation:** Workers start progressively under a common cutoff, so nominal organization size does not mean every worker operates for the full budget. Activation scheduling is not ablated (Appendix C).
- **Unreported uncertainty:** Repeated-run counts, score variance, and confidence intervals are unavailable.
- **Qualitative emergence evidence:** Selected trajectories do not establish that particular roles require particular scales or cause the performance gains (§3.2).

## Open research questions
- **Does decentralization outperform centralized coordination at matched resources?** The paper lacks that comparison; test both designs using the same model, tasks, token budget, and deadline.
- **Which infrastructure components drive improvement?** Their effects are not isolated; ablate shared context, messaging, and claim protocols while tracking pass rate and integration failures.
- **Does the largest-scale improvement generalize?** Only pandoc reaches the largest configuration; repeat that experiment across the other targets with multiple runs.
- **How much does activation scheduling matter?** All experiments use staggered starts; compare schedules while matching aggregate worker time and measuring contention and performance.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly specifies the architecture and headline results, but causal and generalization claims have weaker support.

Material missing information includes per-run variability, complete numerical curves and per-task scores, resource consumption, infrastructure capacity measurements, component ablations, centralized baselines, and systematic coordination statistics. A code link is supplied, but its contents and reproducibility were not verified.
