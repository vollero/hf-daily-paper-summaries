# ImIR: Image-Instruction Tuning for All-in-One Image Restoration

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-23.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.25267) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25267)


## One-sentence takeaway
ImIR predicts image-derived restoration instructions for a frozen image editor, reporting gains over matched text conditioning across six tasks and near-equivalent performance without degradation labels, while leaving generalization untested (§§4.3–4.4, 4.8).

## Problem and motivation
All-in-one restoration must remove different degradations without maintaining a separate model for each task. The authors argue that text-conditioned editors require prompt engineering and provide coarse task descriptions that miss image-specific degradation cues. Their proposed alternative predicts the unavailable clean image’s instruction embedding directly from the degraded input (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Image-derived conditioning:** Qwen-Image-Edit receives degraded-image structure through its VAE and a predicted clean-image embedding through its instruction pathway, with empty text conditioning (§§3.2–3.4).
- **Residual token mapper:** A shared per-token MLP predicts the clean-minus-degraded embedding residual. FiLM modulation incorporates pooled image context and, optionally, task identity. The mapper first learns from paired embeddings; it is then frozen while a shared LoRA adapter learns restoration through flow matching (§§3.4–3.5).
- **Label-free restoration:** A separately trained variant replaces task identities with one shared slot, retaining image context to infer the restoration needed (§3.6).
- **Continuous control:** Scaling the predicted residual changes restoration strength, demonstrated through low-light exposure and dehazing examples (§§3.7, 4.6).

The distinctive contribution is predicting the current image’s clean-target tokens in an editor’s native conditioning space; the pretrained editor and LoRA are adopted components (§2).

## Evidence and main results

The numerical findings below are author-reported in the prose; the supplied text contains table captions but omits the table bodies.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Image instructions improve matched text conditioning | On LOLv2-real, ImIR achieves **21.3 dB PSNR versus 16.3 dB** for Text LoRA on the same backbone; authors report improvements across all six tasks. | §4.3; Table 1 referenced |
| Gains extend across individual images | At a fixed seed, ImIR wins on **88–91%** of low-light images across PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS. Across six tasks and three metrics, **15/18** paired sign tests meet **p < 0.05**. | §4.3; Table 2 referenced |
| Removing task labels preserves image-conditioned performance | Task-agnostic versus task-aware PSNR: **21.2 versus 21.3 dB** on LOLv2-real; **33.0 versus 33.2 dB** on Rain100L. Neutral-prompt Text LoRA falls from **32.5 to 17.5 dB** on Rain100L. | §4.4 |
| Mapper and global context matter | Across six tasks, the mapper adds **2.6–7.0 dB PSNR** over raw degraded embeddings and closes at least **82%** of the identity-to-oracle PSNR gap. Removing pooled context and retraining the adapter costs **1.2–4.9 dB**. | §4.7; Table 3 referenced |
| Full-data methods retain fidelity advantages | On LOLv2-real, InstructIR reaches **31.2 dB PSNR**, versus ImIR’s **21.3 dB**; on Rain100L, AdaIR reaches **37.9 dB**, versus **33.2 dB**. Training regimes differ. | §4.2 |
| Adaptation is relatively short, but inference is costly | **688 pairs**, approximately **3 hours on one H100**, and **326M trainable parameters**: 235M adapter plus 91M mapper. Inference takes **14 seconds/image** at approximately one megapixel, batch size one, and 30 sampling steps. | §4.8 |
| Instruction scaling provides qualitative control | Low-light and haze examples change with residual scaling; mean luma and CAP readouts accompany the examples, but numerical values are absent from the supplied text. | §4.6; Figure 4 caption |

The strongest evidence supports image-derived conditioning within this editor and benchmark setup. Mapper ablations strengthen that interpretation. It does not establish universal superiority over text conditioning, compute-normalized efficiency, or robustness to unseen degradations. External baseline comparisons involve different training data and objectives (§§4.2, 4.8).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** No dedicated out-of-distribution or real-world generalization study; a dedicated blind benchmark remains future work (§§4.4, 4.8).
- **Author-stated:** Iterative sampling is slow, and the generative prior can produce plausible but unfaithful detail (§5).
- **Author-stated:** Full-data methods lead on several fidelity benchmarks; efficiency claims are restricted to adaptation data and training time, without compute normalization (§§4.2, 4.8).
- **Analyst inference:** Fixed-seed comparisons do not establish stability across training or sampling seeds. The reported sign tests measure paired image wins, not run-to-run uncertainty (§4.3).
- **Analyst inference:** The few controllability examples do not establish consistent control or fidelity across a dataset. Claims that visual conditioning uniquely explains label-free performance remain specific to the tested text alternatives (§§4.4, 4.6).

## Open research questions
- **Does label-free restoration generalize to unseen or combined degradations?** Dedicated generalization evidence is absent. Test held-out corruption types, severities, and combinations against the task-aware variant (§4.8).
- **How reliable is the instruction-strength control?** Evidence is example-based. Sweep the scale across full low-light and haze test sets, measuring fidelity, exposure/haze indicators, and human preferences (§4.6).
- **Can faster sampling preserve restoration quality and control?** Inference cost motivates the authors’ proposed distillation work. Compare distilled samplers with the original using latency, restoration metrics, and scale-response consistency (§5).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The method and central comparisons are clearly described, but complete Tables 1–3 and inspectable figure content are unavailable in the supplied text. Other material gaps include per-task training allocations, most test-set sizes, exact mapper dimensions, training duration in steps, JPEG compression settings, repeated-run uncertainty, and code/checkpoint release information.
