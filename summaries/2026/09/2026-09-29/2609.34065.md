# Who Gets a Token, and What Does It Carry? Unequal Name Support and Concept Access in Large Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-29.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.34065) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34065)


## One-sentence takeaway
Among first names matched on observed demographic and lexical properties, single-token support predicts task-relevant internal accessibility differences, but the paper does not establish tokenization as their isolated cause or those differences as final behavioral disparities.

## Problem and motivation
Name-based fairness evaluations often change only a person’s name while assuming the resulting inputs are comparable. The authors argue that demographic matching misses a lexical difference: some names enter a model as one learned token, while others require multiple subwords.

The identified gap is whether this unequal lexical access remains a vocabulary property or appears in task-relevant internal computation and influences subsequent choices (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Lexical-access audit:** Measure exact, lossless single-token access across 497,583 single-word first-name surfaces and 12 model-associated tokenizer rows; analyze demographic associations after controlling for frequency and length (§3; Appendix A.1).
- **NAMETRACE readout:** Score intermediate-layer probabilities over task-specific adjectives using continuous SentiWordNet weights and task orientation. Clinical concern reverses generic sentiment polarity. Axes and one readout layer per model are selected on development names and frozen for evaluation (§4.1; Appendices A.5–A.7).
- **Matched evaluation:** Compare 200 atomic–short-fragmented pairs within eight race/ethnicity–gender-associated strata, splitting them equally into development and held-out sets. Matching uses frequency, length, demographic shares, and weak orthographic cues (§4; Appendix A.3).
- **Transfer and intervention tests:** Subtract development-set support priors from unseen-name gaps, then separately edit name-span hidden states along adjective-derived task directions to test effects on constrained choices (§5).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic access is selective and tokenizer-dependent | Among 497,583 surfaces, 23,095 are atomic in at least one tokenizer and 4,052 in all 12; any-surface counts range from 4,998 for DeepSeek to 20,020 for Aya. Tests include casing and leading-space variants. | §3.1; Table 11 |
| Demographic associations remain after lexical controls | On 7,469 controlled names, adjusted odds ratios for any-tokenizer access are 3.36 for male versus female, 0.42 for NH Black versus NH White, 0.47 for Hispanic versus NH White, and 1.55 for Asian/PI versus NH White, controlling for standardized log frequency and length. | Table 1; Appendix A.3 |
| Held-out intermediate gaps are positive when pooled | Across three primary models and evidence conditions, atomic-minus-fragmented weighted gaps are fellowship 0.131 [95% CI 0.096, 0.168], hiring 0.072 [0.055, 0.091], clinical concern 0.059 [0.048, 0.072], and lending 0.051 [0.041, 0.061]. These are semantic scores, not decision probabilities. | Table 2 |
| Architecture changes magnitude and direction | Qwen’s evidence-averaged gaps range from 0.154 to 0.366; Llama’s from 0.002 to 0.026. Ministral’s lending gap is −0.003 [−0.004, −0.002]. In the eight-model extension, 23/32 intermediate model–task means are positive, with 20 intervals excluding zero positively. | Tables 14 and 16; Appendix C.4 |
| Positive intermediate gaps need not survive to output | Using the same adjective score at native output logits, pooled hiring becomes −0.100 [−0.150, −0.060] and clinical concern −0.213 [−0.278, −0.150]; lending remains positive at 0.081 [0.052, 0.111]. | Table 21 |
| Support priors transfer across disjoint names | Across 36 model–task–evidence cells, development and held-out gaps correlate at Pearson r=0.992. Mean absolute residual is 0.0094 with matched priors versus a shuffled-prior median of 0.0364 over 10,000 within-model permutations, p=0.0001. | Appendix D.1; Figure 11 |
| Task-direction edits affect later choices | On a separate 200-pair inventory, forward-minus-reverse choice-probability contrasts are Qwen 0.153 [0.147, 0.160], Llama 0.155 [0.153, 0.158], and Ministral 0.094 [0.089, 0.099]. Directions are promise/intelligence for Qwen and Llama, competence for Ministral; specificity is weaker at Ministral’s selected layer. | Table 24; Appendix D.2 |

The evidence supports an association between lexical support and internal task-axis scores, predictable across held-out names. Interventions establish that selected task directions can influence constrained choices. They do **not** establish that atomicity causes the observed gaps, that every model shares their direction, or that unedited open-ended responses exhibit corresponding disparities.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Name-specific pretraining exposure is unobserved, preventing an isolated causal interpretation of atomicity (Appendix F).
- **Author-stated:** Controlled experiments emphasize sufficiently frequent, single-word ASCII first names with strong aggregate metadata associations; other scripts, naming conventions, and identity cues remain outside scope (Appendix F).
- **Author-stated:** Compact adjective axes measure targeted accessibility before unrestricted generation; later computation can redirect the signal (Appendix F; Table 21).
- **Analyst inference:** Pooled estimates obscure substantial architecture dependence, including negative cases. Cross-model score magnitudes should therefore be interpreted cautiously (Tables 14 and 16).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Table 15’s post-trained Qwen profile differs from the primary Qwen profile in Table 14 without a fully explicit reconciliation. Tables 19 and 20 also give differing marginal summaries.
- **Reproducibility gap:** The supplied text does not fully specify intermediate-state probability projection, intervention-direction normalization, or development scale selection. Aggregate data release is promised, not confirmed (Appendix D.2; Ethics Statement).

## Open research questions
1. **Does atomicity itself cause accessibility differences?** Matching leaves training exposure uncontrolled. A next experiment could vary tokenization while holding name exposure and training conditions fixed.
2. **Do internal gaps predict unedited user-facing treatment?** Output sign reversals and constrained interventions leave this unresolved. Test held-out names in open-ended interactions and compare internal scores with response-level outcomes.
3. **How robust are results to axis and layer choices?** Axes and layers are development-selected. Repeat evaluation with independently defined concept axes and prespecified layer ranges.
4. **Do findings generalize across naming systems?** Controlled coverage is narrow. Build matched inventories for non-ASCII, multiword, and additional language settings.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text provides extensive protocols, uncertainty estimates, and sensitivity analyses, but supports observational and controlled-computation conclusions more strongly than causal or behavioral ones.

Material missing information includes confirmed asset availability, complete name inventories and matching-balance diagnostics, exact readout and intervention implementation details, reconciliation of differing summaries, and unrestricted behavioral evaluation.
