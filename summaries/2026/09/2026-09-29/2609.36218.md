# CineSubBench: Evaluating LLMs on Long-Form Narrative and Cultural Understanding from Multilingual Movie Subtitles

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-29.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.36218) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36218)


## One-sentence takeaway
CineSubBench evaluates matched multilingual subtitle understanding across narrative, audience-rating, and lexical-safety tasks, finding uneven model capabilities while leaving prior film knowledge and subtitle variation as material validity risks (§§3–5; Appendix I.1).

## Problem and motivation
The authors identify film as an underexplored LLM evaluation domain requiring long-range narrative integration, multilingual interpretation, and culturally situated judgments. Subtitles preserve dialogue and temporal order but generally omit speaker identities, scene structure, actions, and explicit causal links, requiring models to reconstruct narrative structure rather than summarize continuous exposition (§§1–2).

Their proposed gap is a common evaluation setting covering narrative generation, classification, national audience guidance, and indexed safety evidence over the same films, languages, and models (§2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Subtitle-based task setting:** Seven tasks cover plot, spoiler-aware synopsis, key message, genre, age suitability, country-specific ratings, and subtitle-grounded language safety (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Matched benchmark:** CineSubBench contains 1,012 films, 6,072 tracks, and 8.13M subtitle entries, with English, Arabic, Indonesian, Persian, Romanian, and Vietnamese tracks and ten national rating systems per film. Construction combines entity resolution, complete-coverage selection, subtitle verification, and reference curation (§3; Appendices A.2–A.7).
- **Evaluation and diagnostics:** Nine LLMs are compared using task-specific metrics, a common reference-based narrative judge, paired cross-lingual analyses, country-specific baselines, and indexed safety evidence. Additional probes examine title/year knowledge and character-name sensitivity (§4; Appendices B.6, C–F).

The contribution is an evaluation asset and protocol; the paper does not introduce a new trained model.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

| Film identity provides substantial alternative task signal | On 50 paired English films and five models, title/year-only genre micro-F1 exceeds subtitle-only performance by 2.5–11.0 percentage points. | Appendix B.6; Table 21 |

These results support distinct capability profiles within the tested setting. They do not establish complete audiovisual film understanding, isolate the causes of language differences, or prove that narrative scores arise solely from subtitle reasoning. The authors’ film-clustered uncertainty estimates also exclude run-to-run generation variability (Appendix C.2).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated modality boundary:** Subtitles omit visual action, music, gestures, and other non-speech evidence; ratings and narrative references can therefore concern information unavailable in the input (Appendix I.1).
- **Author-stated language confounding:** Comparisons combine translation quality, tokenization, subtitle density, and model proficiency. Matched films do not make the tracks semantically identical (Appendix I.1).
- **Author-stated selection boundary:** Complete coverage favors a particular film cohort; the benchmark does not characterize global cinema (Appendix I.1).
- **Author-stated reference and safety scope:** Labels retain source conventions and normalization choices. Safety gold covers four English lexical categories, excluding gestures and broadly defined derogatory language (Appendices A.6–A.7, I.1).
- **Analyst inference—knowledge attribution:** Withholding titles limits explicit identity cues but cannot demonstrate absence of memorized film knowledge. The title/year and name-perturbation probes expose this concern without quantifying its contribution to the full benchmark (Appendix B.6).
- **Analyst inference—judging and compression:** Agreement between two LLM judges does not establish human validity. Requested synopses are much shorter than many references, potentially affecting omission judgments; the supplied text does not isolate this effect (Tables 10, 17; Appendix B.3).
- **Source limitation:** The supplied source is truncated, including an omitted middle passage; this analysis does not imply complete coverage.

## Open research questions
1. **How much performance depends on prior film knowledge?** Identity-only performance remains competitive on a small probe. A next experiment could use unfamiliar films and systematically altered narrative details, then measure whether outputs follow the supplied evidence (Appendix B.6).
2. **What causes the Persian performance gap?** Existing comparisons combine model and subtitle properties. A next experiment could compare expert-verified, semantically aligned translations with controlled subtitle density and input formatting (Appendix I.1).
3. **How much do missing modalities constrain audience-rating prediction?** National labels describe films beyond dialogue. A next experiment could compare subtitle-only and audiovisual conditions on identical films and rating targets (Appendix I.1).
4. **Can mild-obscenity assessment improve with independently annotated gold?** Current gold uses glossary-derived rules plus discrepancy audits. A next experiment could collect independent contextual human labels and separate boundary disagreements from extraction failures (Appendix A.6; Table 52).
