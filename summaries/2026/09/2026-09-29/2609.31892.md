# NVAlign: Direct-Gradient Optimization for Non-Verbal Control in Continuous Autoregressive Flow Matching Text-to-Speech

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-29.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.31892) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31892)


## One-sentence takeaway
NVAlign improves human-rated non-verbal tag-following on three continuous autoregressive flow-matching TTS systems’ listening sets, but gains are not uniformly significant and do not consistently improve expressive quality or intelligibility (§4.2).

## Problem and motivation
TTS systems can accept inline non-verbal vocalization (NVV) tags yet omit or confuse the requested sounds, partly because diverse annotated examples are scarce. The authors identify a gap in post-training methods for NVV control in continuous autoregressive flow-matching (AR-FM) TTS and investigate whether a recognition model can supply useful gradients directly through speech generation (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **NVV-specific direct-gradient post-training:** Fine-tune TTS and NVV-aware ASR on tagged speech, then freeze ASR and optimize its teacher-forced target-tag probabilities. Multi-token tags use their minimum token probability (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Joint model adaptation:** Apply a borrowed two-step gradient surrogate to update LoRA adapters in the autoregressive backbone, flow-head input projections, and flow head through a frozen decoder (§3.3).
- **Constrained optimization:** Combine the recognition reward with speaker, predicted-quality, waveform peak/level, and reference-velocity penalties to constrain drift (§§3.2–3.3).
- **Empirical evaluation:** Compare against SFT and Flow-GRPO across VoxCPM2, dots.tts, and an English production system (§4).

The contribution is the NVV-focused formulation and integration for continuous AR-FM TTS; the paper explicitly attributes the surrogate and velocity regularization to prior methods (§§2.2, 3.3).

These results support improved tag presence in several evaluated settings, especially English production TTS. They do not establish uniform benchmark superiority or simultaneous gains in expressiveness, naturalness, and intelligibility. Gemini scores are comparable only within each comparison group (§4.1).

## Limitations and validity risks
**Author-stated:** Sparse NVV supervision can weaken the reward model. Optimizing recognition likelihood can exploit shortcuts or damage prosody; multiple constraints introduce gradient-balancing challenges. Continuous or overlapping vocalizations fall outside the discrete-event formulation (§5).

**Analyst inferences from the reported design:**

- Main Flow-GRPO comparisons change both the optimization method and trainable components: Flow-GRPO updates only the flow head, while NVAlign also updates the backbone. A flow-head-only ablation partially addresses this, but does not isolate every difference (§§4.1–4.2).
- Penalty removals are cumulative, limiting attribution of independent effects and interactions (Table 2 caption).
- Supported-tag filtering, Mandarin/English coverage, and proprietary production data limit generalization and reproducibility (§4.1).
- The supplied text contains Table 2’s caption but not its full numerical body, preventing complete ablation inspection.

## Open research questions
- **How much improvement comes from direct gradients versus joint adaptation?** The main comparison changes both. Next experiment: match trainable components, regularization, and training budgets across NVAlign and Flow-GRPO.
- **Does the reward generalize to rare vocalizations?** Scarcity affects both generation and recognition (§5). Next experiment: stratify independent human evaluation by tag frequency and test unseen speakers.
- **Can tag control improve without intelligibility or expressive-quality loss?** Reported metrics diverge (§4.2). Next experiment: sweep penalty weights and measure human tag accuracy, expressiveness, naturalness, and CER/WER together.
- **Can the method handle overlapping or continuous vocalizations?** These are explicitly outside scope (§5). Next experiment: introduce temporal annotations and evaluate overlapping-event control.
