# QwenGyre: An Elastic Reinforcement Learning Framework for Training xLong-Horizon Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-29.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.33848) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33848)


## One-sentence takeaway
QwenGyre combines elastic GPU scheduling with bounded trajectory processing to accelerate online RL for lengthy coding-agent executions, reporting up to 1.849× speedup over Colocate and 1.784× over Async under equal GPU budgets and matched mean scheduling staleness (§6.2, Table 2, Figure 8).

## Problem and motivation
Hours-long agent executions create rollout stragglers that leave GPUs idle: Colocate waits for the entire rollout pool, while Async cannot move resources across its fixed rollout/training partition. Compaction, delegation, and retries also create branching histories whose expansion repeats contexts and training targets (§§1–2).

The authors identify a need to preserve live black-box harness executions while reallocating GPUs, and to construct affordable training samples without changing outputs’ original conditioning contexts.

## Approach and contributions
- **Elastic scheduling:** Independently switchable GPU cells move from rollout to training as outstanding work falls. A proxy reroutes model requests while harnesses and workspaces persist; satellite cells join an ongoing batch using the core cell’s fixed parameter snapshot (§4).
- **Streaming training:** Cells claim disjoint micro-steps as capacity becomes available. The core owns optimizer state, aggregates gradients, and performs one update per batch (§4.4).
- **Bounded trajectory processing:** Exact token records form shared-prefix trees. Task evaluators score preserved work, including assessable timeout outcomes; role-prioritized sampling limits admitted paths, masks repeated policy targets, and normalizes losses within each execution (§5, Appendix B).

The claimed contribution is their integrated scheduling and trajectory-processing design. Token-in/token-out recording, prefix trees, and shared-target deduplication build on cited prior work rather than being introduced independently here (§7).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Faster training for the smaller model | Qwen 3.6 122B, NL2RepoBench, 48 steps: cumulative speedups over Async/Colocate were 1.528×/1.361× at mean scheduling staleness 1, and 1.423×/1.475× at 1.5; training-score curves were comparable. | §6.2, Figure 7 |
| Faster flagship-model training | Qwen 3.8 2.4T, NL2RepoBench, 48 steps: 75.42 h versus Async’s 134.55 h and Colocate’s 91.47 h, corresponding to 1.784× and 1.213× speedups. | §6.2, Figure 8 |
| Benchmark improvement during RL | Qwen 3.8 2.4T NL2RepoBench evaluation pass rate increased from 52.48% to 58.54% over 48 steps. | §6.2, Figure 8(f) |
| Speedups across additional workloads | Qwen 3.6 122B, 24 steps, staleness targets 1/1.5: DeepSWE speedups were 1.569×/1.572× over Async and 1.610×/1.824× over Colocate; TerminalBench achieved 1.383×/1.430× and 1.809×/1.849×, respectively. | Table 2 |
| Fine-grained allocation adds benefit | First 12 NL2RepoBench steps, Qwen 3.6 122B, staleness 1.5: elastic configuration E0 took 16.12 h versus 19.23 h for streaming coarse-switching configuration C2, a reported 1.19× speedup; allocations also differed. | Table 1, §6.3 |
| A trajectory cap reduces training computation | NL2RepoBench, Qwen 3.6 122B, steps 1–42: five admitted trajectories yielded comparable training scores to uncapped sampling while mean forward–backward time was 74.8% of uncapped time; main-only sampling had lower scores and larger gradient norms. | §6.3, Figure 9 |

These results support faster execution of the tested RL pipelines with comparable reported training scores. The flagship pass-rate improvement demonstrates learning during training, but does not isolate a learning-quality advantage of elastic scheduling over baseline schedulers. Statistical uncertainty and repeated-run variability are not reported.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Small GPU budgets may not accommodate multiple cells plus sufficient rollout capacity; substantially smaller total budgets remain unevaluated (§8).
- **Author-stated:** Streaming multi-step bursts prevent global shuffling across the burst’s minibatches. The effect of sample ordering on learning quality has not been isolated (§8).
- **Analyst inference:** Generalization beyond coding and terminal workloads or Claude Code is untested; all experiments use Claude Code 2.1.220 (§6.1).
- **Analyst inference:** Partial scoring, role priority, deduplication, and execution normalization lack separate component ablations. Figure 9 tests the trajectory budget, not each processing choice.
- **Comparison boundary:** All experimental baselines use boundary dispatch. Appendix A analyzes continuous dispatch under explicit assumptions, but does not provide an empirical continuous-dispatch comparison.
- **Reproducibility gap:** Training data are internally constructed, and the supplied text gives no release statement, GPU hardware specification, repeated-run uncertainty, or values for the scheduler’s supply threshold and transition timer (§§4.2, 6.1).

## Open research questions
- **How does elasticity scale down?** Smaller total GPU budgets remain unevaluated (§8). Next experiment: sweep total resources while preserving feasible model parallelism, measuring wall time and inference service continuity.
- **Does streaming order affect learning?** Its learning-quality effect is explicitly unisolated (§8). Next experiment: compare streaming admission with globally shuffled bursts using matched collected data and multiple seeds.
- **Which trajectory-processing choices matter most?** Only the path budget is ablated (§6.3). Next experiment: vary role priority, partial scoring, and loss normalization separately, measuring held-out pass rate and training cost.
- **How competitive is continuous dispatch?** Current experiments compare boundary-dispatch systems, while Appendix A’s timing approximations can fail under timeout clusters. Next experiment: compare both dispatch policies at matched measured staleness, recording backlog and wall time.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported method and numerical results; **medium** in broader generalization and causal attribution.

Material missing information includes training-data contents and sizes, release availability, GPU hardware, some scheduler settings, repeated-run uncertainty, detailed evaluator validation, and held-out baseline pass-rate comparisons.
