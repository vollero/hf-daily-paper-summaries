# Relic: From Multi-Agent Collaboration to Persistent Organizational Capability

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-29.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.32965) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32965)


## One-sentence takeaway
Relic converts collaboration failures into persistent, governed, executable rules, improving verified software delivery and fresh-member performance over matched controls, with higher token use in the main study (Tables 1–2).

## Problem and motivation
Multi-agent coding teams can make locally useful changes that fail during integration because interfaces, branches, reviews, or test evidence become stale. Conversations resolve immediate disagreements, but their obligations may disappear when members change.

The authors identify a gap between retaining knowledge or reusable procedures and making rules govern whoever occupies a role: persistent collaboration needs explicit triggers, responsibilities, evidence requirements, and execution consequences (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Persistent protocol state:** Relic separates member-local memory, shared work, and organization-owned protocols. Rules retain role-based obligations across member replacement (§3.1).
- **Governed rule lifecycle:** Members propose rules from visible work friction; validation and approval precede adoption, executable binding, amendment, or retirement. Main-study governance is semi-automatic, including bounded recovery for pending approvals (§3.2; Appendix C.4.2).
- **Runtime integration:** Protocols influence a fixed Structured Decision Layer’s action priorities, responsibility routing, and execution checks. Rule content evolves while model parameters, action handlers, and scoring code remain fixed (§3.3).
- **Mechanism-focused evaluation:** Matched lifecycle controls, prose-only ablation, content-matched fresh-member transfer, and external harness evaluations test distinct parts of the proposal (§4; Figure 3).
- **Exploratory human interface:** A liaison-first interface translates organizational state and human intent; its evidence consists of formative internal walkthroughs, rather than a broad user study (Appendix L).

These comparisons support the authors’ claim that executable organizational rules add value beyond a structured team and readable rules in the tested software settings. They do not establish universal organizational capability, isolate every lifecycle component, or establish a full-benchmark same-model advantage. The CooperBench “best reported” claim is an author claim tied to the validity-filtered comparison.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Evaluation centers on software production; broader domains, alternative decision layers, selective transfer, and forgetting remain future work (§7; Appendix B).
- **Resource comparison:** Main runs share a scheduling horizon but have no matched hard token, model-call, or primary-action ceiling; tool/container compute is unmetered. Matched-spend sensitivity is reported for two models (Appendices D.5, F.4.4; §5.2).
- **Analyst inference—transfer dependence:** Source rules were selected and normalized into supported runtime guards, including rewriting unobservable clauses. Transfer therefore demonstrates a curated, adapted package rather than arbitrary autonomous rule portability (Appendix G.1.1).
- **Analyst inference—external attribution:** Full CooperBench comparisons combine different models and harnesses. Validity auditing occurred alongside Relic execution, and exclusions include specification ambiguities; identical exclusions across systems improve comparability but do not remove policy sensitivity (Appendix M).
- **Case-study boundary:** Figure 6 explicitly states that the traced rule’s causal contribution is not isolated.
- **Human evidence boundary:** Interface findings come from four author-team participants and formative observations (Appendix L.6–L.7).
- **Coverage limitation:** The supplied source is truncated, with a substantial middle portion omitted; this analysis does not cover the complete paper.

## Open research questions
- **Which binding components drive improvement?** Existing controls remove binding collectively. A next experiment could separately disable action reprioritization, responsibility routing, and transition checks while holding rule content fixed (§3.3; §5.3).
- **When should inherited rules be revised or forgotten?** Transfer freezes target rules and uses adapted packages. Test domain shifts and partial turnover with frozen, adaptive, and selective-retirement treatments (Appendices G.3.4, B.4).
- **Does the full CooperBench advantage survive model and budget matching?** Full released-reference comparisons use different models, while same-model evidence covers a subset. Run all structures on the full benchmark with one model and common resource limits (Appendix M).
- **How should execution proceed during human deliberation?** Walkthroughs identify timing friction without a controlled policy comparison. Compare pausing, continuing reversible work, and selective blocking with external participants (Appendix L.7).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly supports the main reported comparisons and method, but truncation prevents complete verification.

Material unavailable information includes the omitted statistical procedures and detailed robustness tables, full rule-level ablation results, complete run artifacts and configuration records, public release/access status, and uncertainty estimates for the reported external benchmark gains.
