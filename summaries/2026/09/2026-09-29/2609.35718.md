# Hard Vision, Easy Vision: What GPT-6 Astra Reveals Across Computer Vision

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-29.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.35718) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35718)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across the authors’ evaluation of six systems, general-purpose vision is strongest in semantic reasoning and object-centric prediction, while metric geometry, faithful restoration, and fine-grained expert recognition retain substantial gaps to selected references (Table 1; §§3–5).

## Problem and motivation
Existing vision evaluations are scattered across tasks and model comparisons, making it difficult to distinguish leadership among generalists from specialist- or human-level performance. The authors seek a unified view of which visual capabilities general-purpose systems support, where dedicated models remain valuable, and whether reasoning or tools reduce the remaining gaps (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Comparative evaluation:** Six frontier systems evaluated across 34 capabilities, 55 benchmarks, and nine vision areas, using common instructions, visual inputs, and evaluation samples. Outputs include answers, masks, boxes, depth predictions, images, and embodied actions (§2).
- **Landscape synthesis:** Comparisons with task-specific models, prior generalist leaders, and available human scores; a maturity framework organizes performance relative to selected references (Table 1; §7).
- **Reasoning and tool analysis:** Selected experiments examine accuracy–cost tradeoffs from reasoning effort and tools. Image generation and editing use paired reasoning and image models rather than the reasoning model alone (§6; §8).

The contribution is an evaluation and synthesis, rather than a newly trained vision model or newly introduced dataset.

These results support the authors’ descriptive pattern across the selected evaluations. They do not establish that task properties causally determine difficulty, that benchmark success implies deployment readiness, or that all measured capabilities reside inside a single model. Reference selection and internal reporting inconsistencies also constrain interpretation.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Maturity tiers describe evaluated benchmarks, not solved tasks; comparisons depend on the available reference (§§2, 7).
- **Analyst inference—reproducibility:** The supplied text omits exact prompts, sample counts, subset definitions, aggregation weights, repeated-run variability, and detailed tool configurations (§8 supplies only partial implementation information).
- **Analyst inference—attribution:** Generation and editing use separate image models, while other predictions may involve tools or model-written code. Scores therefore need clearer separation by system configuration (§8).
- **Internal inconsistencies:** Table 1 reports a 6.5-point video-segmentation specialist gap, while §3 states 7.9. Table 1 puts segmentation above its reference, while §6 says added reasoning does not close the specialist gap without explaining a different setting.
- **Reference inconsistency:** Figure 9 places visual recognition at 126% of reference using a model reference, despite Table 1 reporting a stronger human score; this conflicts with Figure 9’s stated stronger-reference rule.
- **Analyst inference—uncertainty:** No confidence intervals or significance tests are supplied, limiting conclusions about small score differences.

## Open research questions
- **Which gains belong to the generalist itself?** Tool and image-model contributions are incompletely separated. Next experiment: compare direct inference, explicit tools, and specialist pipelines on identical samples and budgets.
- **Which reasoning budget offers the best tradeoff?** Figure 8 shows task-dependent, non-monotonic score–cost behavior. Next experiment: repeat effort sweeps with absolute cost, latency, and uncertainty reporting.
- **Do the proposed difficulty categories generalize?** The semantic-versus-precision boundary is observational. Next experiment: use matched tasks that vary geometric precision, temporal consistency, or category granularity while holding inputs fixed.
- **Can routing and verification preserve fidelity?** §7 proposes deciding when to call specialists and verify outputs, without evaluating a routing policy. Next experiment: compare learned routing with always-generalist and always-specialist policies under fidelity and cost constraints.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The broad evaluation pattern is supported by multiple reported comparisons, but precise rankings and maturity assignments are weakened by inconsistencies.

Material missing information includes complete benchmark-to-capability mappings, prompts and evaluated sample counts, aggregation rules, run variability, tool access per result, absolute costs and latency, specialist evaluation parity, and verified repository contents.
