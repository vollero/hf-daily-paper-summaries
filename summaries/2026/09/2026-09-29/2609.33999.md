# Rethinking Automated Voice Similarity by Shifting from EER to Embedding Geometry

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-29.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.33999) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33999)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across the tested speaker embedding models, effective dimensionality tracks human voice similarity judgments much better than verification EER, while dimension bottlenecks improve perceptual alignment at a substantial verification cost (§§5.1–5.3).

## Problem and motivation
TTS and voice conversion evaluations often use speaker embedding cosine similarity to estimate how closely generated speech resembles a reference voice. The authors question selecting these embeddings by speaker verification EER: binary identity discrimination may not capture graded similarity between different speakers. Their identified gap is how training objectives and embedding geometry affect agreement with human judgments (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Perceptual evaluation:** Define alignment, \(\rho_{\mathrm{align}}\), as Spearman correlation between embedding cosine similarities and mean listener ratings on different-speaker VoxSim pairs, excluding the identity shortcut introduced by same-speaker pairs (§3.1).
- **Controlled comparison:** Compare seven classification and metric learning objectives across five model conditions, using identity supervision and three seeds per condition (§§4.1–4.2).
- **Geometric analysis:** Apply the PCA eigenvalue participation ratio to normalized, centered speaker centroids to measure effective dimensionality, \(d_{\mathrm{eff}}\), and relate it to alignment (§3.2).
- **Intervention:** Retrain ECAPA-TDNN with narrower embeddings to test whether constraining dimensionality improves alignment (§5.3).

The contributions are the comparative evidence, geometric connection, and bottleneck intervention; the losses and dimensionality measure are adopted methods.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| EER scarcely tracks alignment | Across 35 training conditions, with three seeds averaged, Spearman(EER, alignment) = **+0.07**; EER uses VoxCeleb1-O and alignment uses different-speaker VoxSim pairs. Within-model correlations range from −0.68 to +0.61, with \(p \geq .11\). | §5.1; Table 2 caption |
| Objective changes improve alignment without verification loss in a reported example | ECAPA: AAM-Softmax → Angular Prototypical raises alignment from **0.111 ± 0.013 to 0.406 ± 0.007**, with both achieving **1.47% EER** on VoxCeleb1-O; three-seed results. | §5.1; Table 1 caption |
| Effective dimensionality strongly tracks alignment | Across the 35 seed-averaged conditions, Spearman(\(d_{\mathrm{eff}}\), alignment) = **−0.95**. Within-model correlations range from −0.71 to −1.00; four conditions have \(p \leq .007\), while ReDimNet-B2 has \(p=.088\). | §5.2; Table 2 caption |
| A bottleneck produces the largest reported alignment gain | ECAPA with AM-Softmax: width **192 → 3** raises alignment from **0.080 to 0.738**, reaching \(d_{\mathrm{eff}}=1.5\) and **20.5% VoxCeleb1-O EER**; three-seed means. | §5.3; Table 3 caption |
| Extreme compression can destabilize training | At ECAPA width 3, AAM-Softmax fails to converge in **two of three seeds**, with \(d_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx1.1\) and **EER >40%**; alignment also drops. | §5.3; Table 3 caption |

These results support a strong association between geometry and perceptual alignment within the tested setting, plus evidence that changing embedding width can improve alignment. They do not establish that low dimensionality alone guarantees perceptual quality: collapsed runs provide a counterexample. Nor do they directly validate the proposed criterion on synthesized speech.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-reported:** Narrow bottlenecks impair speaker discrimination and can cause training collapse (§5.3).
- **Analyst inference—generalization:** Perceptual evaluation uses one VoxCeleb-derived dataset and different-speaker pairs; transfer to generated speech, other populations, or other recording conditions remains untested (§§3.1, 4.3).
- **Analyst inference—objective attribution:** Classification and metric objectives use different batch configurations, so the comparison does not fully isolate loss formulation from batching (§4.2).
- **Analyst inference—geometric sufficiency:** Dimensionality is strongly correlated with alignment, but bottlenecks also change model capacity; no intervention holding capacity fixed while independently changing geometry is reported (§5.3).
- **Evidence availability:** Tables 1–3 have captions but no cell contents in the supplied text, limiting inspection of complete per-condition results.

## Open research questions
1. **Does the criterion transfer to synthesized speech?** The motivating TTS/VC use case is not directly evaluated. Next experiment: compare EER, dimensionality, and listener alignment on generated–reference speech pairs.
2. **Can alignment improve while retaining verification accuracy?** Severe bottlenecks raise EER (§5.3). Next experiment: test a dimensionality regularizer at fixed embedding width and measure the alignment–EER trade-off.
3. **How much of the objective effect comes from batching?** Batch configurations differ across loss families (§4.2). Next experiment: repeat objective comparisons with matched sampling and batch sizes.
4. **When does low dimensionality indicate useful structure versus collapse?** Collapsed AAM-Softmax runs have very low dimensionality but reduced alignment (§5.3). Next experiment: track alignment and geometric diagnostics throughout successful and failed training runs.
