# Distillation Defenses Easily Break After Reinforcement Learning

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-29.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.35699) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35699)


## One-sentence takeaway
In the tested small-model math settings, reinforcement learning after distillation often removes defense-induced performance gaps, enabling expanded reasoning summaries to approach full-trace training performance, although antidistillation remains effective in some settings.

## Problem and motivation
Distillation defenses are commonly evaluated immediately after an attacker trains on a teacher’s outputs. The authors argue that this misses subsequent reinforcement learning (RL), which can recover performance and benefit from imperfect reasoning traces. Their identified gap is whether defenses still reduce attacker capabilities after the full distillation-plus-RL pipeline (§§1–3).

This matters because restricting access to hidden reasoning may offer limited protection if API-visible summaries and answers provide enough information to bootstrap further training. Claims about actual industrial attack pipelines remain author hypotheses rather than observations established by these experiments.

## Approach and contributions
- **Threat-model evaluation:** Compare sequence-level distillation, GRPO-based RL, and distillation followed by RL across several model families using matched training questions. Combined training generally performs best where tested (§3; Appendix A).
- **Defense persistence test:** Evaluate antidistillation sampling before and after RL, showing that post-distillation degradation can disappear, while documenting a setting where it persists (§4; Appendix B).
- **Summary-expansion evaluation:** Use a prompted, untrained expander to reconstruct useful reasoning from summaries and answers, then distill and RL-train a student. Compare against full traces in open-source and two closed-source settings (§5; Appendix C).
- **Mechanistic support:** Ablations test expansion and expander-only training; theoretical analysis offers a mode-covering versus mode-seeking explanation, whose connection to observed performance is explicitly speculative (Appendices A.4, C.3).

Batch-level defenses are proposed as future work, not implemented or evaluated (§6.2).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

| RL does not universally outperform distillation. | Qwen2.5-Math-7B: distillation **84.7%** versus RL **79.3%** average accuracy on the same three benchmarks; authors attribute this to code-output hallucination reinforced by RL. | Table 1; Appendix A.3 |

| Antidistillation can retain effectiveness. | Llama-3.2-3B, harder training questions: post-RL average accuracy is **27.9%** unpoisoned, **22.3%** low poisoning and **21.4%** mild poisoning, versus **24.3%** RL-only. | Table 7, p. 24 |

| Closed-source results show similar hard-math performance. | Llama student after RL, full versus expanded traces: GPT-5 mini **21.9% vs 21.9%** Minerva, **17.2% vs 19.4%** MATH500; Claude Sonnet 4.6 **22.8% vs 21.7%**, **18.6% vs 19.2%**, respectively. Gemini expanded traces achieve **22.0% / 19.2%**, without a full-trace comparator. | Figure 8, p. 8 |
| Expansion matters in the tested open-source setup. | After RL, direct-summary distillation achieves **18.1% / 13.8%** Minerva/MATH500 accuracy; expanded summaries achieve **22.5% / 21.0%**. Expander-only traces achieve **17.1% / 13.4%**. | Figures 13, 15 |
| Similarity depends on dataset and training stage. | GPT expanded versus full traces after RL: GSM8K **34.6% vs 47.2%**. After a second open-source distillation→RL round, expanded versus full traces average **31.0% vs 32.3%** across all three benchmarks. | Appendix C.3, p. 27; Table 8 |

These results establish that post-distillation evaluation can overestimate protection and that expanded API outputs can provide useful training data. They do not establish universal defense failure, statistical equivalence to full traces, faithful reconstruction of hidden reasoning, or transfer to frontier-scale and non-math tasks.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Experiments focus on math; their own RL training is limited to models with at most 3B parameters. Agentic coding and larger attack pipelines remain untested (§7; Appendix C.3).
- **Author-stated capability boundary:** Other tested model families performed poorly, suggesting the attack needs a minimum student/expander capability. The attack is also unoptimized (Appendix C.3; §7).
- **Comparison limits:** Gemini lacks a full-trace baseline. Full-trace extraction is checked through token-count agreement, which does not independently establish semantic identity; extraction code is withheld (Appendix C.2; p. 10).
- **Analyst inference:** Closed-source expansion combines summaries with final answers, so the evidence does not isolate the summaries’ marginal contribution. Figure 13 rules out expander-only gains but does not resolve this distinction.
- **Analyst inference:** No repeated-run uncertainty or statistical equivalence tests are reported. “Similar performance” should therefore be read descriptively.
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Mild-poisoning average accuracy is **41.2%** in Figure 5 and **41.3%** in Table 6; the table value is used above. The Gemma model designation also differs between Appendix A.1 and Table 1.

## Open research questions
- **Does recovery persist at larger scales?** Combined defense tests use small students. A next experiment could repeat full-trace, expanded-trace and poisoned-trace comparisons with larger students under matched compute budgets.
- **How much benefit comes from summaries versus final answers?** Closed-source inputs combine both. Compare answer-only, summary-only and combined expansion with identical training and RL.
- **When does poisoning remain effective after RL?** Qwen and Llama results differ. Vary teacher quality, poisoning strength and student family, including a naturally weaker teacher matched for accuracy.
- **Can batch-level defenses prevent extraction while preserving legitimate use?** The paper only proposes this direction. Evaluate an implemented detector on mixed legitimate and extraction query streams, including distributed accounts, measuring detection and false-positive rates.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported comparisons; **medium** in the broader generalization that response-level defenses are likely ineffective.

Material missing information includes repeated-run variability, formal equivalence tests, matched end-to-end compute and monetary costs, answer-only closed-source ablations, Gemini full traces, released extraction code, and larger-scale or non-math defense evaluations.
