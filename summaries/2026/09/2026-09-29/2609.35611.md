# On-Policy Self-Distillation for Multi-Turn Image Editing

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-29.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.35611) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35611)


## One-sentence takeaway
MT-OPSD trains image editors on their own identity-editing outputs using clean-conditioned self-distillation, improving ten-turn editing success and reducing visual collapse across three backbones while largely preserving single-turn scores (Tables 1–2).

## Problem and motivation
Recursive image editing repeatedly feeds imperfect model outputs into subsequent edits, allowing artifacts and identity drift to accumulate. The authors hypothesize that this reflects a conditioning mismatch: training uses clean images, whereas deployment uses self-generated images. Repeated identity edits expose drift even without intended content changes (Section 1, Figure 1).

Multi-turn annotations are scarce, and backpropagating through full editing sequences is expensive. Pixel-restoration approaches also struggle with global edits, where widespread changes are intentional (Section 1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Self-generated conditioning states:** Identity rollouts accumulate model errors while attempting to preserve source content. These states supply training inputs without paired edited targets or multi-turn annotations (Section 3.2).
- **Two complementary objectives:** An identity branch learns to reproduce the current rollout state, preventing further drift rather than restoring the original. An editing branch matches student velocities at student-visited denoising states against the same model acting as a frozen, clean-conditioned teacher (Section 3.2, Equations 4–5).
- **Adaptive training:** Rollout depth increases when pixel drift stays below a threshold. Student checkpoints replace the teacher only after passing a held-out long-horizon gate; GPT-4o selects checkpoints rather than supplying training rewards (Section 3.2, Appendix C).
- **LME-Bench:** A new benchmark contains 100 ten-turn sessions mixing local and global edits, with cumulative instruction-success and persistent-collapse metrics (Section 4.1, Appendix D).

The methodological distinction is applying on-policy self-distillation to the evolving **conditioning image**, using clean source images as privileged teacher context (Section 2).

LME-Bench SR@k requires every preceding turn to pass prompt-following and consistency thresholds; CR@k counts sessions with two consecutive degraded turns. Both use GPT-4o judgments (Appendix D.2). Low collapse therefore does not imply successful instruction execution.

The results support robustness improvements under the tested protocols. They do not independently prove that conditioning mismatch is the sole cause of degradation. Nor does MT-OPSD match the strongest proprietary systems: Table 1 reports higher SR@10 for GPT-Image-2 and Nano Banana Pro.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-described scope:** Identity rollouts isolate an error component rather than model complete semantic editing histories; the identity objective prevents additional drift rather than repairing accumulated damage (Section 3.2).
- **Analyst inference—proxy validity:** Transfer from identity-rollout errors to errors caused by substantive edits is supported empirically, but direct training with real-edit rollouts is not ablated.
- **Analyst inference—evaluation scope:** LME-Bench uses synthetic sources and a fixed edit composition. Instructions require only the immediately preceding image, so historical references and session-level memory are outside its scope (Appendix D.1).
- **Analyst inference—judge dependence:** GPT-4o controls both teacher selection and reported multi-turn evaluation. Separate gate images reduce direct overlap, but no human agreement study or alternative-judge validation is reported (Appendices C–D).
- **Analyst inference—uncertainty and cost:** Component ablations cover only Qwen. No repeated-run uncertainty, training duration, total compute cost, or judge-call cost is supplied. Each run allocates four training GPUs plus four gate-evaluation GPUs (Sections 4.2, 4.4).

## Open research questions
1. **How representative are identity-rollout errors?** The method avoids semantic rollouts, leaving proxy coverage uncertain. Compare identity-only, substantive-edit, and mixed rollouts under matched compute.
2. **Does robustness persist beyond the tested horizon?** Evaluation ends at ten turns, while training depth typically saturates around four. Test longer sequences and different local/global edit schedules.
3. **How sensitive are gains to the judge and gate distribution?** Selection and evaluation share GPT-4o, and the gate uses the benchmark’s source generator. Repeat selection with another judge and evaluate on human-rated real-image sessions.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text includes explicit objectives, configurations, evaluation prompts, and substantial ablations, supporting the reported comparisons.
