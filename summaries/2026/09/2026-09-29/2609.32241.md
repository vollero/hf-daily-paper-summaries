# Residual Transferability in Neural Image Watermarking

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-29.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.32241) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32241)


## One-sentence takeaway
The authors identify architectural mechanisms that reduce cross-image watermark residual transfer and introduce CoverLock, an image-conditioned payload wrapper achieving low forgery success across the tested systems, datasets, and attacks.

## Problem and motivation
Residual-based forgery transfers watermark evidence from released images to unrelated content, potentially causing false provenance or ownership attribution. The authors argue that prior work demonstrates attacks without adequately explaining why some watermark residuals transfer more readily than others (§§1–2).

The paper separates this intrinsic transferability from the quality of an attacker’s residual estimation, then investigates architectural causes and a defense compatible with existing watermark models.

## Approach and contributions
- **Residual transferability metric:** RT@s measures decoding after transferring averaged ground-truth residuals to disjoint target images. It is defined as `max(2 × mean BitAcc − 1, 0)`, separating transferability from attack-specific residual estimation (§3.1).
- **Architectural analysis:** Behavioral comparisons and controlled interventions identify coupled spatial message broadcast–global average pooling (Broadcast–GAP) and content-adaptive embedding attention as mechanisms supporting cover dependence. Tested training variations do not explain the HiDDeN–MBRS RT gap (§3.3; Appendix E).
- **CoverLock:** A frozen DINOv2 backbone and trained projection head produce distortion-stable image codes. These codes XOR-wrap messages before embedding and unwrap decoded payloads during verification. Training uses natural images and distorted views, without watermark models or forgery examples (§4; Appendix B).

The evidence supports architectural effects in the tested interventions and CoverLock’s advantage over the selected defenses. The average ASR below 0.25% does **not** apply to every individual setting: Table 2 includes higher entries. RT measures oracle bit decoding, whereas ASR measures thresholded forgery acceptance; they are related but distinct.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Severe or unseen transformations, broader distribution shifts, and adaptive attacks targeting image binding remain unresolved. CoverLock adds training and deployment overhead; other architectures may achieve cover dependence differently (Appendix A).
- **Architectural trade-offs:** Broadcast–GAP additions reduce watermark quality alongside RT. MBRS PSNR falls from 42.32 to 27.07 dB, and CIN from 41.55 to 21.71 dB; modified VINE fails to train reliably. **Analyst inference:** These interventions do not establish a generally deployable improvement at matched fidelity and decoding quality (Table 5).
- **Threat-model scope:** Although the primary model emphasizes one or a few references, evaluation also includes Yang’s 100-reference attack. Protection-aware attackers are outside the stated primary assumptions (§§2.3, 5.1).
- **Calibration evidence:** Verification thresholds target FPR <10⁻⁶ under a Bernoulli null model (§5.1). Appendix F observes zero false accepts on 50,000 ImageNet images for MBRS with and without CoverLock. **Analyst inference:** This supports distributional agreement but does not empirically establish that rare-event FPR across all systems.
- **Reproducibility:** Implementation release is promised, not established as completed in the supplied text (p. 10).

## Open research questions
1. **Can adaptive attackers defeat image binding?** Appendix A leaves this open. Test protection-aware attacks that jointly optimize watermark acceptance and CoverLock code agreement.
2. **Can architectural RT reductions preserve fidelity and robustness?** Table 5 shows quality losses, and interventions omit distortion training. Repeat interventions with robustness training and matched PSNR/BitAcc operating points.
3. **How well does CoverLock generalize beyond tested transformations?** Appendix A identifies this uncertainty. Evaluate held-out domains and progressively stronger transformations while measuring both ASR and legitimate TPR.
4. **Which CoverLock components drive its gains?** The supplied text details losses but provides no component-removal ablation. Remove feature statistics and individual regularizers, measuring hash stability, ASR, and TPR.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported experimental comparisons; broader security guarantees remain unestablished.

Material unavailable information includes a released implementation/checkpoint location, deployment latency and memory measurements, full forgery-evaluation sample counts and split details, CoverLock component ablations, and adaptive-attack results.
