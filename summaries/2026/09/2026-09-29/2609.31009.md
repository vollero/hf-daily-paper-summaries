# G^2PTQ: Improving LLM Post-Training Quantization with Generalized Gradient Compensation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-29.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.31009) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31009)


## One-sentence takeaway
G²PTQ combines refreshed block-level curvature estimates, exact gradients, and scaled compensation to improve low-bit LLM quantization, with broad distribution-alignment gains but inconsistent downstream accuracy gains and higher calibration costs.

## Problem and motivation
Post-training quantization compresses LLMs without retraining, but quantization errors can accumulate across layers. The authors identify two gaps: local layer-output objectives provide limited model-level supervision, while existing global-objective methods use fixed Hessians and omit first-order gradients. FOEM restores gradients through an approximation that is exact for layer-wise MSE but generally inaccurate for nonlinear block objectives (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Block-level compensation with refreshed information:** G²PTQ matches full-precision block outputs using MSE for intermediate Transformer blocks and predictive-distribution KL divergence for the final block. It refreshes gradients and approximate Hessians before each block; G²PTQ* refreshes before each of four sequential stages within a block (§4.1; §5.1).
- **Exact gradients with trust-region scaling:** Backpropagation computes gradients of the chosen objective directly. A scaling factor bounds the gradient contribution’s estimated loss change relative to the GPTQ update, addressing unstable compensation (§4.1; Appendix B.4).
- **Efficient implementation:** Gradient outer products approximate Hessians, which are shared within output-channel groups. Recursive gradient compensation matches GPTQ’s stated asymptotic complexity; lazy batching and fused kernels improve execution efficiency (§4.2; Appendices B.1, C.3).

The supervision is broader than individual linear layers, but intermediate blocks still optimize block-output MSE rather than an end-to-end task loss (Equation 10).

## Evidence and main results
KL and PPL averages below cover WikiText2, UltraChat, and NuminaMath1.5; QA averages cover the seven benchmarks listed in Appendix D.3.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong low-bit improvement | LLaMA3-8B, W2A16: G²PTQ* versus GPTAQ has average KL **0.612 vs 0.836**, PPL **9.18 vs 12.60**, and QA **51.16% vs 42.38%**. | Table 1, p. 8 |
| Broad alignment gains | Authors report that G²PTQ variants obtain the lowest average KL in **38/39** dense weight-only settings and highest QA on **12/13** models at 2-bit. | Appendix E.3; Tables 12–14 |
| Weight–activation improvement | LLaMA3-70B, W4A4: G²PTQ versus GPTAQ has average KL **0.277 vs 0.373**, PPL **3.79 vs 4.27**, and QA **72.94% vs 68.38%**. | Table 2, p. 8 |
| Components improve the tested configuration | Qwen3-0.6B, W3A16: the cumulative ablation moves from GPTQ’s KL **1.50**, PPL **55.60**, QA **33.09%** to **0.333**, **16.12**, **39.83%**. Removing scaling yields KL **25.3**, PPL **1.29×10¹²**, QA **28.97%**. | Table 4, p. 9 |
| Exact gradients outperform the approximation | Qwen3-0.6B, W3A16: exact versus approximated compensation gives QA **39.83% vs 37.74%** and lower KL on all three evaluation corpora. | Table 8, p. 25 |
| Calibration costs increase | Qwen3-8B: GPTQ uses **4.62 GB / 0.43 h**, G²PTQ **14.57 GB / 1.49 h**, and G²PTQ* **12.54 GB / 2.24 h**. | Table 5, p. 9 |
| Large MoE reasoning remains close to reference | Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, W4A16: mean accuracy across four reasoning benchmarks is **75.40±0.40%** for G²PTQ*, **75.17±0.11%** for GPTQ, and **75.59±0.84%** for BF16, across three seeds. | Table 18, p. 32 |
| Lower KL does not guarantee higher QA | Qwen3.5-9B, W3A16: G²PTQ variants have lower KL on each corpus than GPTQ, but QA is **70.55% vs 71.00%**. | Table 13, p. 29 |

The evidence supports improved distribution preservation across many tested settings and substantial gains in selected aggressive quantization settings. It does not establish uniformly better QA or statistically lossless quantization. Table 18’s uncertainty notation is described as “variance,” leaving its precise interpretation unclear.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated costs and dependencies:** G²PTQ requires additional backward passes and generally more calibration samples to reach its best accuracy. Hessian-based clipping helps Qwen3 but offers no consistent benefit for Qwen3.5 (Appendices E.1–E.2).
- **Analyst inference—approximation validity:** The KL Hessian derivation assumes quantized and reference distributions are close; practical Hessian computation assumes negligible future-token squared gradients. Severe low-bit distortion or substantial cross-token dependencies could weaken these approximations (Appendix B.1).
- **Analyst inference—stability guarantee:** The trust-region bound concerns a quadratic loss estimate. Implementation averages scaling factors across each row, so the supplied derivation does not establish an actual-loss or per-step guarantee for that implementation (Appendix B.4).
- **Analyst inference—evaluation boundaries:** Component ablations concentrate on Qwen3-0.6B at 3-bit. Reasoning evaluation covers one MoE model, and MoE comparisons use GPTQ alone. Dense-result uncertainty and inference latency measurements are absent (Tables 4, 7–8, 16–18).
- The narrative’s claim of consistently best performance across all metrics is broader than the tables support (§5.2; Tables 12–14).

## Open research questions
1. **When do the Hessian approximations become inaccurate?** Their assumptions are not directly validated. Compare estimated curvature with explicit per-token gradients and Hessian-vector products on small blocks across bit-widths and attention architectures.
2. **How reliably does practical scaling control actual loss?** Row-averaged scaling and quadratic predictions leave this unresolved. Measure predicted versus observed loss changes and compare row-averaged with per-step scaling.
3. **How broadly do component gains and threshold transfer generalize?** Detailed ablations and transfer experiments cover limited configurations. Repeat them across larger dense and MoE models using held-out calibration domains.
4. **What explains lower KL with worse QA?** Several settings show this mismatch. Run repeated evaluations and analyze changes by task, token, and calibration domain before treating KL improvements as capability preservation.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the method and reported comparisons; **medium** for broad capability-preservation and stability claims.

Material missing information includes accelerator models and software versions, dense-evaluation uncertainty, precise meaning of Table 18’s “±” values, detailed calibration sample selection and seeds, inference throughput, and independent verification of the linked code.
