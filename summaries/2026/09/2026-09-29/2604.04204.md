# How Does &quot;English (US)&quot; Become the Default? Triangulating Structural Bias Towards American English Across the LLM Pipeline

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-29.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2604.04204) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04204)


## One-sentence takeaway
The authors find convergent American English preferences in pretraining corpora, tokenization, and QA generation, although their observational audits do not establish causal propagation between these stages (§§4–6).

## Problem and motivation
The paper asks whether LLMs implicitly privilege American English (AmE) over British English (BrE), and where that preference appears in their development pipeline. The authors argue that such defaults can undermine dialect-sensitive user experiences and contribute to linguistic homogenization. They identify a gap in research on standard English varieties and pipeline-wide asymmetries, beyond downstream task-performance disparities (§§1–2, 8).

Postcolonial theory provides an interpretive framework, not an experimental method; broader cultural and political consequences are proposed implications rather than measured outcomes (§§2, 7).

## Approach and contributions
- **Curated resource:** 1,813 manually compiled, one-to-one AmE–BrE lexical pairs covering spelling and vocabulary differences (§3; Appendix J).
- **DiAlign:** A training-free alignment score using Google Books AmE/BrE frequency ratios for contiguous 2–5-grams, divergence weighting, and lexicon boosting. It excludes named entities, stopword-only sequences, and sequences with zero frequency in either reference corpus (§3.1). The implementation uses 1950–2022 frequencies and a boosting factor of 1.5 (Appendix C).
- **Three-stage audit:** Lexical distributions and DiAlign scores across six pretraining corpora; fertility and segmentation distributions across regional tokenizers; and QA generations under “English” versus “British English (en-GB)” prompts (§§4–6). The authors claim novelty for this combined examination; the supplied text cannot independently verify priority.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|

| BrE vocabulary variants require more subwords | Falcon3-7B fertility: AmE 2.03 versus BrE 2.41, a reported relative gap of 18.72%; DeepSeek-V3: 2.37 versus 2.67, gap 12.66%. | Table 3 |

The evidence supports recurring asymmetries within the tested settings. It does not isolate the causal contributions of corpus composition, tokenizer design, or alignment training, nor measure factual QA quality or societal harms. DiAlign’s normalized scores are called probabilities by the authors, but probability calibration is not established. Generation results above come from prose because the numerical table body is missing.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Scope excludes other English varieties and multilingual settings; the static lexicon omits non-one-to-one mappings and idiomatic expressions, while type-level counts cannot resolve contextual ambiguity (Appendix J).
- **Author-stated:** DiAlign relies on historical written usage, may miss contemporary internet language or subtle cues, and excludes zero-signal outputs. BBC/HuffPost proxy labels confound dialect with publication style (Appendices E, J).
- **Author-stated:** Generation evaluation covers short QA in two registers, without dialect-marked questions, dialogue, or long-form and specialist tasks. Models vary in compliance with the requested answer length (Appendices H, J).
- **Analyst inference:** Publisher and sample-length differences could inflate proxy classification performance; cross-model generation-length differences could affect available scoring evidence. Neither effect is isolated in the supplied results.
- **Evidence ambiguity:** §5’s claim that all models have higher BrE fertility conflicts with Velvet’s orthographic exception. Generation results are captioned Table 4 but repeatedly referenced as Table 6; ablation results are separately labeled Table 6.

## Open research questions
1. **What causally drives the generation preference?** The audits establish co-occurrence across stages. A next experiment could vary dialect balance and tokenizer vocabulary independently while holding model training otherwise fixed (§§4–7).
2. **Does DiAlign distinguish dialect from publisher style?** Validation uses provenance proxies. Test it on manually annotated, topic- and length-matched texts from multiple publishers and conversational sources (Appendices E, J).
3. **Does the default persist with user dialect cues or longer interaction?** These settings were excluded. Compare matched questions with AmE/BrE cues across short answers, long answers, and multi-turn dialogue (Appendix J).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied source is truncated, so this analysis does not provide complete coverage. Material gaps include numerical bodies of the corpus, generation, and ablation tables; the complete generation-model/version list; exact prompts; exclusion counts; detailed lexicon construction; and confirmed code or asset availability. The directional findings are supported, but precise comparisons and reproducibility cannot be fully assessed.
