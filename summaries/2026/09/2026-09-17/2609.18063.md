# The Other Half of the Memory Wall: Serving 35B MoEs from SSD with Trained Routing Prediction

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-17.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.18063) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18063)


## One-sentence takeaway

Edge0 serves a 35B mixture-of-experts language model from SSD at 20.4 tokens/s with 2.9 GiB peak active memory by predicting and adopting next-token routing in advance, while an unmerged recovery LoRA limits the reported average quality gap from the fp16 base to 3.9 points.

## Problem and motivation

Sparse MoEs reduce computation by activating only a few experts per token, but their full weights must ordinarily remain available. The studied 35B model has a 19.5 GB int4 checkpoint, which strains a 24 GB consumer machine once the operating system and KV cache are included.

Simply loading experts from SSD on demand introduces a sequential stall: the experts needed by layer \(N+1\) are unknown until layer \(N\) produces its output. The authors identify a gap in prior offloading and pre-gating approaches: they do not predict the required expert set far enough ahead to hide a full decode step’s storage latency while guaranteeing that every selected expert is available (Introduction; §2; §3.2).

## Approach and contributions

1. **SSD-streamed expert execution.** Expert weights remain in memory-mapped int4 safetensors and enter a bounded pool on demand. Exact, staged, hot-cache, and whole-layer execution paths share the same quantized computation, while fixed slots and incremental stack updates reduce tensor reconstruction (§3.1).

2. **Prediction-as-routing.** A small head attached to each applicable layer predicts the next layer’s routing one token ahead. At decode time, that prediction becomes the actual routing decision, making the prefetched and executed expert sets identical rather than requiring fallback loads or dropped experts (§3.2; Figure 1).

3. **Recovery training.** The authors first distill the prerouter heads, then train LoRA adapters with prerouter routing active, and finally apply on-policy reverse-KL distillation to student-generated sequences (§4).

4. **Unmerged LoRA serving.** The adapter is evaluated as a parallel full-precision delta over the frozen int4 base. The authors report that merging and requantizing erases much of its effect (§3.3).

5. **Released system tiers.** The paper claims open-source framework, checkpoint, prerouter, and adapter artifacts for 35B and 8B tiers, although artifact links are absent from the supplied text (Introduction; §7).

The results support the claim that advance routing can hide serialized SSD latency in the tested tight-memory, single-request Apple MLX setting. They also show a substantial memory reduction relative to the paper’s resident comparison.

They do not establish performance under concurrency, batching, CUDA, other SSDs or operating systems, or long contexts. The quality summary is incomplete because the supplied rendering omits Table 2’s individual benchmark values and does not isolate the separate effects of int4 quantization, routing replacement, and each recovery phase.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations**

- Serving is FIFO and limited to one request at a time; batching may change the expert working set (§6).
- The remaining decode floor includes 44 ms per step of CPU-side graph construction, which storage optimization cannot remove (§6).
- Benefits shrink with faster storage or hotter caches and depend on expert reuse. Adjacent tokens share only about one quarter of a layer’s expert set in the authors’ traces (§5.3; §6).
- Prefetching consumes unreclaimable memory and displaces page cache. At K=8 it adds 1.43 GiB of resident memory while page cache falls by 1.15 GiB (§5.3).
- Long-chain reasoning shows the largest quality degradation, especially the reported 10-point AIME gap for the 8B tier (§6).
- MLX is the only implemented backend; CUDA support is architectural only (§6).
- The prerouters are trained using base-router features but consume prediction-derived features during deployment; the heads are not retrained for this drift (§3.2).

**Analyst inferences**

- **Component attribution is limited.** The supplied evaluation does not report a full quality ablation separating int4 loss, routing replacement, SFT, on-policy distillation, and unmerged LoRA.
- **External validity is narrow.** Most performance evidence comes from two Apple machines in a regime where the checkpoint does not comfortably fit; the reported advantage may differ materially elsewhere.
- **Memory coverage is incomplete.** Table 1 uses short-context MLX allocator peaks, so it does not characterize long-context KV growth or total system memory pressure.
- **Variance remains material.** The authors report ±40% run-to-run spread and up to 2.3× cross-session variation on the 16 GB machine. Their same-session design reduces comparison bias but does not eliminate broader reproducibility concerns (§5.1).
- **Artifact reproducibility cannot be checked from this text.** Openness is claimed, but the supplied “Artifacts” section contains no repository or checkpoint links.

## Open research questions

1. **How does Edge0 behave with batching and concurrent requests?** The paper tests serialized single-request serving, while batching changes expert overlap and cache pressure. A next experiment could sweep batch size and concurrent sessions while measuring throughput, tail latency, active memory, and SSD traffic.

2. **Which recovery stage contributes most to quality?** The reported scores combine quantization, replaced routing, SFT, on-policy distillation, and LoRA. A factorial ablation across these components on the same benchmarks would identify their separate and interacting effects.

3. **Can prerouters be trained against their deployed feature distribution?** The heads experience feature drift because predicted routing replaces base routing at inference. Iterative or on-policy prerouter training could be compared with the current fixed-head procedure on routing behavior, quality, and throughput.

4. **Do the gains transfer to other hardware and storage regimes?** Only MLX on Apple machines is implemented and measured. A controlled comparison across SSD latency, memory capacity, CUDA hardware, and cache temperature would map where one-token-ahead routing remains beneficial.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the described method and quoted aggregate results, because the supplied text covers the full design, training recipe, evaluation conditions, and limitations.

Material missing information includes:

- the actual rows and per-benchmark values of Tables 1–4;
- the names of all five quality benchmarks;
- statistical uncertainty for quality measurements;
- detailed training hyperparameters, compute, data composition, and licensing;
- explicit artifact and source-code links;
- long-context memory measurements;
- concurrency, batching, CUDA, and non-Apple evaluations;
- a complete quality ablation of quantization, routing replacement, and recovery training.
