# ContextBias: Controlled Evaluation of Bias Persistence Under Context Shift in Text-to-Image Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-01.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.29847) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29847)


## One-sentence takeaway

Across four text-to-image models, unrelated occupational contexts changed scenes more than people: benchmark-wide attribute concentration increased, while many role-linked demographic, garment, and tool cues persisted across contexts and prompt reformulations.

## Problem and motivation

The paper asks whether visual attributes associated with professional roles adapt when prompts move those roles into related or unrelated locations and activities. Existing bias evaluations largely examine roles in isolation, so they cannot distinguish context-sensitive portrayals from learned associations that persist despite conflicting scene cues. This matters because persistent cues can shape occupational representation in generated media and may also reveal failures of compositional generalization (Introduction; Sections 2–3).

## Approach and contributions

- **Controlled evaluation framework:** ContextBias holds occupational role identity fixed while varying location and activity across context-free (CF), related (CA-R), and unrelated (CA-U) conditions. It measures distributional concentration with Bias Intensity (BI) and label-level prevalence and stability with Context Consistency Score (CCS), supplemented by chi-squared homogeneity tests (Sections 3.1–3.5; Figure 2).

- **Benchmark:** ContextBench contains 92 U.S. occupational roles and 1,656 prompts—18 configurations per role—with semantic paraphrases and location/activity substitutions. Context banks were generated with GPT-4o-mini and manually filtered (Sections 3.2 and 4.1; Appendix A).

- **Large-scale audit:** The authors generated 66,240 images with FLUX.1, SDXL, Stable Diffusion 3.5, and Qwen-Image, then used a GPT-5-mini-based schema pipeline to extract 30 dimensions covering people, objects, scenes, and cameras (Sections 3.3–3.4 and 4.1; Table 6).

- **Validation and findings:** The extraction pipeline was tested on explicit-attribute generations and against three human annotators. The study finds persistent role-linked cues, a cross-role increase in pooled concentration under unrelated contexts, and substantially greater sensitivity in scene and camera properties (Sections 4.2–5.3).

## Evidence and main results

The evidence supports the authors’ claim that contextual changes often leave occupational person-level cues intact and that pooled distributions can become more concentrated across roles. It does **not** show that individual occupational prototypes become sharper: the per-role BI analysis finds the opposite overall direction. Nor do BI or CCS establish that any association is socially harmful; BI measures concentration and CCS measures persistence, not normative stereotype severity (Section 7). Cross-model consistency is suggestive of shared regularities, but the study does not identify their training-data or architectural causes.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Only location and activity are varied; lighting, cultural setting, and interpersonal interaction are not tested (Section 7).

- **Author-stated:** The 92-role inventory and photographic contexts are English-language and predominantly Western/U.S.-centric, so findings are not culturally universal (Sections 4.1 and 7).

- **Author-stated:** BI cannot distinguish harmful stereotypes from legitimate occupational regularities; interpreting harm requires normative judgment outside the metric (Section 7).

- **Author-stated:** GPT-5-mini may introduce its own occupational priors during extraction. Human agreement reduces—but does not eliminate—this concern, and `unknown` observations are excluded from frequency analyses (Section 7).

- **Author-stated:** Models use different native resolutions—1024×1024 versus 1328×1328—which may affect extraction of fine-grained attributes (Appendix B).

- **Analyst inference:** Explicit-attribute validation is easier than detecting attributes implied in contextual scenes. The separate human study addresses this partially, but covers only 10 of 92 roles and 1,200 of 66,240 images (Sections 4.2–4.3).

- **Analyst inference:** The role-level bootstrap quantifies uncertainty over roles, but the supplied text does not report multiplicity corrections for the many label-level homogeneity tests among 96,310 evaluated labels (Sections 3.5 and 5.2).

- **Analyst inference:** Context banks rely partly on GPT-4o-mini generation and manual filtering; detailed reviewer agreement or reproducibility statistics for semantic relatedness are not supplied (Sections 3.2 and 4.1; Appendix A).

## Open research questions

1. **Does persistence generalize beyond locations and activities?** The paper excludes cultural settings, interactions, and lighting. A factorial experiment could independently vary these dimensions while holding the role and other prompt components fixed.

2. **How culturally dependent are the observed role cues?** ContextBench is Western/U.S.-centric. A matched multilingual benchmark could compare the same roles across languages and culturally localized context banks, using local annotators and identical persistence metrics.

3. **Are persistent cues caused by training data, architecture, or inference behavior?** Four generators exhibit similar patterns, but causal mechanisms are not tested. A next experiment could compare training-corpus association frequencies with generated CCS values and apply controlled model interventions or prompt-conditioning ablations.

4. **Can mitigation reduce harmful persistence without erasing useful occupational information?** The framework diagnoses persistence but does not evaluate mitigation. A follow-up could test debiasing methods under CF, CA-R, and CA-U and jointly measure stereotype persistence, contextual fidelity, and role recognizability.
