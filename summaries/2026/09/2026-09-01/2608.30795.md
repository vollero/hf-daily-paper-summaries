# Uncertainty-Aware End-to-End AI Weather Forecasting: Disentangling Observation and Model Contributions

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-01.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.30795) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30795)


## One-sentence takeaway
A nested 49-member ensemble adds encoder noise and processor dropout to pretrained Aardvark Weather, improving mean gridded RMSE by 4.2% while producing near-calibrated medium-range uncertainty that can be approximately attributed to observation-side and model-side sources.

## Problem and motivation
End-to-end weather models forecast directly from raw observations, avoiding a separate numerical data-assimilation pipeline, but existing systems are deterministic and provide neither calibrated uncertainty nor its attribution. This limits decision-making and makes it unclear whether forecast uncertainty calls for better observations or improvements to learned dynamics. The authors address this gap by probabilistically upgrading a pretrained modular model while preserving forecast skill and training efficiency (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Component-aligned stochastic uplift:** Learned, input-dependent Gaussian noise is attached to Aardvark’s observation encoder, while Monte Carlo dropout is applied to its forecast processor; the decoder remains deterministic (Fig. 2; Methods, pp. 8–9).

2. **Nested uncertainty decomposition:** Seven encoder draws are each expanded into seven dropout rollouts. A law-of-total-variance/ANOVA decomposition separates between-encoder-draw variance from within-draw dropout variance (Eqs. 2–4; Fig. 2).

3. **Empirical attribution test:** Observation streams are withheld at encoding time to test whether intervention effects appear specifically in the encoder-attributed variance (Fig. 5; Methods, p. 9).

4. **Efficient probabilistic finetuning:** Both stochastic components are warm-started from deterministic Aardvark and separately optimized using fair CRPS rather than trained from scratch (Data and training, p. 10).

The evidence supports improved probabilistic and mean skill relative to the selected modular Aardvark baseline, near aggregate calibration on the ERA5 grid, and intervention-sensitive component attribution. It does not establish that the two variance branches recover true aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty, that performance generalizes beyond Aardvark or 2018, or that the system matches the operational IFS ensemble.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** The decoder is deterministic, contributing no downscaling uncertainty and plausibly explaining part of the station under-dispersion (Discussion, pp. 7–8).
- **Author-stated:** MC dropout uses a fixed \(p=0.05\) and is only an approximate, restricted posterior; deep processor ensembles are suggested as a stronger but costlier epistemic reference.
- **Author-stated:** Evaluation uses one held-out year, one backbone, a 1.5° grid, and modest \(7\times7\) ensembles. The non-negativity floor for encoder variance is occasionally active at long leads.
- **Author-stated:** The backbone ingests about 0.73 TB from nine streams, far less than an operational observing system, and omits aircraft and radio-occultation inputs. This complicates attribution of the IFS ENS performance gap solely to the probabilistic method.
- **Author-stated:** The encoder/aleatoric and dropout/epistemic mapping is component-aligned rather than ground truth. Encoder–processor interactions are absorbed into the dropout term because of the chosen conditioning order.
- **Author-stated:** The deterministic station baseline is the modular release, not the stronger published end-to-end-finetuned configuration, whose released weights were available for only one lead and whose scores the authors could not reproduce (Data and training, p. 10).
- **Analyst inference:** The observation-denial test strongly supports branch responsiveness but does not prove exclusivity: denial may alter downstream dynamics even if its measured dropout-variance response is small in the tested cases.
- **Analyst inference:** Aggregate SSR near one can conceal variable-specific and spatial miscalibration, as demonstrated by under-dispersed mass fields and stations.

## Open research questions

1. **Does encoder variance match physically grounded assimilation uncertainty?**  
   It remains open because the paper compares against ERA5 analyses but not ERA5’s Ensemble of Data Assimilations. A next experiment could compare spatial patterns, magnitude, and lead-zero calibration of encoder spread with EDA spread across seasons.

2. **How much uncertainty belongs to encoder–processor interaction?**  
   The present conditioning order assigns nonlinear interaction to the dropout term. A crossed experiment could reuse fixed dropout masks across encoder draws and estimate a separate interaction component.

3. **Would a stochastic decoder correct station under-dispersion?**  
   The frozen decoder produces no station-specific spread. A plausible test is to add a third probabilistic decoder branch trained with fair CRPS and evaluate station SSR, CRPS, biases, and regional calibration.

4. **Is dropout dominance robust to alternative epistemic models?**  
   The result may depend on masks being redrawn at every rollout step. Comparisons with one fixed mask per member and independently trained processor ensembles could test whether the 0.86–0.96 dropout variance share reflects learned dynamics or the perturbation mechanism.
