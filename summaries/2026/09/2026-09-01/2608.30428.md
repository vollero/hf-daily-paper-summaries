# Lies We Can See: Joint Verbal and Non-Verbal Deception by VLM Agents in Embodied Social Interactions

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-01.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.30428) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30428)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors show that VLM-agent success in an embodied social-deduction game is associated with both verbal and physical deception, with non-verbal kill-cycle and camouflage behaviors providing the strongest—but correlational and game-specific—signals of winning.

## Problem and motivation

Existing agent-deception evaluations are predominantly text-only and use fixed agent configurations. The authors argue that this excludes sensorimotor deception—such as stalking, hiding intent through movement, and acting only without witnesses—and confounds properties of the underlying VLM with choices in its memory, planning, prompting, and other harness components (Introduction, pp. 1–3).

They address this gap in an embodied, hidden-role game where agents must combine visual perception, physical action, and meeting dialogue. This matters as a controlled safety evaluation of strategic deception, although the authors explicitly caution that game performance is not a direct measure of general-purpose deception (Limitations, p. 11).

## Approach and contributions

1. **MINEAMONGUS:** A Minecraft-based 3D Among Us sandbox in which eight agents—two imposters and six crewmates—receive RGB and structured observations, execute Mineflayer programs, and communicate during meetings (Sections 3.1–3.2; Tables 1 and 9).

2. **ARIA:** A configurable VLM-agent harness with five ablation axes: state representation, memory, planning, reflection plus skill memory, and prompt style (Section 3.3; Table 2).

3. **Deception annotation framework:** A taxonomy of 23 verbal and non-verbal “atoms” in six clusters, plus multi-step deception arcs. It was derived by two author-annotators from 48 logs and mapped to three existing deception taxonomies (Section 3.4; Table 3; Appendix E.1).

4. **Scalable judging:** A Qwen3.6-27B LLM judge for atom labeling, evaluated against human annotations (Section 3.4; Appendix E.4).

5. **Controlled evaluations:** A 192-match harness ablation with fixed backbones and a 1,152-match, 12-VLM round robin under fixed harness configurations (Sections 4.1–4.2).

6. **Auxiliary crewmate training:** Behavior cloning of a 9B crewmate using outcome- or vote-filtered gameplay examples, evaluated for game wins and imposter ejection (Appendix U).

These results establish behavioral associations inside this sandbox and show that harness choices can materially alter measured performance. They do **not** establish that the atoms cause wins, that a VLM has a general disposition to deceive, or that the observed rankings transfer to interactions with humans. The cross-model atom analysis is especially exploratory because it selects only six VLMs and computes correlations over \(N=6\) model-level observations.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:**

- The labels measure **game-bounded behavioral deception**; some apparent deception, including efficient movement or fake missions, may partly reflect ordinary task competence (Limitations, p. 11).
- Main experiments require privileged, persistent spatial information. Fully egocentric agents fail to sustain the kill–meeting loop, so the evaluation does not demonstrate deception under realistic first-person perception alone (Limitations; Appendix G).
- ARIA covers only five manually chosen harness axes and excludes personality-conditioned or automatically optimized harnesses (Limitations, p. 11).
- The top/bottom VLM atom comparison uses only six models; the authors treat the correlation signs and magnitudes as comparative rather than inferential (Section 4.2, p. 9).

**Analyst inferences:**

- The default rule awarding an imposter win after 200 steps can reward stalling; the ego-state Qwen result and high-reasoning experiment explicitly contain timeout wins (Table 1; Section 4.1; Appendix T).
- The taxonomy was initially produced by two developers/authors. Independent validation used one non-author on only five games, and the paper itself calls that evidence convergent rather than exhaustive (Appendix E.3).
- Atom–win correlations may conflate deception with mechanically necessary progress: killing is directly tied to the imposter objective, so kill-related atoms are partly proxies for basic game competence.
- Several factorial configurations have only three repetitions, yielding very coarse 0/33/67/100% cell estimates (Table 18).
- The α=0.10 analysis provides exploratory, not conventional confirmatory, evidence; no per-axis comparison was individually significant (Appendix I).
- The source is truncated, including omitted appendix material, so complete assessment of judge robustness, released artifacts, inference settings, costs, and reproducibility is impossible.

## Open research questions

1. **Can the same deceptive behaviors emerge without privileged tracking?**  
   This remains open because ego-state agents produced no kills. A next experiment could train or equip agents with visual mapping and object permanence, then repeat the full RQ1/RQ2 evaluations using only first-person RGB and agent memory.

2. **Do the atom labels transfer beyond Among Us?**  
   The authors explicitly restrict their interpretation to game-bounded deception. A next experiment could preregister a subset of belief-targeting atoms and test their annotation reliability in a different embodied hidden-role task or controlled human-agent interaction.

3. **Which behaviors causally improve imposter success?**  
   The reported atom analyses are correlational. A next experiment could selectively disable fake missions, witness checks, fleeing, or meeting falsification while holding all other harness components fixed and measure controlled WR changes.

4. **How stable are rankings under different rules and budgets?**  
   The study fixes eight players and generally uses a privileged state and 200-step cap. A factorial evaluation over player ratios, maps, mission pressure, timeout handling, and communication limits could test whether model and strategy rankings persist.
