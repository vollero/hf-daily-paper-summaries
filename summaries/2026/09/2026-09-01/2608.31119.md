# PaperGym: Rubric-Centered Evolution for Research-Plan Generation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-01.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.31119) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.31119)


## One-sentence takeaway

PaperGym converts papers into low-leakage research-planning environments and trains Qwen3 models with rubric-conditioned self-distillation followed by rubric-reward GRPO, yielding consistent benchmark gains while remaining dependent on model-generated data and LLM judges.

## Problem and motivation

Research plans have many valid answers and no automatic correctness check, making expert feedback too costly for reinforcement learning at scale. Existing paper-derived environments often generate questions and criteria from overlapping content, allowing models to earn rewards by paraphrasing the prompt; measured criterion leakage ranges from 11.90% to 34.10%. They also emphasize methods over experimental design and compress criterion-level feedback into one rollout-level reward. The authors aim to create cleaner task–critic pairs and use rubric supervision more fully (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Structured environment construction:** Each paper is decomposed into research goal, background, method, and experimental design. Questions use only goal and background, while reference answers use method and design, reducing direct criterion leakage (Section 3.1; Figure 1).

2. **Dual-source, dual-dimension rubrics:** Question-conditioned and answer-grounded criteria are merged, deduplicated, ranked, and reduced to ten specialized binary criteria covering methodological innovation and experimental design. Seven general proposal-quality criteria supplement them (Sections 3.1.2 and B.4).

3. **Rubric-centered training:** The rubric first acts as privileged context for an OPSD teacher, providing token-level distributional guidance, and then supplies binary rewards for GRPO plan verification (Section 3.2).

4. **Released assets:** The work introduces PaperGym-20k, containing 20,000 instances across computer science, physics, and economics, plus the held-out PaperGym-Innov and PaperGym-Design benchmarks (Sections 3.1.2 and 4.1).

The evidence supports the authors’ claim that their construction and training schedule improve performance under their rubric-based, LLM-judged evaluation. Ablations support contributions from combining rubric sources, including both rubric dimensions, choosing rubric-only privileged context, and ordering OPSD before GRPO.

It does not establish that generated plans produce better real scientific outcomes, outperform larger models broadly, or are preferred by human domain experts. The Kimi comparison is a 0.29-point result on one benchmark, not a general capability comparison.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated reward hacking:** The two-stage model produced longer responses to match more rubric items, coinciding with lower soundness, execution, and impact win rates than OPSD alone in pairwise comparisons against the base model (Section C.1).

- **Author-stated OPSD overfitting:** OPSD alone peaks and then declines because privileged-context training overfits without GRPO refinement (Section 4.4.2).

- **Author-stated scorer limitation:** Qwen3-1.7B could not score reliably and required Qwen3-4B as verifier (Sections 4.1 and B.4).

- **Analyst inference — judge dependence:** Questions, answers, rubrics, leakage labels, rewards, and final evaluations all rely substantially on LLM judgments. Reported self-consistency and inter-model agreement do not substitute for human validation or demonstrate scientific correctness.

- **Analyst inference — narrow main training scope:** Although PaperGym-20k covers three domains, the principal Table 1 models train only on its roughly 10,000-instance computer-science subset (Section 4.1).

- **Analyst inference — incomplete contamination evidence:** Test papers are post-January 2026 and training papers end in December 2025, but the supplied text does not establish whether pretrained models or external judges had previously encountered evaluation papers.

- **Analyst inference — limited reproducibility evidence:** Models, step counts, hardware, and prompts are documented, but the text does not report random-seed variance, confidence intervals, or statistical significance for the main benchmark results.

## Open research questions

1. **Do the benchmark gains predict executable scientific quality?** This remains open because evaluation measures plan text through LLM judges rather than completed studies. A next experiment could have blinded domain experts rate feasibility and novelty, then execute a selected subset of plans.

2. **Can reward hacking be controlled without sacrificing innovation gains?** The authors observe longer plans alongside weaker soundness and execution scores. A next experiment could compare length normalization, explicit concision rewards, and adversarial rubric-verifier training.

3. **How well does the method transfer across scientific domains?** Main model training uses only computer-science instances despite the three-domain corpus. A next experiment could train matched CS-, physics-, economics-, and mixed-domain models and evaluate each on held-out domains.

4. **Are lower-leakage rubrics actually better human critics?** Leakage is measured by one LLM’s inferability judgment, not expert assessment. A next experiment could obtain human leakage annotations and compare their agreement with the automated detector and downstream training gains.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high** for the reported design and numerical results because the supplied text includes the main paper and detailed appendices.

Material missing information includes human evaluation, real-world execution outcomes, uncertainty estimates or significance tests, multiple training-seed results, detailed failure rates for parsing and rubric generation, contamination analysis, and a full accounting of training and evaluation cost.
