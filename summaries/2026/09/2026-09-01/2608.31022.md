# MNIST-PRO: MNIST is Back as a Partially Observable World for AI Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-01.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.31022) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.31022)


## One-sentence takeaway

MNIST-PRO shows that multimodal agents that recognize complete MNIST images accurately still struggle to acquire, organize, revise, and interpret sequential visual glimpses, especially for ordered two-digit sequences.

## Problem and motivation

The paper targets agentic perception under partial observability: an agent must choose where to look, retain earlier observations, construct an evolving perceptual state, and decide when it has enough evidence. Existing static vision benchmarks provide all evidence upfront, while active-control and embodied benchmarks introduce navigation, interaction, or execution confounds that make perceptual-state failures difficult to isolate (Section 1, pp. 2–3).

The authors address this gap with a controlled 2D environment based on familiar MNIST images. Since full-image digit recognition is comparatively easy for the evaluated models, performance losses under sequential glimpses can be studied with fewer low-level recognition and physical-control confounds (Section 1, p. 2).

## Approach and contributions

- **Controlled benchmark:** MNIST-PRO casts digit identification as a POMDP in which an agent moves a fixed glimpse window over a masked image and must predict before a sensing budget expires (Sections 2.1–2.3; Figures 1–2).

- **Two difficulty levels:** Level 1 requires spatial integration for one digit; Level 2 concatenates two digits and additionally tests longer-horizon search, retention, and ordering (Section 2.3).

- **Memory-representation evaluation:** The study compares raw visual history, free-form textual state, a structured metric grid map, and programmatically consolidated online or offline visual canvases. It also studies autonomous tool-using harnesses and cross-episode procedural memory (Sections 2.4 and 3.4).

- **Diagnostic decomposition:** Fixed-trajectory canvas replays and predictor swaps separate failures in evidence acquisition, state construction, state interpretation, and stopping decisions (Sections 3.4.1–3.4.3 and Section 4).

- **Stress tests:** Visual-history length and glimpse-window size are varied to control memory load and sensing-channel capacity (Section 3.5; Tables 7–8).

The evidence supports the authors’ central claim that acquisition, representation, interpretation, and stopping are distinct bottlenecks: fixed-trajectory replay and predictor replacement offer stronger localization than aggregate accuracy alone. It does not establish that the findings generalize beyond simplified MNIST-derived canvases, the tested models, or the specific prompting and harness implementations. The experiments also do not prove the authors’ hypotheses about incomplete canvases causing confusion or model familiarity causing overoptimistic stopping.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Narrow visual domain:** The benchmark uses centered, binarized, upscaled MNIST digits on deterministic 2D canvases. This improves diagnostic control but leaves transfer to noisy scenes, GUI interaction, robotics, and other partially observable tasks untested (Sections 2 and 3.1). *Analyst inference.*

- **Limited sequence scale:** The reported Level 2 experiments concatenate two digits; behavior for longer sequences is not established (Sections 2.3 and 3.1). *Analyst inference.*

- **Small per-condition evaluation:** Each task uses 100 episodes, and no confidence intervals, variance estimates, or significance tests are reported in the supplied text (Section 3.1). *Analyst inference.*

- **Default and heterogeneous model settings:** Most models use default API temperatures and thinking levels, while one model also receives an `xhigh` condition. The text does not establish matched inference budgets across providers (Section 3.1). *Analyst inference.*

- **Harness comparisons are partly confounded:** The authors explicitly note that customized system prompts may encourage longer, more verifiable work. Thus, harness gains cannot be attributed solely to tool access or external state construction (Section 3.4.3, p. 17).

- **Incomplete harness coverage:** Some model-task cells are absent, and the Claude harness result is a limited matched evaluation of only 26 valid Level 1 episodes (Table 4 and footnote, p. 15).

- **Causal hypotheses remain unisolated:** Confusion from repeatedly viewing incomplete canvases, familiarity-driven early confidence, and post-training miscalibration are proposed explanations, not experimentally isolated causes (Sections 3.4.3 and 4).

## Open research questions

1. **Does MNIST-PRO predict performance in less controlled environments?** The paper evaluates only simplified MNIST-derived worlds. A next experiment could correlate model rankings and diagnosed failure types with matched partially observable GUI or embodied tasks while holding action complexity as constant as possible.

2. **How does state construction scale beyond two digits?** Level 2 adds only one extra digit and an ordering requirement. A controlled sweep over sequence length, canvas width, and sensing budget could estimate where acquisition, memory, and interpretation fail separately.

3. **Can calibrated stopping improve accuracy without exhaustive search?** The trace audit identifies early commitment, but no learned or rule-based evidence-sufficiency policy is evaluated. A next experiment could compare native stopping with coverage-aware, uncertainty-aware, and fixed-budget policies using identical state representations.

4. **Why can persistent procedures be stored but not used effectively?** Persistent memory standardized reconstruction yet did not improve accuracy and sometimes increased sensing. An intervention that separately controls procedure retrieval, execution fidelity, and final-state interpretation could locate the failure.
