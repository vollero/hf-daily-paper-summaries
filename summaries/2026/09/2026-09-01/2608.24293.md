# Keep-or-Drop? Adaptive Tokenizer for Compact Video Representation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-01.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.24293) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24293)


## One-sentence takeaway

KATok jointly learns continuous video latents and per-token keep/drop decisions, then uses position-aware sparse flow matching to improve reconstruction, generation quality, and compute efficiency with substantially fewer tokens.

## Problem and motivation

Conventional video VAEs use fixed compression ratios, so latent-token counts grow with spatial resolution and duration even when videos contain extensive static or homogeneous content. Existing variable-length tokenizers offer user-selected budgets but may require predefined budgets or inference-time search rather than choosing token counts from each sample’s complexity. The authors target fully differentiable, content-adaptive compression while addressing a downstream problem introduced by sparse tokens: dropping tokens breaks the regular spatio-temporal grid and can misalign generated content with its intended position (Sections 1, 3, and 4).

## Approach and contributions

- **Adaptive VAE:** A transformer encoder produces continuous Gaussian latent tokens and keep/drop logits. Gumbel–Softmax enables differentiable selection during training; hard decisions remove tokens at inference. An \(\ell_1\) mask penalty encourages sample-dependent sparsity (Section 3.1; Figure 1).

- **Consistent sparse decoding:** The decoder applies the same mask as a bias on attention to the latent tokens. Coarse \(16^2\times8\) encoder patches and finer \(8^2\times4\) decoder queries keep the latent set compact while improving reconstruction detail (Sections 3.1 and 3.3).

- **Position-aware generation:** Sparse latents are used with flow matching. One variant jointly generates content and 3D positions with separate noise schedules; the default cascaded variant first generates an occupancy mask with an 8.3M-parameter prior and then conditions content generation on the selected coordinates (Sections 4.1–4.2; Figure 3).

- **Empirical contributions:** The paper evaluates adaptive allocation, reconstruction, video generation, training/inference efficiency, mask robustness, domain shift, and sparsity sensitivity. It also reports token count as an emergent control over generated motion and detail (Section 5; Supplement Sections 2–4).

|---|---|---|
| Strong reconstruction with fewer tokens | On approximately 5,000 Panda-70M validation videos at \(256^2\times16\), KATok reports 31.24 PSNR and 5.12 rFVD with 366 average tokens, versus 5,120 for OmniTokenizer-VAE and 3,846 for ElasticTok-KL. | Section 5.1; Table 1 |
| Scaling advantage at higher resolution | At \(512^2\times32\), KATok reports 33.23 PSNR and 6.40 rFVD with 1,554 tokens, versus OmniTokenizer-VAE’s 24.07, 16.85, and 32,768 tokens; KATok’s reported compression ratio rises from 134.21 to 253.00 across evaluated scales. | Section 5.1; Table 1; Figure 4 |

| Controlled generation improvement | With the same 686M SiT-XL backbone and training protocol on UCF-101 at \(256^2\), KATok obtains 61.53 gFVD with 366 tokens, versus 100.00 with 5,120 tokens for OmniTokenizer-VAE and 712.56 with 3,845 for ElasticTok-KL. | Table S3 |

| Sparse-selection components are necessary | Removing soft attention masking collapses the 100K-stage-1 run to the two register tokens, with PSNR 19.00 and SSIM 0.54; removing Gumbel–Softmax reportedly causes comparable collapse. | Section 5.3; Table 2 |
| Sparsity has a sharp quality threshold | After 70K steps with symmetric patches on Panda-70M, increasing \(\lambda_{\text{sparse}}\) from 0.01 to 0.02 changes average tokens from 372.3 to 34.9, PSNR from 28.78 to 22.46, and rFVD from 50.38 to 462.27. | Table S7 |

These results support the authors’ claim that adaptive sparsification can improve the measured fidelity–token trade-off and that explicit position handling is important for sparse generation. They do not establish universal superiority across architectures or deployment hardware: the strongest causal evidence comes from controlled SiT-XL comparisons, while several cross-paradigm results use different generator families, resolutions, or reported values from other papers.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated failure mode:** Videos combining fast motion with high-frequency detail can reconstruct as blurry, with reported PSNR around 19–20 on selected Panda-70M validation failures (Supplement Section 4; Figure S9).

- **Author-stated extreme-sparsity failure:** Asking the generator for fewer than three tokens—outside training conditions—produces near-uniform, single-color clips (Supplement Section 4).

- **Scheduler sensitivity:** Joint content–position generation requires careful tuning of its two noise schedules; the cascade avoids this search but adds a separately learned mask prior (Section 4.1).

- **Sharp sparsity trade-off:** Reconstruction collapses beyond a narrow range of sparsity weights, and the Gumbel–Softmax temperature schedule was not separately ablated (Table S7 and accompanying text).

- **Analyst inference — comparison scope:** Main reconstruction comparisons deliberately emphasize transformer-based continuous-token VAEs. Cross-paradigm comparisons are less controlled, while ElasticTok generation uses offline tokenization, added chunk markers, and only two datasets because of resource constraints (Sections 5.1–5.2; Supplement Section 2).

- **Analyst inference — train/inference mismatch:** The cascaded content model receives ground-truth positions during training but predicted mask-prior positions at inference. Mask-corruption experiments probe this risk, but only on Kinetics-600 and with random flips rather than all plausible structured errors (Section 4.2; Table S5).

- **Analyst inference — resource demands:** Tokenizer training uses multiple stages and a multi-node H200 cluster; reported speed results are specific to eight H200 GPUs. Performance and efficiency on smaller or different hardware are not established (Table S1; Section 5.2).

## Open research questions

1. **How robust is adaptive allocation on long, high-resolution videos?** Evaluation covers selected resolutions and clip lengths, but not genuinely long sequences. A next experiment could scale duration while holding motion statistics constant and measure token growth, memory, reconstruction, and temporal drift.

2. **Can the sparsity transition be made smoother?** Table S7 shows abrupt degradation between \(\lambda_{\text{sparse}}=0.01\) and \(0.02\). Testing constrained token-rate objectives, adaptive regularization, or rate–distortion training across multiple target budgets could reveal whether stable intermediate operating points exist.

3. **Can moving fine textures be preserved without sacrificing compression?** Figure S9 identifies this as the principal reconstruction failure. A targeted benchmark stratified by motion magnitude and spatial frequency could compare motion-aware selection, multiscale latents, and locally increased token budgets.

4. **Does token-count control independently govern motion and appearance?** The paper’s 200-versus-400-token results are qualitative and both properties change together. A controlled study with motion, identity, and scene complexity annotations could quantify causal effects and test separate controls for motion and visual detail.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: high.** The supplied text includes the main paper and substantial supplementary material, with method details, controlled comparisons, ablations, and failure analyses.

Material gaps remain: the numerical bodies of main Tables 1–4 are not fully reproduced; code and checkpoint availability is not established; random seeds, confidence intervals, and run-to-run variance are not reported; dataset preprocessing and sampling details are incomplete; and efficiency outside the eight-H200 setup is unknown.
