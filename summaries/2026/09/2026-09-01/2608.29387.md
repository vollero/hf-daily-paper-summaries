# EvoGenUI-Bench: Evaluating LLMs as Multi-Turn Generative UI Assistants

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-01.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.29387) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29387)


## One-sentence takeaway

EvoGenUI-Bench shows that leading LLMs can often produce a correct interface revision in isolation, but struggle to preserve executable behavior and synchronized local or external state across five-turn UI evolution.

## Problem and motivation

The paper studies whether LLMs can maintain one executable web interface while users successively add or revise requirements. This matters because generative UI combines interface construction with the environment through which users perform tasks, so polished individual outputs can conceal lost functionality, stale state, unwired controls, or claims unsupported by runtime behavior.

The authors argue that prior frontend-generation and agent benchmarks largely evaluate self-contained generation, revision, or operation within a predefined interface. They do not fully test cumulative maintenance of a persistent interface, including retention of earlier requirements and synchronization among visible behavior, local state, external state, and assistant responses (Sections 1–2).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Multi-turn benchmark:** EvoGenUI-Bench contains 150 human-authored five-turn tasks—750 requested turns—split equally among presentation, interaction, and tool-grounded scenarios (Sections 3.2–3.3; Table 1).

2. **Executable evaluation protocol:** Each generated React interface is built and run in a browser. Evaluation combines screenshots, DOM and source evidence, actor interaction traces, runtime logs, assistant text, and external-state readback where applicable (Sections 4.1–4.2; Figure 2).

3. **Cross-turn reliability metrics:** Alongside per-turn success, the benchmark measures complete five-turn success with TP@5, initial uninterrupted success with CPT, and conditional adjacent-turn retention with APR (Section 4.3).

4. **Comparative and diagnostic study:** Eight models are evaluated under a common harness, followed by a human-validated taxonomy assigning executed failures to six mechanisms such as information architecture, derived-state propagation, affordance binding, and external-state grounding (Sections 5–6; Tables 2 and 5; Figure 4).

5. **Released evaluation assets:** The authors report releasing benchmark v1.0, code, task data, mock fixtures, hidden validation material, pipeline, and reproduction instructions under the MIT License (Appendix A).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|

| Sustained success is low across models. | Macro-averaged across eight models, overall TP is 42.7%, TP@5 is 11.8%, CPT is 1.38 turns, and pooled APR is 66.5%. | Table 2 |
| Tool-grounded tasks are hardest under this benchmark design. | Model-averaged TP is 55.5% for Presentation, 47.6% for Interaction, and 25.0% for Tool-grounded; corresponding TP@5 values are 15.5%, 14.8%, and 5.0%, while APR is 71.1%, 68.7%, and 52.4%. | Performance declines after multiple revisions. | Aggregate pass rate falls to 39.4% at turn 3 and 35.1% at turn 4. Tool-grounded pass rate falls from 39.5% at turn 2 to 18.5% at turn 3 and 14.0% at turn 4. | Figure 3b; Section 5.3 |
| Failed retention reflects both regression and unmet new requirements. | Among 110 attributable failed APR transitions, 58 (52.7%) contain regression on prior behavior and 52 (47.3%) preserve prior behavior but fail the new requirement. | Table 5; Appendix C.2.1 |
| Interaction evidence is important to evaluator accuracy. | On a balanced 240-case ablation set, full-evidence accuracy is 87.5%; removing actor traces reduces it to 55.0%, versus 67.5% without source, 78.3% without screenshots, and 63.8% without private references. | Table 3; Section 5.4 |
| Automatic judgments have substantial but imperfect human agreement. | On a blinded 240-case validation sample, evaluator accuracy is 86.7% with Cohen’s κ=0.73; human inter-annotator Fleiss’ κ is 0.78. | Section 4.4 |

The evidence establishes that, under this fixed benchmark, evaluator, browser environment, and generation protocol, per-turn correctness is substantially easier than completing an entire revision sequence. It also supports distinct observed failure profiles across the three suites.

It does not establish production safety, performance in live services or other UI frameworks, or that external-state grounding alone causes the tool-grounded deficit: that suite also has considerably denser validation contracts. APR is conditional on the previous turn passing and is not a causal or direct regression metric.

## Limitations and validity risks

**Author-stated limitations:**

- Tasks are synthetic, human-authored five-turn episodes rather than naturally occurring interaction histories (Limitations, page 10).
- Evaluation is restricted to a fixed React/Vite browser environment and excludes other frameworks, accessibility requirements, and device conditions (Limitations, page 10).
- Deterministic mock runtimes omit live-service latency, authentication and permission failures, outages, rate limits, and API changes (Limitations, page 10).
- APR cannot distinguish regression from failure on a new request without additional attribution; comprehensive attribution would require replaying all prior validation contracts against later artifacts (Limitations, page 10).
- The benchmark is diagnostic and does not certify deployment safety (Ethical Considerations; Appendix A.1).

**Analyst inferences grounded in the text:**

- The harder tool-grounded results cannot isolate the effect of tool use because scenario type and validation density vary together; the authors explicitly acknowledge this design-level confound in Section 5.3.
- Main results use one generation per model–task–turn slot. Qwen3.6-Plus varies by 6.7 percentage points across three runs, so single-run model rankings may contain generation variance (Section 5.4; Table 8).
- MiMo-V2.5 serves as both the main actor and evaluator. Alternative-pairing and human-agreement diagnostics reduce, but do not eliminate, dependence on model-based evaluation (Sections 5.2 and 5.4; Table 7).
- The evaluator evidence ablation tests evidence removal rather than alternative non-model scoring systems, so it does not establish that the chosen judge is uniquely appropriate (Appendix D.2.1).

## Open research questions

1. **How much of tool-grounded difficulty comes from external-state synchronization rather than requirement density?**  
   The suites differ on both factors. A controlled experiment could match tasks for validation-contract size while varying only whether state is local or externally authoritative.

2. **Can training explicitly improve retention without reducing adaptation to new requests?**  
   Nearly half of audited APR failures involve only the new requirement, while slightly over half include prior regression. A next experiment could train or prompt models with explicit requirement ledgers and separately measure prior-contract replay and current-request success.

3. **Do the findings generalize to realistic development histories and platforms?**  
   The benchmark uses fixed five-turn synthetic tasks in React/Vite. Evaluation could be extended to naturally occurring interaction logs, longer trajectories, mobile layouts, and multiple frameworks.

4. **How robust are results under repeated generation and different evaluators?**  
   Only one model receives three full regeneration runs, and the primary experiment uses one actor–evaluator configuration. Multiple seeded runs for every generator, crossed with independent actors and evaluators, would quantify ranking stability and judge sensitivity.
