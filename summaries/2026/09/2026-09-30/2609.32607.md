# VoxMem: Benchmarking Multimodal Memory in Large Audio Language Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.32607) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32607)


## One-sentence takeaway
VoxMem evaluates memory across controlled multi-session audio histories and finds that all 15 tested models score below 40% overall accuracy at 32K, with particularly weak tracking of vocal and environmental states (§4.2–4.3, Figures 4–5).

## Problem and motivation
Spoken assistants must remember not only words, but also speakers, vocal delivery, and background sounds across separate interactions. The authors identify three gaps in prior benchmarks: incomplete coverage of acoustic evidence, fragmented memory operations, and predominantly single-session evaluation. Comparisons across history lengths can also confound context size with question difficulty (§1–2, Table 1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Taxonomy:** Crosses four evidence types—speech semantics, speaker identity, paralinguistic cues, environmental sound—with extraction, multi-session reasoning, temporal tracking, and answer refusal. It covers 15 combinations; semantic extraction is deliberately excluded (§3.1–3.2, Appendix A).
- **Benchmark:** Contains 799 questions instantiated at four reference budgets, producing 3,196 instances over 34,743 sessions and 177 hours. Nested histories preserve each question and its evidence while adding matched distractors and unrelated dialogue (§3.2, Figure 1).
- **Construction and validation:** Synthesizes user audio, supplies assistant replies as text, and uses paired acoustic controls plus session- and question-level checks to reduce leakage and validate evidence necessity (§3.2–3.3).
- **Evaluation and diagnosis:** Tests 15 existing models and analyzes failures by evidence, operation, and history length. This is an evaluation contribution, not a new memory model (§4, Appendix E).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Low overall memory accuracy | At 32K, the best of 15 models reaches **38.5%**; unweighted means are **33.0%** for five proprietary models and **21.9%** for ten open-weight models. Overall accuracy includes answerable and refusal items. | §4.2, Figure 4 |
| Non-lexical evidence is harder | At 32K, proprietary-group accuracy averages **55.6%** for semantics, **32.7%** for speakers, **20.0%** for paralinguistics, and **21.9%** for environmental sound, including refusal items. | §4.2, Figure 4 |
| Transcript replacement removes much acoustic-task performance | Gemini-3.7-Flash on answerable 8K questions drops from **46.2% to 4.4%** on audio-native evidence; semantic accuracy changes from **75.9% to 71.0%**. | §3.3, Table 2 |
| Acoustic temporal tracking is especially weak | Across 15 models at 32K, mean answerable TET accuracy is **44.5%** for semantics, **20.0%** for speakers, **3.4%** for paralinguistics, and **1.2%** for environmental sound. | §4.3, Figure 5 |
| More surrounding history reduces accuracy | For the same ten models and paired items, overall mean accuracy falls from **30.6% at 8K to 21.0% at 64K**: a **9.6 percentage-point** drop, with item-bootstrap **95% CI [8.3, 11.0]**. | Appendix D.4, Table 18 |
| Error profiles vary by acoustic evidence | At 64K, macro-averaged attributed errors include **48% binding failures** for speakers, **63% recognition/localization failures** for paralinguistics, and **39% unsupported answers** for environmental sound. Analysis excludes refusals, empty responses, and truncated answers. | Figure 7, Appendix E |
| Scoring is broadly consistent across judges | Re-scoring **11,978 predictions at 8K** gives **97.95% agreement**, **κ = 0.953**, and ranking correlation **ρ = 0.999**. | Appendix C.5, Table 13 |

These results establish substantial failures under VoxMem’s constructed input setting. Fixed questions and evidence strengthen the history-scaling comparison, but added history contains both more dialogue and more matched distraction; the experiment does not isolate those effects. Transcript controls support acoustic dependence, while response-based attribution does not establish internal model mechanisms. The authors’ explanation that acoustic refusal reflects processing difficulty remains an interpretation rather than a demonstrated causal result (§4.3).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Reported scope:** Speech is synthesized in English using fixed voice references; environmental sounds use a fixed mixing SNR. Assistant replies are text and deliberately avoid echoing answer-bearing details. **Analyst inference:** Transfer to naturally recorded, fully spoken conversations remains unestablished (Table 9, Appendix B.2, Appendix F).
- **Model coverage:** Only ten models complete 64K evaluation. The paper uses those same ten across budgets for its 64K scaling analysis, but this remains a restricted model subset (Appendix C.6, Table 14).
- **Measurement:** Quality control, scoring, and attribution rely on models. Cross-judge validation covers 8K predictions; the supplied text reports no human performance baseline or human audit of benchmark labels and error assignments (§3.3, Appendices C.5 and E).
- **Comparability:** Semantic extraction is excluded, and cell populations differ, so aggregate evidence-type scores are not fully matched operation comparisons (Figures 4–5).
- **Author cautions:** Small 8K ranking gaps should not be treated as meaningful given measured judge-family effects; some error subtypes rest on relatively few model × evidence cells (Appendix C.5, Table 20).
- **Analyst inference:** Retention estimates have overlapping confidence intervals, so their point ordering alone does not establish distinct degradation rates between evidence types (Table 18).

## Open research questions
1. **Does performance transfer to real speech?** The benchmark uses synthetic, controlled audio. A next experiment could human-validate naturally recorded counterparts with matched questions and evidence.
2. **How much degradation comes from length versus distractor competition?** Both increase together. Independently varying unrelated filler and same-key distractors would separate their effects.
3. **Can explicit acoustic memory improve binding and tracking?** The paper evaluates direct history processing rather than a new memory system. Compare speaker-linked and cue-linked stores against the same models and histories.
4. **Does correct refusal reflect evidence awareness?** Aggregate refusal accuracy does not resolve this. Measure paired answerable/refusal transitions for the same question and inspect whether models answer when evidence is restored.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported benchmark design and descriptive results; **medium** for causal interpretations and real-world generalization.

Material information unavailable in the supplied text includes human accuracy and annotation audits, natural-recording evaluation, a separate length-versus-distraction experiment, exact loaded checkpoint revisions and per-model inference settings deferred to the code release, and reasons five models could not complete 64K.
