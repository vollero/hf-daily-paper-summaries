# Selecting The Most Informative Tokens in Natural Language Autoencoders

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.37040) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37040)


## One-sentence takeaway
A ranker trained on chat structure retains at least 95.8% of exhaustive verbalization’s success at finding one threat-relevant explanation while inspecting 5% of positions on three datasets, but performs worse on Liars’ Bench (§4.5).

## Problem and motivation
Natural language autoencoders (NLAs) translate model activations into readable explanations, but generating an explanation at every token is expensive. Previous position-selection conventions—such as inspecting the final position or evenly spaced positions—can miss useful explanations. The authors investigate which inexpensive scores predict explanation relevance to a specific audit threat (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Token-level evaluation:** Generate explanations at every serialized token, including chat-template tokens, and label their threat relevance using DeepSeek-V4-Flash. The study covers 4,705,657 explanations across four models and four datasets (§§2.2–3).
- **Selection comparison:** Compare thirteen predictive-distribution, attention, and activation signals plus 234 two-signal ensembles against position and chat-structure logistic regressions. Selection and orientation are recalibrated within validation folds containing whole transcripts (§§2.1–2.3; Appendix F).
- **Budget-aware findings:** Evaluate precision and the probability of finding at least one or three relevant explanations under limited inspection budgets. Chat structure usually outperforms computational signals (§4.5).
- **Transfer demonstration:** Apply existing verbalizers unchanged to models fine-tuned to conceal a word, testing whether explanations recover information absent from identical prompt text (§4.2).

The paper studies selection using existing NLAs; their reconstruction-based training is background rather than a new contribution (§2; Appendix P).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Chat structure usually beats individual computational signals | Structure exceeds the best single signal in pooled AUROC in 12/14 dataset–model cells and case-macro AUROC in 11/14; structure is evaluated on held-out transcripts. | §4.3; Tables 2, 8 |
| Structure improves precision under small budgets | At eight explanations per transcript, average precision across ten sparse-relevance cells is 0.491 for structure, 0.392 for selected ensembles, and 0.191 for random selection. | §4.5; Appendix K |
| A small budget often retains exhaustive success | At 5% of positions, structure’s success relative to exhaustive verbalization is 0.995 on OpenPromptInjection, 0.958 on taboo organisms, 1.000 on Tensor Trust, and 0.813 on Liars’ Bench; success requires at least one on-task explanation, with equal model weighting. Random achieves 0.761, 0.594, and 1.000 on the first three datasets. | §4.5; Fig. 3 |
| Requiring redundant evidence reduces success | At the same budget, requiring three on-task explanations gives normalized success of 0.785, 0.568, and 0.998 on OpenPromptInjection, taboo organisms, and Tensor Trust respectively. | §4.5 |
| Activation signals dominate single-signal comparisons | Activation-based signals are strongest in direction-adjusted pooled AUROC in 11/14 cells; attention signals lead the remaining three. | §4.3; Tables 2, 8 |
| Ensembles improve held-out ranking | Model-best ensemble selection improves held-out case-macro AUROC over individual-signal selection in all 14 cells, by 0.0048–0.1941; paired transcript-bootstrap 95% intervals exclude zero in 13 cells. | Appendix L; Table 10 |
| Unchanged verbalizers recover concealed words | Across four base models, own-word recovery averaged over three concealed words is 14.3%–26.8% of positions. On identical shared prompt text, recovery is 12.2%–25.3%, versus at most 2.82% for another organism’s word. | §4.2; Table 5 |
| Cross-model transfer is task-dependent | Label-free leave-one-model-out OpenPromptInjection case-macro AUROC ranges from 0.778–0.820; taboo-organism ensembles do not consistently outperform transferred individual signals. | Appendix D |

These results establish efficiency for obtaining **judge-labeled relevant explanations** in the tested settings. They do not establish explanation faithfulness, successful threat detection, or model safety. The identical-prompt concealed-word experiment supports activation-dependent recovery rather than copying prompt text (§4.2).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Labels come from one evaluation model; independent judging and human review remain untested. Only one verbalizer layer per model and relatively short transcripts are evaluated (§5).
- **Author-stated:** Tensor Trust’s question also accepts explanations of an unattacked model following its protection instruction, limiting threat specificity (§4.1). Its high base relevance also leaves little selection headroom.
- **Author-stated:** Liars’ Bench models receive different on-policy conversations, confounding model and data differences; the selected signal combination is not claimed to be deception-specific (Appendix O).
- **Analyst inference:** Structure’s advantage may depend on stable threat placement within chat roles, which the authors explicitly identify in the dataset setup. Whole-transcript validation does not test changed conversation layouts (§3).
- **Analyst inference:** Reduced explanation counts support potential compute savings, but the supplied text does not measure end-to-end latency or monetary cost.
- Large-scale reproduction has a stated hardware burden: the largest model may require up to four B200 GPUs (p. 10).

## Open research questions
1. **Does structure remain effective when threat placement changes?** The datasets place threats in predictable conversation components (§3). Next experiment: move equivalent threats across roles and turns, then evaluate frozen selectors.
2. **Do relevant explanations provide correct audit evidence?** Labels measure relevance, with one judge (§5). Next experiment: use blinded human and independent-model review against transcript-level threat annotations.
3. **Does selection generalize to longer transcripts and other layers?** Both remain untested (§5). Next experiment: repeat budget evaluation across layer depths and long agent transcripts using compatible verbalizers.
4. **How broadly do verbalizers transfer after fine-tuning?** Recovery is demonstrated for three concealed words, while selector transfer is inconsistent (§4.2; Appendix D). Next experiment: vary concealed concepts and fine-tuning objectives while keeping verbalizers fixed.
