# Understanding On-Policy Distillation: A Mechanistic Interpretability Perspective via Sparse Crosscoders

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.35210) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35210)


## One-sentence takeaway
Across the tested math-reasoning settings, sparse-crosscoder measurements suggest that on-policy distillation and teacher-rollout SFT warm-up mainly change the use of existing shared features, with interventions supporting a causal role for warm-up reweighting in accuracy gains.

## Problem and motivation
On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student on its own rollouts using teacher token-level supervision, but whether it transfers new internal features or changes how existing features are used remains unclear. The authors identify two gaps: prior OPD analyses largely examine outputs, and standard crosscoders jointly encode models into one feature code, preventing reliable attribution of feature-usage changes to individual checkpoints (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Swap readout:** Put the same student checkpoint’s activation into both student slots of a three-model BatchTopK crosscoder while holding the teacher input fixed. This cancels the difference between student encoders and permits checkpoint comparisons within a fixed dictionary, including checkpoints absent from crosscoder training (§2.2; Appendix B).
- **Representation-level OPD analysis:** Compare a common student distilled from teachers differing through RL, scale, or both; measure feature attribution, firing changes, and associations with decision tokens (§3).
- **Warm-up analysis and intervention:** Decompose teacher-rollout SFT changes into components aligned with and outside direct OPD’s direction, then add or remove the decoded feature change during generation without updating model weights (§4; Appendix D.4).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| No detected feature gains or losses after OPD | Across JustRL, Skywork, and R1-7B, none fired at least 10 times in one student checkpoint and zero times in the other, on roughly 3M held-out tokens; no OPD student dominated a feature. | §3.1; Fig. 3; Appendix D.1 |
| Most frequent features changed little | For features firing at least 100 times across the two checkpoints, 98.2%, 99.6%, and 99.4% stayed within a firing-rate factor of 1.2, respectively. | Fig. 3d; Appendix D.1 |
| Teacher-dominant features remained teacher-dominant | Skywork and R1-7B had 42 and 30 such features; mean student MAS remained 0.19 before and after OPD in both settings, with no feature’s MAS changing by more than 0.015. | §3.1 |
| Accuracy improved despite feature preservation | Mean avg@256 across AIME24/AIME25/AMC23 rose from 41.6% for the base student to 56.6%, 49.4%, and 43.5% under JustRL, Skywork, and R1-7B. | Table 6 |
| Decision-token enrichment varied by teacher | Decision-token features constituted 12%, 4%, and 0% of the 50 most-changed features, respectively, versus 0.4% among all features. | §3.2; Fig. 4a |
| Warm-up improved subsequent OPD | Qwen3 student mean avg@8 increased from 17.6% with direct OPD to 22.0% with warm-up + OPD; base and teacher scored 9.7% and 36.3%. Warm-up alone scored 18.9%. | Tables 2, 7 |
| Warm-up partly anticipated OPD and retained distinct changes | Across 8,524 frequent features, alignment slope was 0.46; the residual carried 56% of warm-up’s change by norm after excluding one dominant garbled-character feature, and persisted after OPD with Spearman correlation 0.69. | §4.2; Fig. 6; Appendix D.3 |
| Feature intervention transferred much of the warm-up benefit | With eight samples per problem and an 8,192-token generation cap, adding the change raised direct OPD from 18.6% to 21.3% mean accuracy: +2.7 points, 95% bootstrap interval [+0.5, +6.0]. Removing it lowered warm-up + OPD from 21.9% to 19.2%: −2.7 points [−5.3, −0.4]. Shuffled controls did not establish corresponding benefits or harms. | Table 3; Appendix D.4 |
| Readout reconstruction supported checkpoint comparisons | Student swap-readout FVE ranged from 0.814 to 0.823 in the three OPD settings; unseen warm-up checkpoints achieved 0.756 and 0.759 in the Qwen3 crosscoder. | Table 9; Appendix C.4 |

These results support preservation and reweighting **within the learned dictionaries and sampled activation sites**. The intervention provides causal evidence that the identified warm-up change affects accuracy, but does not isolate which feature groups account for the gain. Table 3 uses a different generation cap from Tables 2 and 7, so its absolute accuracies should be compared within its own conditions.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** The SFT conclusion concerns warm-up on the same teacher’s rollouts for the subsequent math task, rather than SFT generally (§4.1).
- **Analyst inference—measurement coverage:** One middle-layer site, finite dictionaries, firing thresholds, and incomplete reconstruction leave room for undetected changes elsewhere. “No new features” is therefore stronger than the directly observed absence of new crosscoder features (Appendices C.4, D.1).
- **Analyst inference—generalization:** OPD uses one shared recipe and final checkpoints; warm-up is examined in one teacher–student pairing. Other tasks, recipes, and training trajectories remain untested (Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference—causal specificity:** Interventions transfer the whole token-dependent warm-up change and require multiple models during generation. They do not establish a compact, student-only mechanism or the necessity of individual feature groups (Appendix D.4).
- **Analyst inference—uncertainty:** Reported bootstrap intervals address benchmark-problem variation, not independent training-run variation. Crosscoder seed 42 is specified, but repeated-seed results are not reported (Table 8; Appendix D.4).
- The shuffled intervention is similar but unequal in magnitude: 4.5% versus 6.2% of residual-stream norm, limiting strict control matching (Appendix D.4).

## Open research questions
- **Does feature preservation hold across layers and dictionary choices?** Only one activation site and one dictionary configuration are tested. Repeat the analysis across layers, sparsities, dictionary sizes, and seeds.
- **Which warm-up changes cause the accuracy gain?** The intervention combines aligned and residual components. Intervene separately on these components and on format, reasoning-style, and notation feature groups.
- **Can other training regimes produce detectable feature acquisition?** The evidence covers one OPD recipe and teacher-rollout warm-up. Compare longer training, alternative supervision sources, and non-math tasks using matched readouts.
- **Can the intervention work without auxiliary models?** Its change is recomputed from base-student, warmed-student, and teacher activations. Test whether a student-only predictor of that change preserves the benefit.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text includes detailed methods, controls, and appendices, supporting the measured findings; the broader claim that OPD cannot acquire new features exceeds the demonstrated coverage.

Material information unavailable: verified code/checkpoint releases, compute costs, repeated training-seed results, layer and crosscoder-configuration ablations, uncertainty intervals for most descriptive findings, and interventions isolating individual feature groups.
