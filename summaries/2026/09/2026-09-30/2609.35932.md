# Same Bytes, Different Authority: Reserved-Token Representations in Chat-Template Prompt Injection

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.35932) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35932)


## One-sentence takeaway
Byte-identical forged chat markers produce substantially stronger prompt injections when encoded as reserved tokens on three tested model families, but adaptive ordinary-token substitutions recover much of the attack after tokenizer defenses (§§4.2, 4.5).

## Problem and motivation
Untrusted tool output can imitate the role boundaries that distinguish instructions from data in an agent’s conversation. The same marker text can reach the model as either a reserved control token or ordinary subwords, depending on server-side tokenization.

The authors identify a confound in prior template attacks: changing marker spelling changes both visible text and token IDs. Their goal is to isolate the authority associated with reserved representations and measure what tokenizer-side mitigation actually removes (§§1, 3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled measurement:** Compare byte-identical prompts under Reserved, Split, and Matched encodings. Matched retains reserved markers while splitting ordinary text to equal Split’s token count; the identity gap is ASR(Matched) − ASR(Split). Plaintext provides a separate baseline without template markers (§3, Table 1).
- **Mechanism probes:** Test alternative split rules and token placements, replace marker input vectors, compare base and instruction-tuned checkpoints, and suppress reasoning (§§4.3–4.4).
- **Deployment evaluation:** Measure execution-level attacks in AgentDojo, audit tokenizer mitigation coverage, construct a source-aware encoder, and evaluate adaptive marker search (§4.5; Apps. A.3, F–G).
- **Reusable assets:** The paper lists code and a Byte-Authority-Evaluation dataset; their contents and availability were not independently verified (p. 1).

## Evidence and main results

DH denotes direct harm; DS denotes data stealing. Gaps are percentage points (pp).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Large reserved-representation effect on three families | InjecAgent, 400 paired cases per attack type, repeated greedy runs: Matched-minus-Split gaps were Llama-3.1-8B **58.2 DH / 50.2 DS**, GLM-4.5 **60.0 / 65.5**, and Seed-OSS-36B **58.6 / 39.3 pp**; significant in every primary run. | Tables 2, 10 |
| Qwen3-8B differs | Same protocol: **8.1 pp DH** gap, 95% interval **[2.2, 14.2]**; **0.5 pp DS**, interval **[−6.2, 5.8]**, not significant. Reserved and Matched differed by at most **1.7 pp** across all primary configurations. | Tables 2, 10 |
| A single replacement vector can preserve attack strength | Fixed-position embedding swaps, 510 DH cases: Llama-3.1 ASR was **98.2%** with reserved vectors, **98.4%** with nearest ordinary-token vectors, and **58.4%** with subword-mean vectors. Qwen3’s nearest ordinary vectors did not fully recover reserved performance. | Table 4; App. C.5 |
| Instruction tuning increases marker preference | Three base/instruct pairs, 200 cases, teacher-forced attacker-minus-user tool logits: identity-gap shifts were **+7.67**, **+1.14**, and **+2.92 logits** for Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-8B, and Seed-OSS-36B. This is a logit probe, not generated attack success. | Table 26 |
| Reasoning suppression changes Qwen3’s dependence on markers | Qwen3-8B DH, 400 cases, five runs with both settings together: gap increased from **8.8 to 49.8 pp**; Split ASR fell from **74.7% to 35.7%**, while Matched rose from **83.5% to 85.5%**. | Table 27 |
| The effect reaches executed agent tasks | AgentDojo, 409 held-out pairs across four suites, three repeats: execution-level gaps were **10.3 pp [7.5, 13.1]** for Qwen3-8B and **6.8 pp [4.7, 9.1]** for Qwen3-32B. | App. F; Table 28 |
| Standard mitigation has a coverage gap | Among 400 selected chat checkpoints, **33 of 67** distinct tokenizer configurations, covering **255 checkpoints**, retained reachable tool-protocol tokens. Forged tool-channel DH gaps were **9.4–19.9 pp** across four tested models. | Apps. G.1–G.2; Table 31 |
| Adaptive search substantially weakens the defense | Held-out DH evaluation after searching **115–133** candidate spellings per model on separate calibration cases: defended best ASR was **81.2%, 92.2%, 70.4%, 72.6%** for Qwen3, Llama, GLM, and Seed, respectively—only **4.5, 5.8, 1.6, 12.2 pp** below each model’s best undefended attack. | Tables 5, 32; App. G.4 |

These interventions support a substantial causal contribution from reserved marker representations under the tested conditions. They also establish that removing reserved IDs alone is insufficient against the evaluated adaptive search.

The authors’ explanation—that post-training teaches these vectors to signal instruction-bearing boundaries—is an interpretation consistent with the probes, rather than a directly traced training mechanism (§4.4). The evidence does not establish universal behavior across models or equivalence between tool-call selection and completed harm.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Self-hosted open-weight inference only; string-only hosted APIs are outside scope. Embedding swaps are diagnostic interventions that an attacker cannot directly submit as text (p. 9; App. A.1).
- **Outcome limits:** InjecAgent measures the next parsed attacker-tool call; AgentDojo measures execution. The latter has narrower model/suite coverage, including Llama evaluated only on banking because of low completion elsewhere (App. F).
- **Analyst inference—mechanism coverage:** Vector swaps cover only Qwen3-8B and Llama-3.1-8B; base/instruct comparisons cover three pairs. Broader claims about representation and training require further testing (Apps. C.5, E.1).
- **Control qualification:** Extra-token cost is small in the primary results, but equivalence to zero is not established for Qwen3-8B. Some alternative placements and per-character controls impose appreciable costs on Llama (Tables 12, 21–22).
- **Utility uncertainty:** Benign AgentDojo utility differences were nonsignificant, but the study was powered to detect roughly a **13.6 pp** difference, leaving smaller costs unresolved (App. F).
- **Comparison and sampling limits:** Family-specific budgets and GLM’s FP8 serving prevent straightforward cross-family ranking. The census excludes tokenizers requiring custom code (App. A.2; §4.1; App. G.1).

## Open research questions
- **Do injection-resistant training methods remove representation-based authority?** They are proposed as future targets, not evaluated. Next experiment: apply the fixed-bytes protocol and adaptive search to such checkpoints (§5).
- **Which training stages create marker preference?** Base/instruct comparisons show a shift without isolating its source. Next experiment: evaluate intermediate checkpoints from controlled post-training stages (App. E.1).
- **Can defenses withstand ordinary embedding-neighbor attacks?** Search recovers most attack strength despite excluding reserved IDs. Next experiment: compare tokenizer encoding alone with representation-aware training under held-out adaptive attacks (App. G.4).
- **What is the broader benign utility cost?** Existing utility testing cannot resolve small regressions. Next experiment: expand agent tasks and include benign documents that quote control markers verbatim (Apps. F, G.3).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported within-model contrasts; **medium** for the proposed training mechanism and deployment generalization.

Material unavailable information includes independently verified code and dataset contents, the census collection date, broader utility measurements, and experiments on injection-resistant checkpoints or hosted APIs.
