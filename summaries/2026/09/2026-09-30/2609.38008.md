# HybridCUA: Learning to Orchestrate GUI and CLI for Computer-Use Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.38008) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38008)


## One-sentence takeaway
HybridCUA combines mixed-interface demonstrations with CLI-aware reinforcement learning, raising OSWorld mean evaluator score from 38.8% to 53.6% while reducing average environment steps from 31.6 to 14.0 relative to its GUI-only base model (Table 1; Appendix E).

## Problem and motivation
GUI actions offer broad application coverage but can require long, error-prone sequences; application-specific tools reduce interaction costs but require bespoke integration. The authors propose combining GUI interaction with shell commands, arguing that existing agents lack supervision for both interface selection and reliable command execution (§1).

Their motivating evidence is that simply exposing Qwen3.5-9B to CLI access lowers its OSWorld score from 38.8% to 18.4% (Table 1). Shell availability alone therefore does not establish effective orchestration.

## Approach and contributions
- **Hybrid supervision:** HybridCUA-8K contains 5,023 successful supervised trajectories across GUI-only, CLI-only, and interleaved modes, plus 3,000 verified RL tasks. Construction combines GUI-action conversion, teacher-model sampling, and replay-verified rewriting of terminal interactions into direct commands (§3.2; Appendix C).
- **Explicit interface rewards:** After supervised fine-tuning, GRPO rewards successful trajectories whose use of any direct CLI command matches a task-specific preference label. A separate action-level penalty targets shell execution failures (§3.3). Labels come from comparing teacher rollouts under three interface modes (Appendix C.2).
- **Unified execution and trained model:** HybridCUA-9B starts from Qwen3.5-9B and represents GUI operations through PyAutoGUI heredocs alongside direct commands in one `bash` action. Observations include screenshots and preceding CLI output (§3.1). Despite broader descriptions of training on the task pool, Appendix D.2 specifies that online RL uses 1,000 tasks sampled from the 3,000-task pool.

The authors promise release of the model, data, and construction/training pipelines; the supplied text does not establish that release has occurred (§3; Reproducibility Statement).

All benchmark scores are **mean evaluator scores**, including fractional credit where returned, rather than uniformly strict task-success rates. Steps are averaged over all tasks, including failures and control actions. Evaluation uses 361 OSWorld tasks, 361 OSWorld-MCP tasks, and 154 WindowsAgentArena tasks, with one run per model and benchmark and a 50-step limit (Appendix E).

The results support improved benchmark performance and fewer environment interactions. They do not establish statistical robustness, lower wall-clock cost, or broadly reliable real-world operation. OSWorld-MCP reuses OSWorld’s tasks, so its gains demonstrate environment transfer rather than unseen-task generalization. The authors’ interpretation that Windows transfer reflects learned routing rather than memorized commands is plausible but not isolated experimentally (§4.4).

## Limitations and validity risks
**Author-stated:** Benefits depend on available, stable CLIs. Restricted shells and operating-system-specific semantics may change routing requirements. Training concentrates on OSWorld applications, and evaluation does not cover the full diversity of applications, permissions, operating systems, or long workflows (Appendix A). Deployment safety lies outside the sandboxed study’s scope (Ethics Statement).

**Analyst inferences grounded in the text:**

- Single-run evaluations provide no reported uncertainty estimates or repeated-run stability evidence (Appendix E).
- One `bash` action can bundle multiple operations, so fewer environment steps need not imply proportionally less computation or elapsed time (Appendix G.1).
- The routing label captures whether a trajectory uses **any** CLI command; it does not directly supervise every interface switch. Preferences are calibrated using one teacher model, leaving their portability across agent capabilities untested (§3.3; Appendix C.2).
- Comparisons allow different interface capabilities. Exact MCP tool access and the Windows command-execution configuration are not specified in the supplied protocols (Appendix E).
- Table 4 compares schemas with different GUI representations and prompts; attributing its entire gain specifically to a shared grammar remains uncertain (Appendices G.1–G.2).

## Open research questions
- **Do routing labels transfer across model capabilities?** Labels are derived from Qwen3.8-27B rollouts. Re-label the same tasks with several teachers and compare downstream routing and performance (Appendix C.2).
- **Does reduced interaction count reduce actual cost?** Bundled actions complicate the step metric. Measure elapsed time, tokens, execution cost, and success-conditioned steps alongside evaluator scores (Appendices E, G.1).
- **How robust is orchestration under restricted or changing CLIs?** These settings are acknowledged limitations. Evaluate permission restrictions, missing utilities, and changed command semantics on held-out applications (Appendix A).
- **Would finer routing supervision help?** The current reward checks task-level CLI presence. Compare it with switch-level or operation-level rewards under the same RL budget (§3.3).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** Methods and benchmark results are documented in detail, but causal and generalization claims have narrower support.

Material missing information includes repeated-run uncertainty, wall-clock and total training costs, exact shell-failure detection rules, CLI-preference label counts, the sampled RL-task composition, detailed Windows/MCP execution access, and confirmed availability of the promised artifacts.
