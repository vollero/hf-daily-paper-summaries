# SEAD: A State-Based Perspective on Attack and Defense in Tool-Using Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.34518) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34518)


## One-sentence takeaway
SEAD models agent safety through partially observed state transitions, pairing execution-guided attack search with a state-investigating defense that reduces executable attack success from 48.0% to 4.0% in its online evaluation (Table 5).

## Problem and motivation
Earlier tool actions can change files, permissions, or records so that a later routine-looking action causes harm. Visible interaction history may omit the state needed to distinguish safe preparation from harmful execution. The authors identify a gap in jointly modeling attacker feedback, defender intervention timing, and the continuation after a block (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions
- **SEAD formulation:** A shared execution loop models attacker instructions, target-selected tool actions, and pre-execution defense under partial observation. It defines a goal-specific harm-enabling transition and separates recorded-path interception from online protection (§3).
- **DART attack:** Searches alternative executed trajectory prefixes, combining executable progress with model-based proposal and execution assessments. Replayed environments let sibling candidates start from the same prefix (§4.1; Appendix D.3).
- **SAGE defense:** A fine-tuned model optionally gathers private, read-only state evidence before allowing or blocking each pending action, including replanned actions after refusal (§4.2).
- **Evaluation assets:** Adapts MT-AgentRisk tasks into controlled, replayable environments with executable checks; separately constructs OAS-derived benign trajectories to measure preservation (§5.1; Appendices D.1, F.1). The authors report code and data availability in the abstract.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| DART leads the tested attack baselines | On 187 tasks per target across four models, Semantic ASR exceeds the best competing baseline by 18.8–35.9 percentage points; Hard ASR improves by 8.1–17.9 points. Comparators: Direct, MTA, STAC, Intent Hijacking. | Table 2; §5.2 |
| SAGE preserves benign recorded execution while intercepting most selected harmful paths | On 95 benign trajectories, all-pass rate is 95.79%. On 55 Semantic-success DART paths, first blocks are 58.18% early, 34.55% exact, 0% late, and 7.27% missed—92.73% intercepted by closure. | Table 3; §5.3 |
| SAGE has the best reported offline harmonic trade-off scores | On those same sets, timing-aware interception score H2 is 58.79%; harmonic scores F1/F2 are 50.78%/72.86%, highest among evaluated defenders. | Table 3 |
| Online protection substantially reduces DART success | On 75 tasks with GPT-5.6 Luna, Semantic ASR falls from 65.3% without defense to 10.7%; Hard ASR falls from 48.0% to 4.0%. | Table 5; §5.4 |
| Protection transfers to held-out tool domains | On 30 PostgreSQL/Web tasks excluded from SAGE fine-tuning, DART Semantic successes fall from 20 to 3 and Hard successes from 9 to 1. Domain-specific read-only tools are supplied at inference. | §5.4; Figure 3 |
| Overall protection extends across tested attacks | With SAGE on the same 75-task setting, Hard ASR is 1.3% for MTA, 4.0% for STAC, 6.7% for Intent Hijacking, and 4.0% for DART; all decrease from their undefended counterparts. | Appendix D.5; Figure 6 |

These results support effectiveness within the tested environments and budgets. Offline interception is measured on selected successful recorded paths; it does not establish safety after replanning. Online results address that continuation, but benign utility is evaluated separately offline. “Hard” success is operational verifier success: some checks inspect state, while others inspect executed target-authored text (Appendix F.1).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** The interface assumes consequential tool actions pass through a trusted gate. Concurrent processes, compound actions, and disclosure through assistant output require extensions (Appendices B–C).
- **Author-stated:** Defense evaluation uses one target model and four tool domains. Controlled studies of individual search and investigation components remain future work (Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference:** Component effects are not isolated. Binary and SAGE controls differ in training data and optimization as well as investigation capability (Appendix D.6).
- **Analyst inference:** Attack comparisons use method-specific default budgets rather than matched execution or compute budgets, limiting attribution of DART’s advantage solely to its mechanism (Appendix F.4).
- **Analyst inference:** Goal-specific, judge-annotated closure labels approximate the formal harm boundary; they do not establish that every earlier action is safe under every possible harmful objective (§4.2; Appendix D.6).
- SAGE still blocks many paths before closure, and the supplied text reports no uncertainty intervals or repeated-run variability (Table 3; Appendix D.4).

## Open research questions
- **How much does investigation itself improve protection?** Existing comparisons do not isolate it. Next experiment: disable queries in the same SAGE checkpoint and compare online ASR, benign completion, and interception timing.
- **Does online protection preserve legitimate task completion?** Benign preservation is measured on fixed trajectories. Next experiment: run fresh benign tasks with blocking and replanning enabled, measuring completion and investigation cost.
- **How robust is protection across targets and concurrent state changes?** Defense uses one target and assumes the gated execution interface. Next experiment: evaluate additional targets and introduce controlled state changes between investigation and execution.
- **Does DART retain its advantage under matched resources?** Default method budgets differ. Next experiment: compare attacks at equal target executions and model-token budgets.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported methods and aggregate findings; causal attribution and broader deployment validity remain uncertain.

Material information unavailable in the supplied text includes component-isolating ablations, repeated-run uncertainty, aggregate latency/token costs, online benign-completion results, detailed task-by-task verifier predicates, and independent confirmation that the reported released artifacts are accessible.
