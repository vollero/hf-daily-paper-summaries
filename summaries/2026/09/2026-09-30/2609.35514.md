# One Proposal for Every Margin: Zero-Shot Amortized Sequential Importance Sampling for Binary Matrices

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.35514) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35514)


## One-sentence takeaway
MarginFlow learns one GFlowNet-based sequential importance proposal across fixed-margin binary matrices, achieving a reported median effective sample fraction of 99.8% on 1,190 held-out margins while preserving unbiased count estimation through importance weights (Table 1; §3.2).

## Problem and motivation
The paper addresses counting binary matrices with prescribed row and column sums and sampling from their uniform distribution. These tasks support conditional statistical inference in ecology, psychometrics, and network analysis (§1–2.1).

Exact counting becomes expensive as the number of columns grows. Sequential importance sampling (SIS) provides independent weighted draws and an unbiased count estimator, but poorly matched proposals produce highly variable weights. The authors identify a gap in analytically designed proposals: their accuracy varies across margins, and selecting a good configuration requires additional work (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Counting–flow correspondence:** The authors prove that a unit-reward GFlowNet on the row-by-row construction tree has flows equal to completion counts. Its ideal forward policy is therefore the zero-variance SIS proposal (§3.1, Lemma 3.1).
- **Learning without known counts:** Training minimizes the variance of sampled log importance weights. The expected gradient follows reverse KL toward the uniform target; counts still come from averaging importance weights rather than fitting a GFlowNet normalizer (§3.1, Theorems 3.2–3.3).
- **Amortization across margins:** A shared set transformer reads reduced margins. It scores feasible row types that group columns with equal reduced sums, then samples uniformly within the selected type. Its logits combine exact type multiplicity, a Harrison–Miller analytical prior, and a learned correction (§3.2).
- **Zero-shot evaluation:** One trained checkpoint per seed serves unseen margins without instance-specific training or proposal selection (§4.1; Appendix B.3).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong overall zero-shot proposal quality | On 1,190 held-out margins, median ESS/N was 99.8%, versus 99.4% for the per-margin best of 31 analytical configurations and 97.4% for untrained Harrison–Miller. Win/tie/loss: 415/772/3, with ties within 0.02 nats. | Table 1, p. 8 |
| Largest improvement on difficult margins | On 56 margins where the post-hoc best had ESS/N below approximately 37%, median ESS/N increased from 10.3% to 94.1%; MarginFlow won all 56. | Table 1, p. 8 |
| Results combine exact and sampled evaluation | Exact limiting ESS/N was computed on 681 margins. For 509 larger state graphs, estimates used 4,000 draws and medians over 16 repetitions; MarginFlow results aggregate three training seeds. | §4.1, p. 8; Appendix B.5 |
| Analytical initialization improves difficult cases | On the 50 hardest jointly evaluated ablation margins, median ESS/N was 81.6% without the analytical term versus 95.1% with it. Seven ablation evaluations did not finish. | Table 2; Appendix C.1, pp. 17–18 |
| Extrapolation works on one structured failure family | On the 376×301 test margin, exact draws per effective sample were 1.21 for MarginFlow’s median seed, versus 13.6 for Harrison–Miller and 2.3×10²⁷ for CDHL; MarginFlow’s seed range was 1.02–2.87. | Table 3; Appendix C.3, p. 19 |
| More precise counting on known-count cases | Across 18 margins, median standard deviation of log counts was 0.0007 for MarginFlow and 0.017 for CDHL, both using 4,000 draws; the 60-minute Curveball counter achieved 0.015. Reported times were 8.5 seconds on one GPU, 42 seconds on one core, and 3,600 seconds on one core, respectively. | Table 4; Appendix C.4, p. 19 |
| Deployment throughput improves under mixed hardware | On 12 of 13 timed margins, MarginFlow delivered 1.2–9.2 times Harrison–Miller’s effective draws per second, using an A100 for network inference versus CPU execution for analytical proposals. | Table 6; Appendix C.5, p. 21 |

These results support strong proposal quality within the evaluated margin distributions and computational caps. “Matches or beats” includes the stated tie tolerance. Unbiasedness applies to the full-support importance-weight count estimator; it does not guarantee that every finite run finds rare heavy weights or that unweighted learned draws are exactly uniform (§2.1; Appendix B.5). The timing evidence does not isolate algorithmic gains from hardware differences.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scalability boundary:** Enumerating row types grows costly with the number of distinct reduced column sums. Training and evaluation use type caps; the authors propose a more expensive group-by-group policy for broader coverage (Appendix D).
- **Author-stated metric risk:** Sample-based ESS can be optimistic when rare heavy-weight regions are missed. Cross-proposal count agreement provides a check, but exact references are unavailable for the larger state graphs (Appendix B.5).
- **Author-stated ablation incompleteness:** Seven difficult cases timed out without the analytical prior, making the completed-case comparison favorable to that ablation (Appendix C.1).
- **Analyst inference—generalization remains bounded:** Held-out seeds, sources, interpolation, and one extrapolation family do not establish reliable performance on arbitrary feasible margins or the proposed nonbinary extensions.
- **Analyst inference—medians hide difficult cases:** Table 8 includes substantially lower fractions and seed variation, such as 48.8% median ESS/N on a dense 600×20 bimodal margin.
- **Resource limitation:** Each training seed costs about 25 hours on eight A100s. Deployment timing excludes this amortized cost, and CPU/GPU comparisons are unequal (§4.1; Appendix C.5).

## Open research questions
- **Can the proposal scale beyond row-type enumeration?** The authors identify enumeration as the main boundary. A next experiment could train the group-by-group policy and compare quality, memory, and throughput on margins exceeding the current caps (Appendix D).
- **How robust is training to unseen heavy-weight regions?** Reverse KL training and sampled ESS need not reveal these regions reliably away from the optimum. Test deliberately difficult margins with exact counts where feasible, increasing sampling budgets to measure ESS stability (§3.1; Appendix B.5).
- **When does training repay its computational cost?** The paper reports training expense and mixed-hardware deployment speed. Compare GPU implementations of analytical baselines and measure total cost across increasing numbers of deployed margins (Appendix C.5).
- **Does amortization transfer to additional constraints?** Integer entries, structural zeros, and prescribed-degree graphs are proposed extensions without reported experiments. Start with small exactly countable instances and test whether suitably enriched state inputs retain proposal quality (Appendix D).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the supplied method description and reported results; broader applicability remains uncertain.

Material unavailable information includes release status and access details for code, checkpoints, and exact splits; exact counts for the larger evaluated state graphs; matched-hardware baseline timings; completed no-prior results for the seven timed-out cases; and experimental validation of the proposed extensions.
