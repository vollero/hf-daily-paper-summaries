# CrossBFM: Distilling a Shared Latent Behavior Space Across Humanoid Embodiments

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.38087) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38087)


## One-sentence takeaway
CrossBFM distills a frozen humanoid behavior space into a shared encoder and robot-specific tracking policies, transferring three prompting modes across three target humanoids while showing strongly morphology-dependent generalization to unseen robots (Sections 4–5).

## Problem and motivation
Behavior Foundation Models (BFMs) support motion tracking, goal reaching, and reward prompting through a latent behavior space, but independently training each robot is expensive and produces incompatible latent coordinates. The authors identify a gap between this reusable behavior interface and prior cross-embodiment methods that primarily share morphology representations or address individual tasks (Sections 1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Transfer an existing behavioral coordinate system:** Freeze the G1 BFM’s backward map and use synchronized, retargeted motion frames as supervised latent labels for other robots. This replaces target-side backward-map learning with cosine regression; robot control still requires PPO training (Sections 4.1, 4.3–4.4).
- **Share one encoder across embodiments:** A transformer consumes canonical joint, body, and root features with presence masks and scale normalization. It has no robot-specific learned weights, although each robot requires configuration maps and its own tracker (Section 4.2; Appendices A.2–A.5).
- **Adapt prompting and extend generation:** Target-side reward inference uses cached retargeted states and percentile-mapped arm-height thresholds. A conditional rectified-flow model generates shared latent sequences from behavior modes and history; free-form text is proposed as an extension, not demonstrated quantitatively (Section 4.5; Appendices B.3, D).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Latent tracking approaches joint-conditioned tracking | Joint MAE, latent versus TWIST2: M3 **0.2024 vs 0.1777 rad**; T1 **0.1901 vs 0.1844**; N1 **0.1391 vs 0.1362**. Matched training settings; three seeds reported. | Table 1; Appendix A.5 |
| Goal prompting supports pose transitions | Latent goal MAE: **0.2345, 0.1967, 0.1512 rad** for M3, T1, N1 respectively. No joint-conditioned goal baseline is reported. | Table 1; Section 5.1 |
| Target-side reward inference improves aggregate returns | Across the **41-task** suite, mean normalized return is **0.431 vs 0.183**, reported as **2.35×**, for target-side versus source-side latent computation. Tasks score the settled second half of episodes. | Table 2; Appendix B.2 |
| New clips of known behaviors remain trackable | Validation local key-body MPJPE, inferred versus oracle latents: M3 **7.07 vs 6.40 cm**; T1 **5.60 vs 5.26 cm**; N1 **5.60 vs 5.46 cm**. All behavior classes occur in encoder training. | Table 3; Section 5.2 |
| Reduced encoder data preserves much of tracking performance | M3 joint MAE rises from **0.2132 to 0.2241 rad** with **100% versus 25%** distillation data. The tracker remains trained on full data, and sampled segments cover all clips. | Section 5.3; Figure 4 |
| Unseen-robot encoder transfer varies substantially | With fixed pretrained target trackers, signal-gap closure is **89.3% ± 3.5** for M3, **55.5% ± 3.2** for N1, and **12.8% ± 10.3** for T1. This measures improvement over random latents relative to the seen-robot encoder. | Table 4; Section 5.4 |
| Hardware results support limited sim-to-real transfer | M3 latent tracking MAE: **0.2206 real vs 0.2024 simulated rad**, over **five clips**; goal MAE: **0.2675 vs 0.2345 rad**, over **nine poses**; reward return: **0.562 vs 0.508**, over **three tasks**. | Table 5 |
| Unified encoding improves cross-robot agreement | Pairwise latent cosine agreement is **0.938–0.965** for the unified encoder versus **0.867–0.913** for separate encoders, with slightly lower source alignment for the unified model. | Table 16 |
| Training savings apply to distillation and adaptation | Encoder training is reported below **one GPU-hour**; tracker training below **ten hours per embodiment** on one RTX 4090. Source BFM training is excluded. | Tables 9–10 |

The evidence supports a shared, practically usable latent interface for the tested robots and behaviors. It does not establish universal embodiment transfer, unseen behavior acquisition, or controller transfer without target training. Aggregate reward returns also do not establish successful execution of every individual reward prompt. Flow-generated behaviors are demonstrated qualitatively, without reported numerical generation results.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope:** Motion generalization concerns new clips of existing behaviors; embodiment generalization works better when a morphologically similar robot appears in training. Richer behavior data, more embodiments, and text-driven long-horizon tasks remain future work (Sections 5.2, 5.4, 6).
- **Analyst inference—encoder transfer is narrower than full robot transfer:** Unseen-robot experiments retain pretrained target trackers. They test encoder generalization rather than deployment on a robot with no prior control training (Table 4).
- **Analyst inference—reward gains have multiple causes:** The source/target comparison changes the encoder, state buffer, and reward thresholds together. No isolated ablation separates their contributions (Section 5.1; Table 2).
- **Analyst inference—hardware coverage is limited:** Quantitative hardware evaluation covers M3, a small prompt set, and no reported uncertainty. T1 hardware and flow generation are mainly qualitative (Section 5.5; Table 5).
- **Reporting ambiguities:** The headline “around ten hours” should be read alongside the per-embodiment tracker budget. The corruption summary also describes bias tolerance more broadly than the degradation visible in detailed tables (Tables 10, 18–20).
- **Reproducibility gap:** Architecture and training details are extensive, but the supplied text does not confirm released code, checkpoints, or retargeted assets.

## Open research questions
1. **How much morphology diversity enables reliable encoder transfer?** T1 transfers poorly in the current robot set. Train on progressively broader embodiment sets and evaluate held-out morphologies with a consistent tracker protocol.
2. **Which reward adaptation component drives the gains?** Current comparisons combine several changes. Independently ablate target-state caching, percentile threshold transport, and encoder choice across the reward suite.
3. **Can the inherited space support new behaviors and sustained generated control?** Validation retains known behavior classes, and generation evidence is qualitative. Hold out entire behavior classes and measure long-rollout tracking, falls, and mode fidelity.
4. **How robust is correspondence under realistic retargeting failures?** Existing perturbations primarily alter M3 canonical inputs while keeping labels and trackers fixed. Compare independent retargeters and evaluate downstream hardware performance after systematic and clip-dependent alignment errors.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the method description and reported comparisons; **medium** in broader transfer claims.

Material missing information includes per-task reward outcomes, hardware uncertainty and fall statistics, quantitative generator performance, a clearly identified robot setting for the causal-attention table, confirmed artifact releases, and an unambiguous total compute budget across all target robots.
