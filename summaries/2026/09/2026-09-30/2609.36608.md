# Act First, Reason Later: Accelerating On-Policy Distillation for Multi-Turn Agents via Reference-Conditioned Inverse Dynamics

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.36608) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36608)


## One-sentence takeaway
ActFirst-OPD accelerates multi-turn agent distillation by executing reference-guided actions while asynchronously generating reasoning for supervision, reporting average training speedups of 2.3×–4.9× across three benchmarks with the best or tied-best mean success rate in eight of nine benchmark–model settings (Tables 1–2).

## Problem and motivation
Standard on-policy distillation (OPD) waits for a student’s full reasoning before executing each action, delaying environment transitions and collecting new experience. On ALFWorld with Qwen3-1.7B, reasoning accounts for 81–95% of per-turn rollout time; simply generating actions without reasoning produces more unproductive repetition and fewer successful trajectories per environment transition (Figure 1).

The authors address the gap between fast action generation and useful rollout collection: how to remove reasoning from the interaction dependency chain while preserving full-response teacher supervision.

## Approach and contributions
- **Reference-guided fast interaction:** The student predicts an action from its actual interaction context and a reference next observation. Benchmark-specific checks assess the resulting transition; after divergence, autonomous action-only prediction continues for the remaining rollout (§§4.1–4.2; Appendix B.5).
- **Asynchronous full-response distillation:** The same student generates think-then-act responses from collected contexts without reference observations. A frozen teacher supervises these responses; their generated actions are not executed. Evaluation uses ordinary think-then-act interaction without references (§4.3).
- **Bottleneck analysis and validation:** The paper profiles reasoning delays, derives idealized and finite-concurrency speedup bounds, and evaluates training efficiency, rollout quality, and component ablations (§§4.3, 5; Appendix A).

The contribution combines an adapted inverse-dynamics conditioning scheme with asynchronous multi-turn OPD; the paper explicitly attributes the underlying conditioning formulation to prior RIDM work (§2).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Faster student training across all tested settings | For Qwen3-0.6B, 1.7B, and 4B after 250 updates on eight A100-80GB GPUs, average wall-clock speedups over Vanilla OPD are **2.3× ALFWorld, 1.8× WebShop, and 4.9× ScienceWorld**. Offline preparation and evaluation are excluded. | §5.1–5.2; Tables 1–2 |
| Success-rate gains are broad but not universal | Best or tied-best mean SR among compared OPD methods in **eight of nine** settings. Exception: WebShop Qwen3-1.7B achieves **39.33±1.53%**, versus Vanilla OPD’s **40.33±0.58%**, with **1.80×** training speedup. Evaluation uses three seeds on the same trained checkpoint, without references. | Tables 1–2; §5.1 |
| Reference conditioning materially affects performance | Averaged across the three student sizes, removing inverse dynamics lowers SR by **17.31 percentage points on ALFWorld, 1.00 on WebShop, and 3.04 on ScienceWorld**, while retaining asynchronous full-response generation. | Table 3; §5.3 |
| Continuing after divergence helps | Terminating at failed transition checks instead of using autonomous fallback lowers average SR by **1.05, 3.11, and 2.82 percentage points**, respectively, across the three benchmarks and student sizes. | Table 3; §5.3 |
| Concurrency determines whether overlap pays off | Fixed ALFWorld contexts, Qwen3-1.7B, 16 tasks per batch, 30 virtual turns, and 16-token actions: increasing concurrency from **16 to 256** changes rollout speedup from **0.80× to 1.49×** for 128-token responses and **0.92× to 2.42×** for 512-token responses. | §5.4; Figure 3; Table 8 |
| Throughput and fewer transitions both contribute | Separate ALFWorld Qwen3-1.7B training measurements through update 250 show **5.68 versus 3.65 transitions/s** and **38,616 versus 55,525 transitions** for ActFirst-OPD versus Vanilla OPD. These windows exclude initialization and shutdown. | §5.4; Table 9 |
| Idealized speedup has explicit limits | For a fixed \(T\)-turn rollout, speedup is \(T\ell_{\mathrm{full}}/[(T-1)\ell_{\mathrm{fast}}+\ell_{\mathrm{full}}]\), bounded by \(\min(T,\ell_{\mathrm{full}}/\ell_{\mathrm{fast}})\), assuming negligible environment overhead and unrestricted overlap without latency inflation. | Proposition 1; Appendix A.1 |

The evidence supports faster student training with largely preserved or improved benchmark success under the reported setup. It does not establish end-to-end savings including reference preparation, improved inference latency, or elimination of exposure bias. Controlled rollout-quality comparisons support the authors’ proposed mechanism, but do not establish that it generalizes beyond ALFWorld.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated reference dependency:** High-quality offline trajectories are required; settings where they are difficult to obtain are outside scope (§6).
- **Author-stated distribution mismatch:** Full responses are sampled on-policy conditional on collected contexts, but fast actions induce a different context distribution from evaluation-time think-then-act interaction. Exposure bias is not shown to be eliminated or uniformly reduced (Appendix E).
- **Resource sensitivity:** Low concurrency makes the asynchronous scheme slower in the controlled experiment. The theoretical gains depend on assumptions about overlap and overhead (§5.4; Appendix A).
- **Analyst inference—limited generality:** Evaluation covers one student model family and three text benchmarks, including ScienceWorld’s easy preset. Transfer to other architectures, modalities, or deployed environments remains untested (§5.1; Appendix C.1).
- **Analyst inference—uncertainty and cost accounting:** Reported performance deviations measure evaluation randomness for a single trained checkpoint, not variability across independent training runs. Main speedups exclude preparation; Table 5 reports teacher training and candidate collection but not a complete reference-construction cost breakdown (Appendices C.2–C.4).

## Open research questions
1. **How much reference quality and coverage are necessary?** Construction yields selected references for every training task, without a quality or coverage sweep. Next experiment: vary reference scores, lengths, and missing-reference rates while measuring SR and total preparation-plus-training cost.
2. **Can training better match inference-time contexts?** Appendix E acknowledges a remaining context-distribution mismatch. Next experiment: mix fast rollouts with think-then-act rollouts and evaluate recovery from controlled mistakes.
3. **Can reference guidance resume after divergence?** Current fallback permanently disables it, and realignment is proposed as future work (§6). Next experiment: compare permanent fallback with checked reference realignment.
4. **Where does the method remain efficient under tighter resources?** Controlled concurrency measurements show slowdowns at the lowest tested cap. Next experiment: vary GPU allocation and concurrency in full training runs, reporting success per wall-clock hour and preparation-inclusive cost.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** for the reported method and benchmark results, given the supplied main text and detailed appendices.

Material missing information includes independent training-run variability, reference-quality and coverage sensitivity, complete reference-construction costs, broader model and hardware evaluations, and verification of the stated repository and released artifacts.
