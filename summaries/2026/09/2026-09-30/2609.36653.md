# Scheduling Recursive Reasoning in Looped Transformers

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-30.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.36653) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36653)


## One-sentence takeaway
TAPS adapts the magnitude of recurrent latent updates using trajectory persistence and fluctuation, improving reported structured-reasoning accuracy and matched-quality efficiency, with smaller and less consistent gains on language benchmarks.

## Problem and motivation
Looped transformers reuse a learned transformation to refine latent states, usually applying every update at unit scale. The authors argue that this can underuse consistently productive updates or amplify fluctuating ones. Prior work primarily controls recurrence architecture and depth; this paper studies update magnitude as an additional control axis without requiring target labels at inference (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Sensitivity decomposition:** An exact identity splits temporally averaged terminal-loss sensitivity into persistent-progress and centered-fluctuation contributions. These oracle quantities depend on unavailable task gradients (§3, Propositions 1–2).
- **Online scheduler:** TAPS estimates persistence and fluctuation from observed latent updates, then amplifies or damps the learned update through a clipped scalar multiplier. Six variants use adjacent updates, temporal moments, or directional curvature (§4.1; Appendix D).
- **Conditional guarantees:** The authors derive sufficient conditions for beneficial scale adjustments, lower expected terminal loss, and fewer loops at a target quality; these require proxy alignment, bounded temporal mismatch, and smoothness (§4.2).
- **Training and execution extensions:** A fluctuation penalty shapes recurrent dynamics during training; experiments combine scheduling with adaptive exit, hierarchical recurrence, parallel rollouts, and fused controller execution (§5; Appendices C.2–C.7).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Frozen-model scheduling improves grid accuracy | TRM, Sudoku-Extreme, horizon 32: BB reaches 90.19% exact accuracy versus 89.67% unit-step, with 1.039× wall-clock speedup to baseline terminal accuracy. Maze-Hard, horizon 16: Adam reaches 79.10% versus 78.80%, with 1.117× speedup. | Table 1; Appendix C.1 |
| Co-design strengthens gains | Sudoku: Momentum reaches 91.39% exact accuracy, with 1.300× speedup to the pretrained unit-step target of 89.67%. Maze: P/S-Sign reaches 79.90%, with 1.561× speedup to the 78.80% target. | Table 1; §5.2 |
| Adaptive exit reduces recurrent computation | On 1,000 held-out Sudoku examples, Adam achieves 91.20% exact accuracy at 316.5 mean effective updates; FPRM achieves 91.10% at 1,497.0. This is a compute comparison, not measured wall-clock speedup. | Table 7; Appendix C.3 |
| Fixed-point matching benefits depend on target quality | TAPS-Adam matches the 87.40% accuracy target reached by fixed-point inference after 1,000 updates using 256.2 updates; matching its 65.20% target at 50 updates requires 144.0 TAPS updates. | Table 9 |
| Language-model average gains vary by model | Four-benchmark mean accuracy: Ouro-1.4B BB, 62.86% versus 61.10%; Huginn BB, 39.15% versus 38.87%; Ouro-2.6B P/S-Sign, 71.70% versus 71.35%. Individual task scores sometimes decline. | Tables 3, 12; Table 5 protocols |
| Intermediate-layer improvements are selective | Frozen Qwen3-4B-Instruct, three Euler stages over layers 15–18: BB averages 59.37% versus unit-step Loop’s 59.22%; Adam averages 58.71%. Mean is over CommonsenseQA, GPQA-Main, and MMLU-Pro. | Table 4; Appendix C.1 |
| Parallel-rollout gains are not universal | At sampling budget 10, Adam reaches 98.6% exact accuracy versus 97.8%; at budget 50, BB reaches 98.5% versus 98.9%. | Table 10; Appendix C.5 |

The evidence supports useful update-scale control across several recurrence settings, but does not establish uniform improvement across controllers, tasks, noise conditions, or compute budgets. The theoretical results are conditional guarantees rather than proofs that the evaluated models satisfy their assumptions.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Broader guarantees, richer observable signals, and deeper integration into training remain open (§7). Strong high-level-state perturbations reduce benefits and sometimes make TAPS worse than unit-step inference (Appendix C.5).
- **Analyst inference:** Small language-model gains are difficult to assess without confidence intervals or repeated-run uncertainty estimates; the supplied tables provide neither. The paired Sudoku co-design experiment uses one training seed (Table 6).
- **Analyst inference:** Co-design changes both training-time scheduling and the penalty. Their separate contributions are not isolated in the supplied paired comparison (Appendix C.2).
- **Analyst inference:** Intermediate-layer hyperparameters were selected on CommonsenseQA and GPQA-Main, but a separate tuning split is not specified, limiting interpretation of those benchmark gains (Appendix C.1).
- **Reporting inconsistencies:** §5.3 describes consistently improved parallel performance, while Table 10 contains regressions. Its fixed-point reference points to Table 10, although those results appear in Table 9. The headline “up to 1.56×” is also narrower than Table 8’s reported 1.647× adaptive outer-loop Maze speedup.

## Open research questions
1. **When do trajectory energies predict task-loss sensitivity?** The guarantees assume this alignment; direct validation is supplied only through a toy example. Next experiment: measure oracle sensitivities on labeled held-out trajectories and test controller-sign agreement across depths (§4.2; Appendix B.3).
2. **Which co-design component causes the gains?** Scheduling and regularization change together. Next experiment: compare task-only training, scheduling alone, penalty alone, and their combination across training seeds (Appendix C.2).
3. **Do language gains survive independent tuning and uncertainty analysis?** Several improvements are small or task-dependent. Next experiment: tune on separate development data and report paired uncertainty estimates on untouched benchmark examples (Tables 3–4, 12; Appendix C.1).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text includes detailed algorithms, proofs, protocols, and numerical tables.
