# On the Diffusibility of High-Dimensional Latents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.28473) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28473)


## One-sentence takeaway
Predicting clean latents instead of velocity improves text-to-image generation for two strong-reconstruction autoencoders, consistent with—but not proving—the authors’ explanation that reconstruction concentrates signal into a smaller effective subspace (Tables 1–3).

## Problem and motivation
Pretrained semantic encoders can lose fine image details, limiting reconstruction. Reconstruction tuning restores information but makes standard velocity-based flow matching harder to optimize in the resulting high-dimensional latents. Compressing those latents can help generation while sacrificing reconstruction fidelity; the authors investigate how to generate directly in the full representation space (§§1, 3.2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Representation diagnosis:** Measure effective dimensionality through the variance explained by singular components of normalized, centered patch features. Reconstruction-trained encoders concentrate variance into fewer components, including across additional datasets (Table 1; Figure 3; Appendix A.2).
- **Mechanistic analysis:** Under an exact linear-subspace model with isotropic Gaussian noise, the optimal velocity predictor includes an orthogonal noise component, whereas the optimal clean-latent predictor stays within the signal subspace (§3.2; Appendix A.5).
- **Practical adaptation and evaluation:** Apply established \(x_0\)-prediction to reconstruction-rich representation spaces, retaining the velocity-equivalent loss \(\mathbb{E}\| (h_0-x_\theta)/t\|^2\), with \(t\) clamped to at least 0.05. Evaluate reconstruction-tuned DINOv2-L and frozen MAE-RAE (§§3.1–4.3).

The novelty is the diagnosis and application to these latent spaces, rather than inventing clean-data prediction; the paper explicitly builds on JiT (§2.3).

## Evidence and main results
Main generation comparisons use a 1B DiT trained for 90k steps on 34M image–text pairs, at 256×256 resolution, with a 100-step Euler sampler (§§4.1–4.2).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Reconstruction tuning concentrates feature variance | On ImageNet validation, DINOv2-L retains 1024 ambient dimensions, but \(R_{90}\) falls from 672 to 129 while reconstruction PSNR rises from 17.34 to 29.12. | Table 1 |
| Clean-latent prediction improves tuned DINOv2-L | Switching \(v\rightarrow x_0\): GenEval 30.96→39.48; DPG-Bench 67.47→71.83; COCO-30k FID 29.97→16.80. | Table 2 |
| Tuned DINOv2-L with \(x_0\) exceeds frozen DINOv2-L with \(v\) | Frozen baseline: GenEval 37.63, DPG-Bench 70.21, FID 18.34; tuned \(x_0\): 39.48, 71.83, 16.80. | Table 2 |
| Benefit also appears with MAE-RAE | At 768 latent dimensions, \(v\rightarrow x_0\): GenEval 36.17→40.89; DPG-Bench 67.54→73.90; FID 22.20→17.24. | Table 3 |
| Compression presents a tradeoff | The 32-dimensional tuned DINOv2-L with \(v\) achieves better GenEval/DPG scores than 1024-dimensional \(x_0\): 40.99/73.04 versus 39.48/71.83; worse FID: 17.64 versus 16.80; lower reconstruction PSNR: 25.83 versus 29.12. | Table 2 |
| Larger-data results support feasibility | With internal 90M pretraining and BLIP3o-60k SFT, the 1B model scores 78.62/80.13 on GenEval/DPG at 256 resolution and 79.69/81.15 at 512. | Table 4 |
| Reconstruction tuning reduces linear-probe performance | ImageNet-1k accuracy drops from 84.5% for original DINOv2-L to 63.9% after tuning. | Appendix A.3 |

These results support the practical benefit of \(x_0\)-prediction under the tested training budget. They do not establish effective dimensionality as the sole cause, universal superiority over compression, or a measured wall-clock speedup. The larger-data comparison with Scale-RAE differs in training data, model size, and resolution (Table 4).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-acknowledged tradeoff:** Preserving understanding while improving reconstruction remains unresolved; semantic-preservation training does not prevent the linear-probe decline (Appendix A.3).
- **Analyst inference—geometry versus causality:** Variance concentration is not an exact intrinsic-dimensionality measurement. The derivation assumes a linear subspace; it explains optimal prediction structure, rather than proving finite-network optimization speed (§3.2; Appendix A.5).
- **Analyst inference—missing controls:** Generation tests cover two strong-reconstruction tokenizers. Table 2 lacks frozen DINOv2-L with \(x_0\), so the frozen-versus-tuned comparison changes both tokenizer and prediction target.
- **Analyst inference—uncertainty and reproducibility:** No repeated-run variability or confidence intervals are reported. The scaling dataset is internal, and the supplied text does not establish released code or weights (§4.3).

## Open research questions
1. **Does variance concentration causally drive the prediction-target advantage?** The evidence combines correlation with an idealized model. Next experiment: vary latent spectra under controlled reconstruction quality and compare both targets.
2. **Can reconstruction improve without sacrificing understanding?** Linear-probe accuracy declines despite semantic preservation. Next experiment: sweep preservation-loss strength and jointly measure probing, PSNR, and generation.
3. **Where is the best compression–quality tradeoff?** The tested compressed representation improves alignment but worsens FID and reconstruction. Next experiment: sweep bottleneck dimensions with both prediction targets.
4. **Does \(x_0\)-prediction reduce compute to a fixed quality threshold?** Fixed-budget results and qualitative overfitting examples do not quantify that gain. Next experiment: report quality-versus-step and wall-clock curves across seeds.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported benchmark comparisons; **medium** in the proposed causal explanation.

Material missing information includes repeated-run uncertainty, quantitative convergence timings, inference guidance settings, exact selection of the public training subset, composition of the internal dataset, full tokenizer stage schedules, and confirmed code/checkpoint availability.
