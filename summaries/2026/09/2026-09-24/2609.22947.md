# RewardVerse: Rubric-Guided Policy Optimization for Video Reward Modeling

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.22947) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22947)


## One-sentence takeaway
RewardVerse combines query-specific evaluation rubrics, continuous score readout, and preference-margin training to improve video reward alignment on EvalVerse and VGRB, with limited downstream evidence from one video generator.

## Problem and motivation
Video reward models must provide useful scores for optimizing generated videos. The authors identify **scalar drift**: scores collapse into a narrow range or change with instruction wording and input order. They argue that directly assigning a scalar without explicit evaluation criteria contributes to these problems (§§1, 3).

Their proposed gap is a learned, explicit criterion-setting stage between the evaluation query and video scoring; static rubric prompting alone still leaves systematic score bias (§3.2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Dynamic rubric intermediate representation.** A shared Qwen2.5-VL-7B backbone generates themes, weights, and evaluation tips from the target dimension and generation prompt, without seeing the candidate video. Its scorer evaluates each video independently, reads expected ratings from the logits of tokens 1–5, and aggregates theme scores by weight (§4.1).
- **Two-stage Rubric-Guided Policy Optimization, or RGPO.** Stage 1 warms up scoring with teacher-generated, iteratively verified seed rubrics. Stage 2 trains rubric generation through GRPO while calibrating the scorer to human score differences. Generator and scorer share parameters but receive different optimization signals (§§4.2–4.3).
- **Low-data empirical evaluation.** Training uses 480 preference pairs across 16 dimensions, with human score margins and Gemini-3.1-Pro seed-rubric generation. Experiments cover pointwise evaluation, external pairwise transfer, ablations, and downstream generation (§5; Appendix A.2).

The authors claim the first rubric-as-reward approach for video reward modeling; priority cannot be independently established from the supplied text.

These results support improved measured alignment and preference ranking in the tested settings. They do not establish elimination of scalar drift: residual bias remains, and paraphrase robustness improves relative to the expanded score range rather than in absolute variation. The downstream results are consistent with the authors’ reduced-reward-hacking interpretation, but do not directly demonstrate general resistance to reward exploitation.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Performance is bounded by the backbone’s visual and temporal perception. Individual theme scores lack direct supervision, limiting the reliability of rubric-level explanations. Scaling beyond the tested backbone, training budget, and downstream generator remains unverified (Appendix A.6).
- **Analyst inference — comparison scope:** External baselines use official checkpoints without additional training, and coarse outputs are mapped onto fine-grained dimensions; some reuse one score across dimensions. The same-budget rubric-free ablation is therefore especially useful, but does not isolate every architectural and training choice (Table 12; Table 3).
- **Analyst inference — data efficiency:** The small preference-pair count also requires numerical human margins and a frontier teacher. It does not alone establish low total annotation or compute cost (§4.2; Appendix A.4).
- **Reporting ambiguities:** Seed verification is described as recovering preference order in §4.2, but Appendix A.4.1 also describes revision against absolute human-score error. The validation set used for checkpoint selection lacks size and separation details.
- **Statistical limits:** Main benchmark and downstream comparisons lack repeated-run variability or confidence intervals. The text also does not specify VGRB tie thresholds or provide separate ablations for learned weights and theme-alignment regularization.

## Open research questions
1. **Are theme scores faithful explanations?** Aggregate-margin supervision leaves theme correctness unresolved. Next experiment: collect theme-level human ratings and compare aggregate-only versus theme-supervised calibration (Appendix A.6).
2. **Does robustness hold under sustained optimization?** Downstream evidence covers one generator and one target dimension. Next experiment: run longer, repeated RL trials across generators with independent human evaluation and reward-exploitation probes (Appendix A.3).
3. **Which rubric components drive gains?** The paper ablates tips and training stages but not adaptive weights or alignment regularization separately. Next experiment: freeze or remove each component under matched training budgets (Table 3).
4. **How dependent is low-data performance on the teacher?** Seed construction uses Gemini-3.1-Pro without a teacher comparison. Next experiment: compare expert-written, weaker-teacher, and current seed rubrics using identical preference pairs (§4.2).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the method and reported benchmark improvements, but broader stability and reward-hacking conclusions have narrower evidence.

Material missing information includes validation-set construction, VGRB tie handling, main-result uncertainty, teacher-generation cost, full downstream training duration and evaluation counts, and independent verification of the advertised code and model releases.
