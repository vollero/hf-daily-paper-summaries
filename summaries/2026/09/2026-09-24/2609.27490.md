# WhatWorkedBench: Benchmarking Experimental Understanding in AI Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.27490) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27490)


## One-sentence takeaway
WhatWorkedBench evaluates agents’ predictions of component effects after budgeted experiments, showing that finding an optimal configuration can coexist with poor effect reconstruction and that numerical inference and enforced code equivalences can improve recovery (§6).

## Problem and motivation
Research agents need to predict how experimental changes behave across different configurations, beyond finding one successful setting. The authors identify a gap in evaluating this acquired knowledge: experiment plans, execution success, and best-achieved scores do not establish accurate understanding of conditional component effects (§§1–2).

Their proposed target is operational: the accuracy of a complete predicted response surface under fixed data, implementation, and evaluation conditions—not a broader claim about scientific understanding (§3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Executable evaluation protocol:** Agents receive two anchor scores, purchase additional configuration measurements, and predict every configuration’s utility. Exhaustive references score conditional effects, pair interactions, configuration regret, and artifact delivery (§3).
- **Benchmark assets:** The catalog contains 36 task conditions on 30 sources across eight workflow families, with 1,248 configuration records, 3,392 conditional effects, and 342 mean pair interactions. The authors provide standalone execution and grading artifacts (§§4, 7).
- **Controlled comparisons:** Shared-estimator evaluations hold observations fixed while changing reconstruction methods; code-equivalence controls exploit configurations with identical behavior. These comparisons help distinguish measurement selection, numerical inference, and use of program structure (§§5–6).

## Evidence and main results
Recovery is \(R=\max(0,1-E/A)\), where \(E\) is conditional-effect MAE and \(A\) is mean absolute true effect, with a separate flat-table rule. Results use family-macro averaging unless stated otherwise. Budget \(B\) excludes the two free anchors (§§3, 5).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Conditional effects frequently reverse sign | At least one reversal occurs in 35/36 task conditions at the numerical floor; 24/36 retain one when both directions exceed 5% of the task’s utility range. | Figure 3; Appendix L.4 |
| Optimization success does not imply accurate reconstruction | On 22 four-factor sources at \(B=8\), pair ridge achieves recovery 0.612 and 15 exact choices, but only three strict reconstructions. Strict success bounds every effect error by 10% of utility range. | §6.1; Appendix L.2 |
| Average and worst-case accuracy rank methods differently | On the same 22 sources at \(B=8\), effect GP reaches recovery 0.701 and 16 exact choices, but only one strict reconstruction. | §6.1 |
| Shared inference improves Flash’s aggregate recovery | On six matched four-factor sources at \(B=8\), same-observation GP changes recovery from 0.632 to 0.698 across 12 original episodes and from 0.621 to 0.720 across six additional episodes. Four sources improve and two decline in each cohort. | Table 3; Appendix L.1 |
| Enforcing code equivalences improves numerical controls | Across six paired six-factor workflows at \(B=20\), equivalence-aware pair ridge improves recovery from 0.157 to 0.389; equivalence-aware effect GP improves from 0.248 to 0.462. | §6.3 |
| Reporting rules does not ensure compliant predictions | In eight diagnostic episodes on four sources at \(B=20\), agents report all registered rules, but every submitted table violates equivalences. Post-hoc projection raises mean recovery from 0.338 to 0.507 and reduces raw effect error in every run. | Appendix K.3 |
| The shared-GP benefit extends to completed added-family artifacts | For six completed Flash submissions—three beat-detection and three link-prediction sources—at \(B=32\), recovery rises from 0.303 to 0.455, with gains on every source. | §6.4; Appendix L.6 |

These results establish prediction improvements within the frozen benchmark and separate configuration choice from effect accuracy. They do not establish general scientific understanding or universal GP superiority. Shared fitting isolates reconstruction on fixed observations; code-equivalence controls change both acquisition and prediction.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Documented scope:** Tasks use deterministic, small binary configuration spaces. Measurement credits constrain information rather than real experimental runtime (§§3–4).
- **Documented execution effects:** Missing submissions receive zero recovery, so delivered scores combine prediction quality with operational reliability. Rate limits, deadlines, empty response streams, and dated model aliases complicate model comparisons (§5; Appendix J).
- **Documented study chronology:** Original-cohort shared GP fitting was retrospective; subsequent studies followed inspection of earlier results. The additional cohort supports replication but does not erase development-stage dependence (Appendix J).
- **Analyst inference—limited generalization:** Core agents cover selected sources and two API identifiers; added-family agent results condition on completed artifacts. Broader model, source, and delivery-rate conclusions remain unsupported (§5; Appendix L.6).
- **Analyst inference—attribution limits:** Full information jointly adds code, semantics, source details, and summaries, so that comparison cannot isolate code’s contribution. The structure diagnostic also elicits rule reporting and supplies ridge helpers that violate equivalences (Appendices K.1, K.3).

## Open research questions
- **Can agents enforce recognized program rules during execution?** Diagnostic agents report correct rules but submit inconsistent tables. Compare ordinary helpers with equivalence-constrained helpers under matched budgets and observation sets (Appendix K.3).
- **Does stronger inference improve subsequent experiment selection?** Shared GP results mainly evaluate reconstruction after observations are acquired. Give agents an online GP helper and compare both acquired evidence and final recovery against ridge-only access (§6.2).
- **Do the findings extend to noisy or larger intervention spaces?** Current references freeze randomness and enumerate small binary spaces. Test repeated noisy measurements and larger spaces, using exhaustive tractable subsets for validation (§3).
- **Which workflow information helps?** Full versus opaque views show no consistent aggregate advantage, but bundle several information types. Run separate code-only, semantics-only, and source-summary ablations (Appendix K.1).
