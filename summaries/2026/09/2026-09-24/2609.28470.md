# StudentBench: AI and human tutoring yield equivalent GRE learning gains

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.28470) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28470)


## One-sentence takeaway
StudentBench finds pooled AI tutoring statistically equivalent to expert human tutoring for immediate gains on combined GRE-style assessments after one hour, but does not establish equivalence in Verbal alone or lasting learning benefits (§3; Appendix B.2).

## Problem and motivation
Most AI benchmarks measure model performance rather than improvements in human capabilities. The authors address whether autonomous, general-purpose LLM tutors can improve students’ unaided performance comparably to expert human tutors, while making individualized instruction less expensive (§§1, 8.2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Learning-outcome benchmark:** Randomized AI, live human, and no-tutoring conditions, with counterbalanced, newly authored GRE-style pre/post assessments. The analyzed sample contains 2,469 sessions from 2,383 adults; each section evaluates 13 AI tutor configurations (§2; Appendix A).
- **Tutoring platform:** Shared planning and conversational prompts let models select concepts, generate practice, and tutor from students’ pre-test records. The application supplies context, manages timing, checks answers, and prompts responses to mistakes; “minimal scaffolding” therefore does not mean no scaffolding (Appendix A.5).
- **Complementary evaluations:** Expert preferences for plans and practice problems, deterministic conversation indicators, and cost/engagement comparisons supplement learning outcomes (§§4–7).
- **Reusable assets:** The authors report releasing the platform, study data, and analysis code; recorded activity includes 176,614 student–AI messages and 45,463 answered practice problems (§1; Appendix D; Reproducibility).

## Evidence and main results

Here, **pp** means percentage points of assessment accuracy.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Pooled AI–human equivalence | Across 2,139 AI and 140 human sessions, adjusted combined difference was −0.58 pp, 90% CI [−2.18, 1.03]; TOST p=.015 within ±4.09 pp bounds. Also passed ±0.20-SD bounds, p=.023. | Fig. 1; Appendix B.2 |
| Section-specific qualification | Quantitative: +0.88 pp, 90% CI [−1.80, 3.56], equivalence p=.028. Verbal: −2.19 pp, [−4.31, −0.07], equivalence p=.085; equivalence was not established. | Appendix B.2 |
| Tutoring improves on control | Combined adjusted AI–control gain: +6.15 pp, 95% CI [4.08, 8.21]; human–control: +7.04 pp, [3.88, 10.20]; both p<.001. Control included graded pre-test review and unrelated videos. | Table B.2 |
| Learning rankings are largely descriptive | Highest AI mean exceeded the human mean in five of seven domains, selecting different tutors by domain. The combined omnibus comparison among 12 shared AI tutors did not detect differences, p=.755. | Appendices B.1, C.2 |
| Low inference cost with exploratory individual equivalence | Gemma 4 31B versus humans: adjusted difference −1.09 pp, 90% CI [−3.93, 1.75], p=.044. Cost per **observed** gain point: $0.00524 versus $4.80508, approximately 918× lower, using AI inference costs and a $75 human session reference. Individual equivalence tests were uncorrected across tutors. | Appendix B.7; Fig. 5 |
| Expert preferences distinguish teaching materials | 51 experts completed 2,028 paired reviews based on 381 pre-tests; authors report strong Opus performance. Accounting for repeated reviewers removes some significant pairwise differences. | §4.1; Appendix E; Table E.2 |
| Quantitative process associations | In 1,137 sessions, 10 seconds longer replies associated with 5.19 fewer messages; 10 additional messages with 0.52 more correct practice problems; 10 additional correct problems with +5.62 pp gain. Holm-corrected p values were <.001, .00197, and <.001, respectively. Corresponding Verbal tests were not significant after correction. | Table D.2; Fig. 6 |

The evidence supports equivalence **within the specified margin, population, assessments, and statistical model**, rather than identical effectiveness. It does not establish superiority of selected domain winners, a causal latency-to-learning mechanism, or recursive human self-improvement beyond its proposed initial teaching condition.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Authors’ stated scope limits:** No delayed retention, independent GRE-practice control, or deployment across languages and educational settings was tested (§9).
- **Statistical sensitivity:** The combined equivalence test has only 1.59 effective degrees of freedom because human sessions form a few connected clusters. Excluding repeat participants and omitting individual tutors preserves combined equivalence, but a gain-only sensitivity analysis fails to establish section-level equivalence (Appendices B.2–B.4).
- **Analyst inference—selection:** Completion, effort, and AI-participation filters make this an analysis of qualifying participants rather than all assigned students; generalization to disengaged users is uncertain (Appendix A.2).
- **Proxy validity:** Conversation rules favor longer or more verbose sessions and were not validated as learning predictors. Spoken human and typed AI interactions are not directly comparable; “correct practice” uses AI-authored keys, and student disputes are not verified errors (§4.2; Appendices D.2, F).
- **Exploratory comparisons:** Individual tutor equivalence lacks multiplicity correction; dialogue metrics were selected after exploratory comparisons, although their tests receive Holm correction (Appendices B.7, D.2).
- **Analyst inference—cost interpretation:** The headline multiplier compares inference spending with a reference human rate, excludes unpriced API calls, and divides by total observed gain rather than incremental gain over control; it is not a complete deployment-cost estimate (Appendices B.7, D.1).
- **Prompt evidence:** Prompt selection used a separate pilot; later prompt comparisons were nonconcurrent and inconclusive. Claims that additional scaffolding would improve outcomes remain unestablished (Appendix G).

## Open research questions
- **Do gains persist and transfer?** Immediate matched assessments cannot establish retention or broader competence (§9). Run delayed, unaided assessments with new topics and item formats.
- **What does conversation add beyond practice?** No independent-practice control isolates this contribution (§9). Randomize students to matched practice with or without interactive tutoring.
- **Does lower latency cause better learning?** Current process results are observational and temporal checks are less consistent (Appendix D.2). Randomize response delays within the same tutor while holding content constant.
- **Does Verbal equivalence hold under a stronger comparison?** Its equivalence test failed and modalities differ (Appendices B.2, F). Recruit more independent human tutors and compare human and AI tutoring in matched communication formats.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text provides extensive methods and sensitivity checks, but the main conclusion remains bounded by statistical sensitivity and immediate outcomes.

Material information unavailable includes delayed retention, actual examination outcomes, independently audited practice-answer accuracy, full deployment costs, and outcomes for all randomized participants regardless of exclusions. Public data/code availability is asserted by the authors but was not independently checked.
