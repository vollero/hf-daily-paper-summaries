# HappyWorld-Bench

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-24.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.24308) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24308)


## One-sentence takeaway
HappyWorld-Bench evaluates video, spatial, and embodied world models under a shared capability framework, finding that strong visual quality can coexist with unreliable interaction, state persistence, and editing (§§3–5).

## Problem and motivation
World models must preserve coherent state and respond correctly to actions to support dependable exploration and planning. The authors identify fragmented evaluation across video generation, explicit spatial environments, and embodied prediction: existing benchmarks cover different capabilities and interfaces, making their findings difficult to organize under a common framework (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Capability taxonomy:** W1–W6 describes perceptual construction, interaction, persistence, intervention, scalability, and universal world modeling. Each track operationalizes only a subset; the taxonomy is broader than the demonstrated evaluation (Table 1; §§3–5).
- **Benchmark and protocols:** 1,138 video cases, 300 spatial scenes, and 254 embodied task instances combine controlled rollouts, geometry-based operations, and paired interventions. Embodied W4 instances represent matched groups rather than individual videos (§§3.1, 4.1, 5.1; Table 17).
- **Complementary evaluation:** HappyWorld-Arena collects human comparisons alongside automated visual, geometric, simulation-based, and VLM assertion scores. The empirical study covers 14 interactive video models, nine spatial systems, and eight embodied candidates (§1).

The contribution is an evaluation framework and assets, rather than a new world-model architecture. Public availability of executable assets is not established by the supplied text.

These results support separating appearance, behavioral correctness, and preservation. They do not establish downstream robot success or a causal effect of rollout duration. Elo is track-specific; spatial Elo additionally incorporates deterministic outcomes for unsupported capabilities, so it is not solely a human-preference measure (Appendices B.7–B.8).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Spatial evaluation uses static scenes; embodied evaluation tests generated videos rather than downstream control. Video W4 compares only two systems, and W6 is not operationally demonstrated (§§3.3, 4.1, 5.1).
- **Author-stated metric limitations:** Spatial edit success can accept an already-satisfied no-op. HPS-based consistency can reward uniformly poor views; endpoint tracking can hide intermediate drift; expansion “preservation” measures preference changes rather than unchanged geometry or identity (Appendices B.4–B.6).
- **Author-stated comparability limits:** Video W2 and W3 use different cases. Spatial aggregates can involve different valid populations and omit unavailable metrics. Hosted Genie evaluation leaves checkpoint and sampling controls unavailable (§3.3; Appendices A.1, B.7–B.8).
- **Analyst inference:** Spatial W5 growth and view-quality tests do not demonstrate the taxonomy’s multi-agent synchronization or conflict handling (Table 1; Appendix B.6).
- **Analyst inference:** The qualitative claim that physical-rule responses are often absent is not quantified separately from action edits in the pooled embodied W4 table. Its prevalence therefore remains unclear (§5.3; Table 17).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Table 13 calls W4 entries “cases” while describing pairs; Table 17 explicitly identifies matched groups. The reported rollout-record total is not reconciled with model coverage in the supplied text.

## Open research questions

1. **How much persistence loss comes from duration itself?** Different case pools prevent isolation. Repeat identical tasks at increasing horizons with controlled revisit intervals (§3.3).
2. **Do physical-rule edits fail more often than action edits?** W4 results pool both types. Report separate paired-effect scores and manually audit failures against prescribed outcomes (Table 17).
3. **How robust are rankings to evaluator errors and aggregation choices?** Several metrics use learned judges, proxies, and unequal valid populations. Re-score a human-annotated subset, report uncertainty, and vary aggregation weights (Appendices B.4–B.8; C.6).
4. **Do benchmark scores predict useful control?** Downstream task success is outside the current evaluation. Compare model rankings with performance in a common closed-loop planning or robot-task evaluation (§2.2; Appendix B.7).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** Main results and many metric definitions are explicit, but the source was truncated, preventing complete coverage. Material unavailable information includes omitted protocol details, verified asset access, complete evaluator configurations and assertion sets, ranking uncertainty, separate embodied intervention-type results, and reconciliation of task-group and rollout counts.
