# Online Learning with LLM Experts from Limited Feedback

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.05820) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05820)


## One-sentence takeaway

Selecting informative past responses for evaluation improves budget-limited bandit routing in the reported Nectar experiments, while the full-information gains are mixed and deployment effectiveness remains untested (§5, Table 1).

## Problem and motivation

The task is to route each arriving prompt to the LLM with the highest expected response quality while paying for only a limited number of evaluations. The authors distinguish this **feedback budget** from inference cost: human or LLM judgments are expensive even when responses are already available (§§1–2).

Their identified gap is contextual online routing where the learner controls both **when to request feedback and which past response to evaluate**, subject to a hard budget (§1, Appendix C).

## Approach and contributions

- **LimFullFeed:** Route using linear reward estimates over frozen prompt embeddings. At regular feedback intervals, select an unevaluated past prompt that maximizes the covariance determinant, then observe all experts’ rewards and update their estimates (§3, Algorithm 1).
- **LimBanFeed:** Maintain separate reward estimates and confidence bounds per expert. Select experts optimistically; after enough uses of an expert, evaluate its most informative previously served prompt (§4, Algorithm 2).
- **Theory and cost extension:** Provide regret upper bounds, lower-bound constructions, and an extension that spaces evaluations according to expert-specific evaluation costs (§§3.2, 4.2, Appendix A).

The claimed technical novelty is retrospective information-based feedback selection and its regret analysis under sparse updates; linear reward estimation itself is an assumed modeling component (§1, “Technical Novelty”).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Full-information regret guarantee | Authors state \(O(dT m^{-1/2}\log(TL))\), with bounded parameters, contexts and reward gaps; each feedback query reveals all experts’ rewards. | Theorem 3.2 |
| Bandit regret guarantee | Authors state \(O(dT K^{1/2}m^{-1/2}\log(TL))\), under the corresponding assumptions and prescribed confidence width; each query reveals one previously selected expert’s reward. | Theorem 4.1 |
| Bandit advantage on Nectar | At \(K=6,d=40,T=60{,}000,m=5{,}000\), cumulative regret is **2295.7 ± 42.3** for LimBanFeed versus **3020.7 ± 17.2** for NoLookBack. LimBanFeed has lower reported means at every listed budget. | §5, Table 1 |
| Full-information advantage is inconsistent | Same Nectar setting: at \(m=1{,}000\), LimFullFeed records **672.9 ± 37.4** versus **631.6 ± 22.1**; at \(m=20{,}000\), **73.6 ± 6.0** versus **75.8 ± 6.1**. Lower regret is better. | Table 1 |
| Runtime increases with horizon | Nectar runtime sweep: from \(T=500\) to \(T=15{,}000\), LimBanFeed rises from **0.41 ± 0.00 s** to **14.35 ± 0.03 s**, and LimFullFeed from **0.15 ± 0.00 s** to **4.47 ± 0.03 s**. Other sweep settings are not fully specified. | Table 2 |

Results are averaged over five runs; the meaning of “±” is unspecified (§5). Table 1 supports a bandit benefit over NoLookBack, but does not support uniformly better full-information performance. AllFeedback uses more feedback, so it is not an equal-budget comparison. These simulations do not establish improved live user outcomes.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Retrospective evaluation may require storing outputs and obtaining noisy judgments. Static benchmarks may miss changing user behavior and deployment constraints (Limitations).
- **Analyst inference—evaluation construction:** Appendix D fits expert parameters using benchmark labels, but does not fully explain subsequent reward generation or stream construction. Direct evaluation on original response judgments versus a fitted linear simulation is therefore unclear.
- **Analyst inference—metric ambiguity:** Equation 2 compares against the best expert **for each prompt**, whereas §5 says “best expert in hindsight.” The experimental comparator needs clarification. Reward-gap regret also need not equal a count of routing mistakes, despite §5’s interpretation.
- **Analyst inference—scope of guarantees:** Fixed linear reward parameters and independent noise constrain applicability (§2). The stated bounds do not guarantee sublinear regret in \(T\) for a fixed feedback budget.
- **Evidence limits:** Only NoLookBack and AllFeedback are evaluated. Several equations are degraded in the retrieved text, limiting assessment of lower bounds and proof details; exact near-optimality is not independently established here.

## Open research questions

1. **Does the advantage survive realistic reward mismatch and judge noise?** The linear model and unclear simulation pipeline leave this open. Next experiment: route using original benchmark judgments and introduce controlled judgment errors.
2. **How robust is retrospective selection under changing conditions?** The authors identify dynamic experts and changing prompt distributions as future work (§6). Next experiment: introduce domain shifts and expert arrivals or removals during the stream.
3. **Which feedback-selection component drives the gain?** Existing comparisons do not isolate historical access from determinant-based selection (§5). Next experiment: compare current-prompt, random-history and determinant-selected feedback at identical budgets.
4. **Can the bandit lower bound be tightened?** The authors explicitly acknowledge an upper–lower bound gap (§4.2). Next study: construct harder instances and test empirical regret scaling across feature dimensions and expert counts.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The algorithms and Table 1 comparisons are clear; theoretical tightness and experimental realism are less certain.

Material missing information includes executable code, complete Nectar preprocessing, stream and reward-generation procedures, experimental comparator details, hyperparameter settings, uncertainty definitions, runtime hardware and fixed sweep settings, and readable numerical curves for Figures 1–2.
