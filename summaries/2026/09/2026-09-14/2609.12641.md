# Breaking the Vision-Action Shortcut: Latent Interface Training for Generalizable Robotics Foundation Models

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.12641) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12641)


## One-sentence takeaway
LIT combines image-free action pretraining with a pose-supervised visual interface, improving LIBERO-Plus success by 3.87–10.70 percentage points across four architectures, with evidence consistent with reduced reliance on irrelevant visual cues (Table II; §IV-E).

## Problem and motivation
Robot policies can exploit visual cues that correlate with demonstrated actions but become unreliable when viewpoints, lighting, or backgrounds change. The challenge is to suppress these dependencies while retaining spatial information needed for control.

The authors identify two gaps: richer representations do not constrain how action experts use visual information, and image-free pretraining alone leaves later visual conditioning unrestricted (§I).

## Approach and contributions
- **Spatial-goal-conditioned action pretraining:** Train an action expert without images using language, robot state, and each demonstrated action chunk’s terminal end-effector pose plus gripper positions. The backbone remains frozen (§III-A–B).
- **Exclusive, pose-supervised visual interface:** During subsequent joint training, latent tokens aggregate semantic and visual backbone features and provide the action expert’s only visual conditioning pathway. An auxiliary decoder reconstructs the same terminal goal used during pretraining (§III-C).
- **Integration and empirical validation:** Apply this combined training design to π0.5, MolmoAct2, FAST-WAM, and ImageWAM, retaining their native action-generation mechanisms. At inference, the interface remains active; neither demonstrated terminal goals nor pose encoder/decoder modules are required (§III-D; §IV).

The proposed contribution is the combination of shared spatial supervision, staged action learning, and restricted visual access—not any one component alone.

## Evidence and main results

All numerical results below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Average in-distribution success improves across architectures | LIBERO baseline → LIT: π0.5 **87.75% → 91.80%**; MolmoAct2 **93.50% → 94.10%**; FAST-WAM **97.60% → 98.10%**; ImageWAM **98.10% → 98.40%**. Evaluation: 40 tasks, 50 rollouts each. | Table I; §IV-A–B |
| Zero-shot generalization improves | LIBERO-Plus overall baseline → LIT: π0.5 **68.97% → 79.67%**; MolmoAct2 **63.62% → 71.92%**; FAST-WAM **51.44% → 60.63%**; ImageWAM **83.02% → 86.89%**. Overall is the unweighted mean across seven perturbation dimensions; 10,030 instances, one rollout each, fixed evaluation seed. | Table II; §IV-A, §IV-C |
| Improvements are not universal | LIT improves **26/28** architecture–perturbation comparisons. Language-instruction success decreases by **2.11 points** for MolmoAct2 and **0.92 points** for ImageWAM. | Table II; §IV-C |
| Physical manipulation benefits in aggregate | MolmoAct2 baseline → LIT across three tasks: ID **74.7% → 88.0%**; lighting **53.3% → 70.0%**; top-camera-only **30.0% → 46.7%**; distractors **50.0% → 63.3%**. Each policy uses 300 demonstrations; evaluation uses 75 ID rollouts and 30 rollouts per OOD condition. | §IV-D; Fig. 6 |
| Each main component contributes in the tested architecture | MolmoAct2 LIBERO-Plus overall: full LIT **71.92%**; without Stage 1 **68.23%**; without pose supervision **68.86%**; with direct visual access **67.74%**. | Table III; §IV-E.3 |
| Simpler alternatives recover smaller gains | MolmoAct2 LIBERO-Plus overall: token aggregation alone **65.70%**; LA4VLA-inspired staged training **65.46%**; baseline with pose supervision **65.45%**, versus baseline **63.62%** and LIT **71.92%**. | Table III; §IV-E.3 |
| Behavioral probes support the proposed explanation | Qualitative attention and trajectory examples show greater stability under distractors/blur and redirection after instruction changes. No aggregate intervention metric is supplied. | Figs. 4–5; §IV-E.1 |

These results establish improved measured success under the evaluated conditions and support the combined design. They do not demonstrate that shortcut reliance is eliminated or uniquely explain the gains: the mechanism evidence is qualitative, and component ablations are confined to MolmoAct2.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Larger and more diverse real-world datasets, tasks, and environments remain unevaluated (§V).
- **Analyst inference—uncertainty:** No confidence intervals or repeated-training-seed results are reported. LIBERO-Plus uses a fixed evaluation seed, and physical OOD evaluation has only 10 rollouts per task per condition (§IV-A, §IV-D).
- **Comparison boundary:** Baselines train randomly initialized action experts from pretrained backbones. The authors explicitly caution that these results are not directly comparable with published results from fine-tuning pretrained policy checkpoints (§IV-A).
- **Analyst inference—mechanism isolation:** There is no otherwise matched LIT variant removing only Stage 1 terminal-goal conditioning. The staged-training alternative also changes later visual conditioning, limiting isolation of that specific contribution (Table III).
- **Analyst inference—resource equivalence:** Equal optimization-step budgets do not establish equal compute cost; runtime, memory, and inference overhead are not reported (§IV-A).

## Open research questions
1. **Does LIT help already-pretrained robot policies?** The experiments deliberately avoid policy checkpoints. Next experiment: compare matched LIT adaptation and standard fine-tuning from the same pretrained policies.
2. **How much does Stage 1 spatial-goal conditioning contribute independently?** Current ablations do not isolate it. Next experiment: remove only terminal-goal conditioning while retaining the same Stage 2 interface, supervision, and budget.
3. **How reliably does LIT suppress nuisance dependence?** Current intervention evidence is qualitative. Next experiment: measure trajectory deviation and task success over a larger controlled intervention set with repeated seeds.
4. **Does the benefit persist across physical tasks and embodiments?** Physical evaluation covers one model and three tasks. Next experiment: expand tasks and robot platforms while reporting uncertainty and compute costs.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly supports the reported performance comparisons; causal interpretation and broader generalization remain less certain.
