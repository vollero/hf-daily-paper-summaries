# Pelican-Sim 1.0: A General World Model Simulator for Embodied Intelligence

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.12036) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12036)


## One-sentence takeaway

Pelican-Sim combines numerical robot configurations with rendered motion guidance to improve video prediction and RoboTwin policy performance, while its broader transfer evidence remains qualitative (Tables 2, 9, 12, 14; §4.9).

## Problem and motivation

Robot world models must predict how actions change visual observations across different embodiments and scenes. Numerical commands preserve configuration details but leave their image-space effects implicit; visual action representations expose motion geometry but can obscure the underlying configuration. The authors address this complementary information gap, alongside the computational cost of repeated predictive rollouts (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Complementary action conditioning:** A unified 28-dimensional configuration layout is paired with synchronized whole-arm skeleton videos rendered using robot URDFs and camera calibration. Numerical modulation and visual residuals enter alternating blocks of a Cosmos-Predict 2.5 Video DiT (§§3.1–3.3).
- **Capacity for heterogeneous data:** Sparse mixture-of-experts layers use one shared expert and eight routed experts, selecting two routed experts per token. Training combines approximately one million real and simulated trajectories (§§3.3, 4.1).
- **Accelerated rollouts:** Causal adaptation and distribution-matching distillation produce a four-step autoregressive student used for downstream experiments (§3.4).
- **Downstream integration:** Generated videos augment policy demonstrations; a separately fine-tuned VLM supplies outcome scores for policy ranking, action selection, and GRPO policy improvement (§§4.5–4.8).

The contribution is chiefly this integrated action interface and simulator pipeline; the backbone, MoE, distillation, and policy-optimization methods build on cited work. Checkpoint and inference-code release is promised, not established as completed (§§2–3; contribution list, p. 4).

## Evidence and main results

All results below are author-reported.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Stronger held-out video prediction | PSNR: **22.276 vs 17.640 dB** on AgiBotWorld Beta versus EnerVerse-AC; **23.850 vs 21.770** on RoboMIND and **30.383 vs 20.040** on RoboTwin versus Ctrl-World. Baselines were retrained on the same mixed corpus. | §4.2; Table 2 |
| Higher aggregate action-generation scores, with exceptions | Adapted EWMBench overall: **4.997 vs 4.431**, **4.666 vs 4.276**, and **5.044 vs 3.971**, respectively, versus each dataset’s strongest evaluated baseline. Some motion, scene-consistency, and diversity components favor baselines. | Table 3 |
| Complementary conditioning and MoE help | AgiBotWorld Beta PSNR: dual conditioning **22.276 vs 21.276 dB** for visual-only conditioning; selected interleaving **22.276 vs 21.372** for late fusion. Mixed-data MoE FVD: **10.850 vs 17.380** for dense. | Tables 4–6 |
| Faster sampling | Four-step causal student: **2.2 seconds per 21-frame clip**, reported **5.67×** speedup over the original 35-step model. Corresponding quality comparison is absent. | §3.4 |
| Demonstration augmentation improves policy success | With **50 randomized originals plus 500 generated trajectories per task**, π0.5 success rises **70.0%→93.0%**; evaluation uses **200 unseen RoboTwin seeds per task**. | §4.5; Table 9 |
| Cross-domain augmentation is a separate, smaller result | **50 clean plus 500 generated trajectories per task** yield **72.0%**, versus **70.0%** from **50 directly collected randomized trajectories**, averaged over five tasks and 200 unseen seeds per task. | Table 8 |
| Adapted pipeline predicts checkpoint performance | Across five checkpoints from one training run, with 200 matched-action initial conditions each, **1,000 adaptation rollouts** yield Pearson **r=0.994**, ranking correlations **1.0**, and success-rate MAE **2.4 percentage points**; without adaptation, MAE is **8.6 points**. | §4.6; Table 11 |
| Predicted futures improve candidate selection | Ten-candidate selection achieves **63.8%**, versus **43.2%** single-sample, **52.3%** Ctrl-World, and **68.1%** oracle success; five tasks, 200 conditions per task. Generation plus scoring takes **15.9 seconds per decision on four GPUs**. | Tables 12–13 |
| Imagined-rollout optimization improves policies | Full GRPO pipeline: **75.4%** mean success versus **62.7%** initial SFT, **67.0%** Iter-SFT, **68.5%** terminal-only GRPO, and **83.8%** simulator-GRPO oracle; five tasks, three policy-training runs. | §4.8; Table 14 |

The evidence supports better within-source visual prediction and useful downstream simulation pipelines. It does not establish physically accurate general simulation or real-robot policy improvements. Policy ranking tests fixed action trajectories, whereas policy improvement uses closed-loop imagined observations; these establish different capabilities (§§4.6, 4.8).

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Authors acknowledge incomplete generality testing:** Quantitative prediction uses within-source splits; distribution-shift evidence consists of qualitative examples. Broader transfer protocols and distillation quality–efficiency analysis remain future work (§§4.1, 4.9, 5).
- **Authors qualify physical consistency:** Rendered skeletons obey nominal kinematics, but generated RGB has no hard kinematic constraint. Reversed trajectories do not demonstrate reversible contact dynamics, and edited objects need not share physical properties (§§3.2, 4.9).
- **Authors acknowledge ablation confounds:** Mixed-domain training changes both corpus size and diversity. The reward comparison does not isolate progress scoring from validity weighting (§§4.4, 4.8).
- **Analyst inference—limited downstream scope:** RoboTwin tasks and five checkpoints from one policy-training run provide limited evidence for unrelated policies, new tasks, or real-robot deployment (§§4.5–4.8).
- **Analyst inference—uncertain attribution and precision:** Dense-versus-MoE comparisons do not establish a parameter- or compute-matched advantage. Reported tables omit uncertainty intervals; the small cross-domain augmentation difference is not evidence of statistical superiority (Tables 4, 8, 14).
- **Analyst inference—pipeline dependence:** Downstream gains depend on task-specific simulator adaptation, annotated VLM supervision, and selection or optimization procedures, so they should not be attributed to the world model alone (§§4.1, 4.6–4.8).

## Open research questions

1. **Does transfer preserve physical outcomes on unseen robots?** Embodiment transfer is qualitative. Next experiment: execute matched trajectories on held-out robots and measure contact outcomes, object displacement, and task success (§4.9).
2. **What quality is lost through acceleration?** Speed is reported without a matched teacher–student quality study. Next experiment: compare prediction error and downstream success across sampling steps and rollout horizons (§§3.4, 5).
3. **Can ranking generalize beyond related checkpoints?** Current checkpoints share one training run. Next experiment: rank independently trained policy families on held-out tasks under fixed adaptation budgets (§4.6).
4. **Which reward components prevent misleading optimization?** The terminal-only ablation bundles progress and validity changes. Next experiment: independently vary those components and compare VLM reward with simulator-checker success (§4.8).

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** Main comparisons and protocols are explicit, but broad generality and physical fidelity remain weakly tested.

Material missing information includes exact downstream task identities, uncertainty estimates, adapted EWMBench scoring details, evaluator training-set size and several hyperparameters, matched distillation-quality results, quantitative long-horizon/OOD evaluation, real-robot downstream validation, and confirmation that promised artifacts were released.
