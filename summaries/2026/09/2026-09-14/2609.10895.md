# ReactHuman: A Physics-Grounded Benchmark for Human-Like Reactive Decision-Making in Embodied Multimodal LLMs

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-14.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.10895) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10895)


## One-sentence takeaway
ReactHuman evaluates simulated household hazard responses through executable motor plans, finding limited action accuracy, frequent unsafe responses when evasion is required, and substantial spatial errors across seven multimodal models (§5).

## Problem and motivation
The authors identify a gap between passive physical-reasoning tests and deliberate embodied tasks: neither directly measures whether a model can turn a brief observation of a sudden hazard into a safe, physically appropriate reaction. ReactHuman targets this decision layer while distinguishing action selection, safety, and spatial grounding (§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Executable reaction benchmark:** Models observe approximately 0.6 seconds of synchronized multiview imagery, then output intent, confidence, base velocity, and hand keyframes while simulation is paused. A simulated Unitree G1 executes the committed plan; deterministic rules derive Catch, Dodge, or No_Action from the motor commands (§3.1).
- **Procedural generation:** An LLM selects semantic scene elements, while a seeded randomizer controls physical and visual parameters. Genesis simulation supplies action labels, impact points, and time-to-floor. The authors report over 1,000 scenes across 17 event families and procedural scalability beyond 10,000 scenes (§1, §3.4).
- **Diagnostic assets and metrics:** Appearance–physics mismatches test reliance on visual identity. Five metrics assess action accuracy, rule-based safety, hand endpoint error, intent alignment, and hand-distance evolution (§3.3–4).
- **Zero-shot study:** Seven models are evaluated on a family-balanced subset; the paper reports action biases, slow-hazard failures, and weak responses to disguised physical properties (§5).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Evaluation scope | Seven models × 306 scenes, with 18 scenes per family; 2,138 decisions after four API losses. | §5.1 |
| Action accuracy is modest | Mean semantic action accuracy: 54.0%; GPT-5.5 leads at 63.7%. Always choosing Dodge achieves 51.0% on the scene labels. | Table 1; §3.1 |
| Safety depends strongly on the required action | Mean safety validity: 80.8%; Gemma-3-27B leads at 88.2%. On Dodge-required scenes, 35.9% of decisions violate a safety rule. | Table 1; §5.2 |
| Correct action selection does not ensure spatial accuracy | For decisions described as choosing the correct action, median hand error is 0.48 m; 89% of misses fall short. The exact qualifying subset size is not reported. | §5.2, “Correct but out of reach” |
| Slow hazards elicit inaction | Across 826 speed-probe decisions in 12 families, freezing occurs in 42% at the slowest band versus approximately 21% at faster bands; SAA is 38.1% versus 47–54%. | §5.2; Figure 5 |
| Adversarial materials rarely change reported behavior | Across 280 decisions on 40 adversarial scenes, no model verbalizes material or weight doubt; steel and light cans both receive reaching responses in 39% of decisions. | §5.2, “Adversarial probes” |
| Ensemble voting does not beat the strongest model | Majority-vote accuracy is 62.1%, versus 63.7% for the best individual model and 54.0% mean individual accuracy. | §5.2 |

These results establish weaknesses under this particular simulated, open-loop protocol. The “roughly one hazard in three” characterization corresponds most directly to **Dodge-required scenes**, rather than overall safety validity. Physical execution provides observable consequences, but the headline metrics use simulator labels and commanded trajectories; the paper does not report aggregate executed catch-success or collision rates (§3.1, §4).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Rigid-body simulation excludes deformation and shattering; hardware validation remains necessary. Plans are executed without replanning, and results depend on the queried API snapshots (§6).
- **Analyst inference — human-likeness:** “Reasonable” behavior is defined through catalog properties and rules. No human-participant comparison validates whether these labels capture human reactive judgment (§3–5).
- **Analyst inference — scoring scope:** Safety validity measures specified action violations, while spatial metrics compare hand positions against an impact point. Neither alone establishes successful, timely physical avoidance or interception (§4).
- **Analyst inference — material observability:** The paper asserts that disguised properties are recoverable from motion but does not demonstrate observability for each probe. Absence of verbal doubt does not establish absence of internal updating (§3.3, §5.2).
- **Analyst inference — broad causal claims:** Cross-model rankings and class-conditioned accuracies support behavioral differences, but do not establish universal failure to adapt or a controlled model-scaling law. No controlled scaling study is reported (§5).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** The text reports 253 freezing mistakes in the Dodge analysis and 162 R3 freezing violations overall without explaining their relationship. Safety-rule overlap and assignment priority are also unclear (§4–5.2).

## Open research questions
- **Does replanning improve safety?** Only one committed plan is tested. Compare the current protocol with repeated observation–action cycles on matched scenes, measuring executed collisions and catches (§6).
- **Can models infer disguised properties when motion is demonstrably informative?** Probe identifiability is not established. Construct matched appearance pairs with validated motion cues and measure both property estimates and actions (§3.3).
- **How much spatial failure comes from reasoning versus the interface/controller?** Scores and execution are separated. Compare model plans with oracle interception plans through the same controller, reporting contact timing and execution success (§3.1, §5.1).
- **Does performance match competent human judgment?** Human-likeness lacks a participant baseline. Have humans respond to the same observations and compare decisions, safety, and achievable interception plans (§1, §4).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly describes the benchmark and principal results, but broader behavioral conclusions exceed the reported evidence.

Material missing information includes exact prompts and decoding settings, API snapshot dates, action-classification thresholds, safety-rule precedence, distance-metric denominators, uncertainty estimates, full execution-success statistics, and independently verified release contents.
