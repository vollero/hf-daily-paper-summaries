# CodeGraph: Open-Taxonomy Knowledge Graph for Source Code with Wikidata Grounding

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.29474) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29474)


## One-sentence takeaway
CodeGraph adds LLM-extracted, Wikidata-linked semantic annotations to approximately 145 million source files, demonstrating large-scale construction but leaving annotation accuracy and downstream utility only partially validated.

## Problem and motivation
Code archives contain engineering knowledge about algorithms, programming paradigms, design patterns, and application domains that is difficult to retrieve through tokens or syntax alone. The authors identify a gap between large, sparsely annotated corpora and smaller resources with task-specific or structural metadata (§1, Table 1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Open-taxonomy extraction:** Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct processes whole files using separate structured prompts for domains, algorithms, paradigms, and patterns. Free-form labels undergo normalization and consolidation; algorithm annotations also include categories and inferred complexity (§§4.1–4.2).
- **Wikidata grounding:** SPARQL resolves unambiguous labels; a Qwen3.6-27B agent handles ambiguous or empty candidate sets; hierarchy expansion adds parent concepts. The graph preserves local concepts, external identifiers, and file provenance (§4.3).
- **Quality assessment:** Human judgments select an LLM verifier, whose acceptance decisions accompany the extracted annotations (§5).
- **Large-scale resource:** The principal artifact is a typed semantic graph over Stack-Edu’s programming-language files. The authors claim novelty in combining open-taxonomy semantics, grounding, and this scale; Table 1 provides resource positioning rather than a controlled performance comparison.

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Large graph constructed | Reports 158,202,834 nodes and 1,020,887,480 edges, including 144,910,008 source files across 14 programming languages. | §6 |
| High vocabulary linking coverage | Consolidated labels linked to QIDs: domains 36,879/37,412 (98.6%); algorithms 17,492/19,660 (89.0%); patterns 5,666/5,955 (95.1%); paradigms 188/193 (97.4%). These are coverage measurements, not linking accuracy. | §8, Fig. 9 |
| Human agreement is weak | On the study covering 127 files, overall Fleiss’ κ is 0.153; domains 0.213, algorithms 0.200, patterns 0.024, paradigms −0.111. | §5 |
| Devstral leads verifier selection | Against human gold labels, Devstral-2 2512 achieves 80.62% accuracy, 82.05% precision, 96.00% recall, and 88.48% F1; six candidates span approximately 84.9–88.5% F1 across 1,536 model–human comparison pairs. | §5, Table 2 |
| Local-label acceptance exceeds grounded acceptance | On the 10,000-file silver subset, acceptance is 88% algorithms, 82% patterns, 88% domains, 93% paradigms. On the grounded subset, whose size is unspecified, rates are 62%, 58%, 62%, and 94%, respectively. | §5, Figs. 7–8 |
| Substantial compute expenditure | Approximately 400,000 GPU-hours on 256 A100 GPUs; §3.2 attributes this to extraction, whereas §9 distributes it across extraction, grounding, and verification. | §§3.2, 9 |

The evidence supports construction at scale and measures agreement with a limited human reference. It does not establish extraction recall, independently audited entity-linking accuracy, or improvements in retrieval or algorithm selection; §7 presents proposed applications. The authors’ suggested 68–88% precision bracket combines human approval and verifier acceptance under different settings, so it should not be treated as a validated statistical interval (§5).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** The human gold set is small, and paradigm/pattern judgments are unreliable enough that §8 recommends treating these axes as exploratory. Low-frequency concepts likewise provide weak foundations for quantitative claims (§§5, 8).
- **Author hypothesis:** Lower grounded acceptance may reflect a mismatch between function/class concepts and whole-file evaluation. The paper does not test this explanation (§5).
- **Analyst inference—limited generalization:** Validation covers six languages, whereas construction covers fourteen; pedagogically filtered Stack-Edu also limits claims about arbitrary public code (§§3.1, 5).
- **Analyst inference—missing component evidence:** No quantified ablation establishes the benefits of separate prompts, agent disambiguation, or hierarchy expansion. Grounding coverage alone cannot establish correct semantic matches (§§4–5).
- **Reporting ambiguities:** §5 reports 292 judgments and three annotators per pair without reconciling the evaluation denominators; it separately gives Devstral 77.3% accuracy on 256 comparisons versus Table 2’s 80.62%. It also alternates between caution and high-confidence claims about paradigms.
- **Reproducibility ambiguities:** Hierarchy expansion is described both as a closure and as two hops, with inconsistent property descriptions (§4.3). Full dataset availability is asserted in Data Availability but deferred in §9. Compute accounting also differs between sections.

## Open research questions
1. **Does the graph improve semantic code retrieval?** §7 provides scenarios without retrieval results. Compare lexical, dense, and graph-assisted retrieval on the same relevance-labeled queries.
2. **Does finer annotation granularity improve grounded validity?** The file-versus-function explanation remains hypothetical (§5). Repeat expert evaluation with matched file-, function-, and class-level evidence.
3. **What does agent grounding add beyond SPARQL?** Stage-specific accuracy and coverage are absent (§4.3). Audit deterministic and agent-resolved links separately and ablate the agent.
4. **How reliable are labels across languages and frequency bands?** Existing validation is small and narrower than construction (§§5, 8). Expand expert adjudication across all construction languages and common versus rare concepts.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The architecture and reported scale are clear; accuracy and utility claims have weaker support.

Material missing information includes reconciled evaluation denominators, uncertainty estimates, grounded-subset size, independent linking accuracy, extraction recall, component ablations, downstream benchmarks, exact consolidation thresholds, and unambiguous release and compute-accounting details.
