# CARD: Cluster-level Adaptation with Reward-guided Decoding for Personalized Text Generation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2601.06352) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06352)


## One-sentence takeaway
CARD combines shared cluster LoRA adapters with compact user vectors for decoding-time personalization, ranking first on 10 of 12 reported ROUGE comparisons, although practical efficiency and privacy benefits remain unverified.

## Problem and motivation
Personalized generation must capture individual writing preferences without expensive per-user model adaptation. The authors identify two gaps: retrieval-based personalization depends on context quality and length, while per-user adapters impose storage and onboarding costs; sparse histories also make reliable preference supervision difficult (§1).

CARD aims to share adaptation across similar users while reserving lightweight decoding controls for individual differences.

## Approach and contributions
- **Hierarchical adaptation:** Embed user histories with a frozen encoder, apply K-means, and train a LoRA adapter on each cluster’s pooled examples (§2.3).
- **Implicit preference construction:** Treat user-authored responses as preferred and cluster-model responses to the same prompts as dispreferred. The authors argue that this reduces topical confounding; the paper does not directly establish that the differences are purely stylistic (§2.4).
- **User-specific logit steering:** Project hidden states into a compact space, multiply by a learned user vector, and map the result into vocabulary-logit corrections. A Bradley–Terry loss trains the personalization head and vectors; corrections affect only top-ranked candidate tokens (§2.5).
- **Compact onboarding:** Assign a new user to a cluster and optimize only their preference vector while freezing the backbone and cluster adapter (§2.6; Table 10). This still requires user-specific optimization.

The proposed contribution is this combination and supervision strategy; clustering, LoRA, and pairwise preference learning are constituent methods rather than individually new inventions.

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

| Broad ROUGE advantage | With Qwen3-8B on six LaMP/LongLaMP tasks, CARD leads 10/12 task–metric combinations. Exceptions: LaMP5 R-1, 0.459 versus PPLUG’s 0.464; LongLaMP2 R-1, 0.252 versus PAG’s 0.255. | Table 1; §4.1 |
| Gains vary substantially by task | LaMP4 R-1/R-L: CARD 0.218/0.195 versus strongest competing scores 0.178/0.160 from Contriever RAG. LongLaMP3: 0.405/0.156 versus Contriever RAG’s 0.398/0.155. | Table 1 |
| Both components help | On LaMP4, full CARD R-1 is 0.218, versus 0.207 without LoRA and 0.148 without the user vector; removing either component reduces both metrics across all three LaMP tasks. | Table 2 |
| Limited-history performance is promising | Retaining the first five histories per testing user yields approximately 0.216 LaMP4 R-1 for CARD versus approximately 0.146 for the non-personalized baseline. | §4.5; Figure 4 |
| Personalization configuration matters | A 128-dimensional vector outperforms dimensions 32, 64, and 256 on both ROUGE metrics across the three LaMP tasks; four aggregated hidden layers outperform one and eight. | Tables 3–4 |
| Judge agreement is positive but incomplete | Across LaMP4/5/7, reported mean LLM–human Spearman correlation is 0.670 and weighted kappa is 0.618; sample sizes and uncertainty are absent. | Appendix E; Table 11 |
| Efficiency evidence is analytical | Default user vectors contain 128 dimensions; top-k logit correction has stated cost O(kJ). The comparison supplies asymptotic costs rather than measured latency or memory. | §§2.5, 4.7; Table 6 |

These results support improved reference overlap and contributions from both personalization stages in the reported benchmark settings. They do not establish statistical significance, purely stylistic improvement, end-to-end deployment savings, or privacy guarantees. Model-scale results cover Qwen models only and lack matched non-personalized results at each scale (Table 5).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** K-means may miss complex user relationships; a single uninterpretable vector may inadequately represent diverse or evolving preferences; the multistage pipeline adds coordination; noisy histories can degrade personalization (Limitations).
- **Analyst inference—supervision:** Sharing an input does not ensure that generated negatives and user responses preserve identical meaning. No negative-construction ablation directly tests the claimed removal of semantic confounding (§2.4).
- **Analyst inference—evaluation separation:** Validation sets serve as test sets, while PAD’s steering coefficient is explicitly tuned on validation. An independent tuning/evaluation partition is not described (Appendices C, D.7).
- **Analyst inference—privacy and context ambiguity:** Claims of avoiding raw history in prompts conflict with Equation 1’s explicit history injection. No leakage evaluation resolves the privacy claim (§§1–2; Ethical Considerations).
- **Reporting inconsistency:** Table 7 reports a 71.7% LongLaMP1 R-L improvement, whereas Table 1’s 0.184-to-0.216 change implies approximately 17.4%. Relative-gain summaries therefore require caution.
- **Reproducibility gaps:** The text omits cluster count, CARD’s candidate-token count, full shared-head training details, human evaluation sample sizes and annotator procedures, and measured deployment costs.

## Open research questions
1. **Does cluster-generated supervision isolate style?** The claim lacks a direct ablation. Compare cluster, generic-model, and random negatives while separately rating semantic fidelity and style.
2. **Can compact vectors track changing preferences?** Fixed vectors are an acknowledged limitation. Evaluate sequential adaptation on chronologically held-out histories against periodically retrained and context-dependent vectors.
3. **Do storage savings translate into serving gains?** Only asymptotic efficiency is reported. Measure onboarding time, total memory, throughput, and user-switch latency across increasing user and cluster counts.
4. **Does CARD work equally well without history in inference prompts?** The formulation and privacy narrative differ. Compare explicit history-free and history-conditioned variants for quality and leakage.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The mechanism and primary ROUGE results are clearly documented, but reporting inconsistencies and incomplete evaluation details weaken broader conclusions.

Material missing information includes independent test results, uncertainty across runs, complete human-evaluation methodology, LongLaMP dataset statistics, key clustering/steering settings, measured efficiency, and empirical privacy evidence.
