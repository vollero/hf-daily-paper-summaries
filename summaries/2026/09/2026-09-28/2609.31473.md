# Game Arena: Strategic LLM Evaluation in Competitive Environments

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.31473) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31473)


## One-sentence takeaway
Game Arena provides a shared platform for outcome-based LLM evaluation across Chess, Poker, and Werewolf, finding that model rankings differ substantially across strategic settings.

## Problem and motivation
The authors argue that static benchmarks face saturation and contamination, while human- or model-judged evaluations introduce subjective biases. Existing game benchmarks, they contend, often lack continuous evaluation infrastructure, extensible environments, or sufficient sampling for high-variance games (§1).

Their proposed alternative is repeated competition with objectively scored outcomes and increasingly capable opponents. Resistance to future saturation and transfer to real-world decision-making are motivations, rather than demonstrated results.

## Approach and contributions
- **Shared evaluation infrastructure:** Text-based game interfaces, action validation, retries, trajectory logging, and replay visualization. The authors report releasing environments, harnesses, prompts, evaluation scripts, and gameplay datasets (§2; Appendix A.1).
- **Three complementary environments:** Chess tests planning and move legality; heads-up poker tests decisions under uncertainty with within-episode opponent histories; Werewolf tests communication and asymmetric team play. A Chess Opening variant checks sensitivity to opening positions (§§2.1–2.3).
- **Game-specific statistical evaluation:** Bradley–Terry Elo for Chess, episode-block-bootstrapped BB/100 for Poker, and an adaptation of existing game-theoretic evaluation for Werewolf. Poker uses mirrored deals; Werewolf adds rule-balance experiments and role-specific analysis (§2.4; Appendices A.5–A.7).

The main contribution is the integrated benchmark and empirical evaluation, rather than a new model-training method.

These results support game-dependent rankings under the supplied harnesses and opponent pools. They do not establish universal strategic competence, causal explanations for model differences, real-world transfer, or long-term immunity to benchmark saturation.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Fixed match budgets waste computation on lopsided pairings; model churn complicates longitudinal comparisons; cross-game aggregation remains unresolved; the game suite is an initial pilot (§4).
- **Analyst inference—configuration sensitivity:** Provider-default generation limits do not establish equal computational budgets. Prompting, retries, context truncation, and action handling can influence rankings; their separate effects are not quantified (§§2.1–2.5; Appendix B).
- **Analyst inference—adaptation is not isolated:** Poker supplies prior-hand histories and reveals both players’ cards after every hand, but no history-removal or card-revelation ablation establishes how much performance comes from opponent adaptation (§2.2).
- **Analyst inference—limited balance validation:** Werewolf balance is tested using one model’s self-play, and the final configuration bundles several changes. This does not establish balance across model populations or isolate each change’s contribution (Appendix A.5).
- **Reporting ambiguities:** §2.5 says ten models but lists eleven; Appendix Table 1 contains different pools across games. Table 2 also has slightly incomplete hand counts for two poker models without explanation. The cooperative Substitution Index formula subtracts replacement performance from original performance, while its prose gives the opposite sign interpretation (Appendix A.6.1).

## Open research questions
1. **Does poker history produce measurable adaptation?** The paper enables adaptation without isolating it. Compare matched deals with full history, no history, and concealed non-showdown cards.
2. **How robust are rankings to harness and compute choices?** Defaults and retry policies vary across settings. Repeat evaluations with matched token budgets and controlled prompt/retry ablations.
3. **Does Werewolf balance generalize across populations?** Current calibration uses one model. Repeat rule experiments with several self-play models and mixed-model teams.
4. **Can evaluation become cheaper while preserving rankings?** Fixed scheduling is an acknowledged limitation. Compare uncertainty-driven scheduling against full round-robin results using held-out matches and interval coverage.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the main benchmark design and ranking differences, but reporting inconsistencies limit precise reconstruction.

Material gaps include exact API versions and generation settings, explanations for differing model pools and missing poker hands, fully specified Werewolf assignment sampling, and numerical cost/token data underlying the cost-performance plots. Release availability was not independently checked; the retrieved text does not expose all plotted values.
