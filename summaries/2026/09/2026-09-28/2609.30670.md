# TRACE: Temporal Audit and Condition-aware Evaluation of Streaming Video Understanding

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.30670) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30670)


## One-sentence takeaway
TRACE shows that similar streaming-video accuracy can conceal different completion, response timing, false alarms, and workloads, motivating evaluation tied to audited evidence timing and explicit execution conditions.

## Problem and motivation
Streaming-video models must answer using evidence available at the response time and decide when monitored conditions justify speaking. The authors argue that causal access and task accuracy alone leave comparisons underspecified: history reconstruction, persistent state, response initiation, and system boundaries can change what a score measures (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Temporally audited benchmark:** TRACE revises visual-only tasks from StreamingBench and OVO-Bench, annotating QA evidence timing and instruction-dependent proactive triggers: event onset, event completion, sufficient evidence, and state intervals. The standard set contains 833 QA records and 407 proactive records, totaling 1,240 records across 517 videos (§4).
- **Core–Adapter execution protocol:** A shared Core supplies causal, timestamped frames; model-specific Adapters record history processing, submissions, outputs, and failures. Reports distinguish persistent streaming state, query-time history reconstruction, and complete-system boundaries (§3.2).
- **Multidimensional measurement:** QA reporting separates accuracy, parsing validity, completion, latency, and workload. Proactive reporting separates in-window content accuracy, observed response delay, false alarms, and missed windows. Eight tested configurations illustrate why these dimensions should remain separate (§§3.3, 5–6).

The contribution is an evaluation framework and audited assets, rather than a new video-understanding model.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Temporal review changed substantive annotations | Of 328 edited records, 325 had timing/trigger changes; 52 records were excluded. Median absolute comparable timestamp revisions were 3.0 s for QA and 2.24 s for proactive tasks. These measure annotation movement, not score changes. | §4.2 |
| Nearly equal QA accuracy hid operational differences | On 833 scheduled QA records, LiveCC versus MOSS-Preview: accuracy 65.19% versus 65.07%; completion 93.88% versus 100%; invalid outputs 6.12% versus 1.92%; observed total output tokens 146,064 versus 42,206. Both use native model + Adapter boundaries. | Table 4 |
| Similar proactive accuracy hid timing and false-alarm differences | On 1,270 target windows with 5 s point-event tolerance, MOSS-VL versus AURA: in-window accuracy 8.05% versus 7.92%; median observed delay 0.33 s versus 1.15 s; false-alarm episode rate 41.1% versus 59.1%. | Table 5 |
| Covering windows did not ensure appropriate response selection | Under the same proactive setting, LiveCC had 12.98% in-window accuracy and 0.31% missed windows, alongside a 65.0% false-alarm episode rate. | Table 5 |
| System boundaries affect interpretation | JoyAI achieved 17.08% proactive in-window accuracy over the standard windows, measured across its complete system, including memory and scheduling components. | Table 5; §6.3 |
| Delay estimates have incomplete coverage | Observed onset timestamps covered 45.4% of MOSS-VL’s answered windows and 53.7% of AURA’s; their reported delay medians use only those observed subsets. | Table 9 |

These results support the authors’ central claim that task accuracy alone does not characterize observed execution behavior. They do **not** establish hardware-normalized efficiency rankings or isolate the causal effects of history mechanisms. Token totals are observed telemetry, not comparable compute costs. Proactive accuracy retains partial credit; false alarms use response episodes as their denominator, while misses use target windows.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Visual-only, single-instruction evaluation at 1 FPS limits generalization to audio, multi-turn interaction, higher sampling rates, and sustained operation. Positive-trigger tasks do not establish false-positive behavior on videos with no target event (§8).
- **Annotation uncertainty:** Review was assisted rather than blind duplicate annotation; researchers did not independently re-annotate every record (§4.2).
- **Judge uncertainty:** Semantic scoring uses a text-only judge. Calibration involved one rater and a small stratified sample, cannot establish model-independent bias, and does not validate continuous partial-credit calibration (Appendix A.2).
- **Measurement limitations:** Missing onset timestamps constrain delay interpretation. Some runs executed with longer tolerances before uniform rescoring; image accounting, frame drops, and token coverage also differ (Appendix B.2).
- **Interface dependence:** MiniCPM-O’s poor QA results reflect substantial mismatch between spoken-style output and option parsing, limiting conclusions about underlying capability (§6.3).
- **Analyst inference:** No before/after model-score experiment isolates the effect of temporal auditing. Annotation revisions demonstrate changed boundaries, but not quantified improvements in ranking validity. Also, the prose sometimes implies evidence-time edits change legal QA input, whereas the formal protocol defines visibility by question-arrival time; that distinction is not fully resolved (§§3.3, 4.2, 6.1).

## Open research questions
1. **How much does temporal auditing change scores and rankings?** Revision magnitudes alone do not answer this. Rescore fixed response traces against original and audited annotations, separating evidence-time from question-arrival changes.
2. **Can models remain appropriately silent without target events?** Positive-trigger coverage leaves this open. Add matched no-trigger videos and measure false responses per unit monitoring time.
3. **How sensitive are proactive comparisons to timing and judge uncertainty?** Onset coverage is incomplete and calibration preliminary. Repeat evaluation with complete onset logging and independent human scoring on a model-balanced sample.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the descriptive findings; **medium** in broader capability comparisons.

Material information absent from the supplied text includes detailed checkpoint/Adapter manifests, the exact judge prompt, complete per-record traces, counts separating retained human-reviewed records from model-screened-only records, and controlled before/after audit results. The paper says several supporting artifacts are released, but their contents were not supplied or independently verified.
