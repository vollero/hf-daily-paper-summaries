# Morphometric Imitation: From Morphology and Contact Aware Hand Retargeting to Sim-to-Real Visuomotor Policy

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.28660) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28660)


## One-sentence takeaway
Morphology- and contact-aware retargeting improves human-motion transfer across three robot hands, enabling category-specific visuomotor policies to achieve 89.33% success in 300 hardware trials on one arm–hand system without real-world training data (Tables II–V).

## Problem and motivation
Human manipulation demonstrations could reduce reliance on robot teleoperation, but transferring them requires reconciling hand morphology, producing physically feasible contacts, and overcoming the simulation-to-reality gap. The authors identify limitations in retargeting methods that inadequately preserve contact geometry and pipelines that require specially constrained human demonstrations or real robot fine-tuning (§I).

The evaluated input is motion-capture data from GRAB; learning from monocular-video reconstructions remains future work (§VIII).

## Approach and contributions
1. **Morphometric optimization (MMO).** Extend MANO with independent palm and finger scaling, align it to the robot’s morphology, restore demonstrated contacts through per-frame optimization, then transfer the aligned skeleton through linear blend retargeting and inverse kinematics. Table-collision penalties are included (§IV).
2. **Contact- and object-aware residual RL.** A PPO teacher corrects the kinematic joint targets using privileged simulation state. Observations, rewards, and termination rules incorporate object tracking and contact information. The contact reward matches the number of contacting fingers, not their exact contact locations (§V).
3. **Integrated visuomotor transfer.** Simulated teacher demonstrations train a point-cloud student using ManiFlow’s existing architecture and training formulation. Physical and observation randomization support deployment without real-world training data. Separate teachers and students are trained per object category (§VI).

The principal methodological contributions are MMO and the residual-RL formulation; the student architecture is adopted from prior work.

## Evidence and main results
Values below use the hand order **Dex3 / Allegro / Sharpa** where grouped.

The controlled reference comparison supports MMO’s benefit within this residual-RL pipeline. However, pointwise bootstrap intervals exclude zero in only **13/15 kinematic F1**, **11/15 dynamic success**, and **10/15 C-ADD** comparisons. These intervals resample selected demonstrations or categories, have no multiplicity correction, and do not measure independent training-seed variability (Tables VIII, X).

Hardware results establish feasibility for the tested objects and platform; they do not establish superiority over competing sim-to-real pipelines, which were not compared (§VII).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Separate policies are required per category, hardware evaluation uses one arm–hand system, and training demonstrations comprise ten motion-capture trajectories. Unified policies, other hardware hands, monocular reconstructions, and bimanual interaction remain untested (§VIII).
- **Author-observed generalization failures:** Localization depends on workspace position; inadequate pre-grasp opening causes failures on thicker objects. Anisotropic scaling does not capture some torus shape changes (§VII-F; Appendix D).
- **Perception qualification:** Transparent wineglasses require ping-pong balls for visibility. The results demonstrate transfer with that intervention, not unaided perception of transparent glass (§VII-A; Fig. 9).
- **Analyst inference—limited attribution:** The supplied experiments do not separately ablate MMO’s morphology and contact stages or point-cloud randomization. RL ablations remove information jointly from observations, rewards, and termination, so they cannot identify each mechanism’s individual contribution (Tables II–IV; Appendix D).
- **Metric and comparison limits:** Contact measurements use discretized geometry and operational distance thresholds, not physical contact certificates. Some baselines require added arm IK, and dynamic contact distances include failed executions, complicating interpretation as grasp fidelity alone (Appendices A, C).
- **Statistical limits:** Reported spreads primarily reflect variation across demonstrations or categories, not repeated training runs. Highest mean performance therefore does not imply a reliable advantage against every baseline (Appendices B-C, C-B).

## Open research questions
- **Can category-specific teachers support one general policy?** Unified control is not evaluated. Train a shared student on pooled demonstrations and compare category-wise success and held-out-category transfer against separate students (§VIII).
- **How robust is MMO to reconstruction error?** Inputs come from motion capture. Evaluate monocular-reconstructed trajectories with measured hand, object-pose, and mesh errors, tracking their effect through contact F1 and execution success (§VIII).
- **Which changes improve pose and shape generalization?** Failures cluster spatially and include geometry outside scale randomization. Compare expanded pose sampling and shape variation under equal demonstration and compute budgets (§VII-F; Appendix D).
- **Does hardware transfer hold across embodiments?** Only Sharpa is deployed physically. Run matched Dex3 and Allegro hardware trials using the same object set and task criteria (§VIII).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported setup and descriptive results; **medium** in broader generalization and causal attribution.
