# Depth-adaptive Inference of Looped Language Models via Continuous Depth Batching

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-28.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2608.09444) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09444)


## One-sentence takeaway
Continuous depth batching turns token-level early exits into measured throughput gains for looped language models, but the strongest results rely on replayed exits and a shared-cache configuration that severely degrades Ouro’s accuracy in a separate evaluation.

## Problem and motivation
Looped language models can save computation by assigning different recurrence depths to individual tokens. Standard token-level batching cannot directly accommodate these divergent execution paths, and reducing FLOPs does not necessarily reduce latency when weight loading dominates.

The authors identify an implementation gap: earlier continuous depth batching (CDB) work used simulations or evaluations excluding prefill, scheduling, and KV-cache updates, leaving its practical serving efficiency unresolved (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions
- **End-to-end CDB implementation:** Separate queues schedule prefill, prelude, recurrent core, and coda. Refill replaces exited tokens between loop steps; no-refill lets batches shrink. A minimum coda batch size balances boundary-stage efficiency against prompt replacement of exited tokens (§4.1).
- **Cache and asynchronous execution support:** Depth-aware caching supplies states missing after early exit. A lookahead gate predicts exits one recurrence ahead so CPU batch preparation overlaps GPU execution (§§4.2–4.3). These mechanisms are integrated with paged attention and CUDA graphs.
- **Efficiency analysis and architecture guidance:** A prefill-corrected FLOP speedup estimate and a fitted recurrent-step latency model explain when refill helps. Experiments and boundary-layer ablations identify costly non-recurrent stages as a constraint (§§5–6).

The authors claim the first end-to-end CDB implementation, rather than inventing the CDB concept itself (§3).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Ouro benefits most from refill | At selected thresholds across Alpaca, ShareGPT, and ArXiv, refill achieves **1.32–1.53× generated-token throughput** versus full-depth CB and **96–99% of the estimated end-to-end speedup**. Main experiments use a single 80 GB H100 and replayed exits. | §6.2; Fig. 5 |
| Huginn favors no-refill | In the corresponding workload evaluation, no-refill achieves **1.30–1.37× CB throughput**, or **83–96% of the estimated end-to-end speedup**. | §6.2; Fig. 5 |
| Scheduling overhead remains measurable but small | Without early exits, staged CDB retains **95–99% of full-depth CB throughput** in the reported evaluation. | §6.2 |
| Prefill limits available gains | Prefill accounts for **3.7–36% of GPU time** across evaluated workloads; its contribution is largest for long-context ArXiv requests. | §6.2 |
| Boundary-stage batching depends on architecture | On ShareGPT, immediate coda execution performs best for Ouro; Huginn favors a minimum coda batch of one-quarter of maximum decode width. Removing Huginn boundary layers makes immediate refill more competitive. | §6.4; Fig. 7 |
| Offline gains translate into higher serving capacity | With Poisson arrivals, CDB delays the sharp increase in end-to-end latency per output token. Ouro benefits most from refill; Huginn’s two modes reach similar capacity. | §6.3; Fig. 6; Appendix D |
| Cache sharing creates a major accuracy caveat | On GSM8K, flexible match with greedy decoding, Ouro at depth **4** falls from **78.09%** with a full depth-indexed cache to **0.23%** with single-slot sharing. Huginn at depth **16** scores **30.93%** and **32.52%**, respectively. Main performance experiments use shared caching. | Table 1; Appendix E.1; Table 4 |
| Lookahead gating approximately preserves the evaluated accuracy–depth trade-off | Separate GSM8K experiments report that Ouro’s distilled lookahead gate closely matches its teacher, while Huginn’s delayed convergence gate needs threshold recalibration without reported accuracy loss. | Appendix E.2; Fig. 14 |

The evidence supports efficient scheduling of recorded adaptive-depth workloads and architecture-dependent benefits from refill. It does **not** establish a universal wall-clock optimum: the quoted near-optimality is relative to an estimated, workload-dependent bound. Nor does it establish the headline speedups at matched accuracy under live generation, especially for Ouro’s shared-cache setup.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Main serving evaluation covers two architectures on one GPU. Multi-GPU execution, preemption, prefix caching, speculative decoding, and mixture-of-experts interactions remain unevaluated (§7).
- **Replay validity — analyst inference:** Benchmarks fix reference response lengths and replay previously recorded exits instead of using an active gate. This controls comparison variability but leaves live gate overhead and generation-dependent workload changes unvalidated in the headline measurements (Appendix C).
- **Accuracy–performance mismatch — analyst inference:** Ouro’s single-slot cache collapse means the main throughput configuration cannot be assumed to preserve useful accuracy. The paper explicitly reports an unresolved discrepancy with earlier cache-sharing results (Appendix E.1).
- **Exit-depth restriction:** Lookahead requires at least two loops for the learned gate and three for delayed convergence gating, potentially limiting shallower models (Appendix E.2).
- **Model approximation:** The authors acknowledge that fitted latency slopes mildly underestimate per-token costs, making saturation-batch estimates mildly high (Appendix B).

## Open research questions
1. **Do gains persist with live gates and matched accuracy?** Replay and cache sensitivity leave this unresolved. Run actual generation with active gates and accuracy-preserving cache layouts, reporting throughput and task accuracy together.
2. **Which architecture gives the best accuracy–latency trade-off?** Boundary-layer removal improves throughput, but that ablation does not establish preserved accuracy. Train matched-compute variants with different boundary/core splits and evaluate both outcomes.
3. **Can asynchronous scheduling support useful single-loop exits?** Current lookahead imposes minimum depths. Test a gate predicting only the first exit before entering the core, followed by ordinary lookahead (§7).
4. **How does CDB interact with production serving features?** Their effects are explicitly left open. Add prefix caching, preemption, and multi-GPU execution incrementally and measure throughput plus latency distributions.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported controlled scheduling findings; **medium** in their applicability to live, accuracy-preserving serving.

Material missing information includes a public implementation/release location, repeated-run uncertainty estimates, detailed lookahead-gate training specifications, exact tabulated online-capacity values, and a joint live-generation accuracy/throughput evaluation of the headline configurations.
