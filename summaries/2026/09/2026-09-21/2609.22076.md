# APort Vault: Benchmarking AI Agent Payment Authorization with the Open Agent Passport

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-21.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.22076) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22076)


## One-sentence takeaway
In a simulated payment benchmark, deterministic pre-action authorization reduced forbidden-recipient transfers from 105 to zero across 68,970 matched evaluations while preserving similar aggregate request rates, but this establishes enforcement of the configured policy rather than general agent safety (§§4.5–4.7).

## Problem and motivation
A payment agent can request an unauthorized transfer even when instructed to follow a policy. The author argues that refusal-based benchmarks inadequately measure whether an independent enforcement mechanism prevents that request from executing. The proposed evaluation therefore compares the same models and attacks with and without authorization at the tool boundary, measuring executed transfers rather than judged compliance (§§1–1.1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Human-attack replay benchmark:** Replay 4,371 CTF attacks across 14 models, five policy configurations, two replay tracks, and two architectures; 225,964 evaluations completed. Each attack belongs to one policy level, so this is not a full crossing of attacks with policies (§§3.1–3.9).
- **Controlled enforcement comparison:** Insert a local deterministic Open Agent Passport policy check before `transfer_funds` execution while holding the model, prompt, decoding settings, tool schema, and replay track fixed. The OAP specification itself comes from earlier work (§§2.4, 3.4, 3.10).
- **Execution-based measurement:** Separate requests, successful payments, authorization decisions, recipient membership, and forbidden-recipient transfers; use source-session clustering and matched comparisons (§§3.7–3.8, 4.1–4.7).
- **Failure-structure analysis and release:** Analyze session concentration, forged verification receipts, cross-model transfer, and judge reliability; report releasing evaluation rows, passports, scoring code, and analysis scripts, subject to substantial reproducibility qualifications (§§5, 8).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Enforcement prevented observed forbidden-recipient transfers | Levels 2–4: model alone **140/76,842** evaluations; behind layer **0/69,297**. Matched model–prompt–track comparison: **105 versus 0** across **68,970** pairs. | §§1.2, 4.4–4.5 |
| Zero has a finite uncertainty bound | Guarded Levels 2–4 cover **790 source sessions**; reported per-session rule-of-three upper bound **0.380%**. The **0.0043%** per-evaluation bound does not account for session dependence. | §4.4 |
| Payments continued behind the layer | **25,370 evaluations** contained successful payments; policy allowed **25,453/25,640 calls** and denied **187**, including **148** for forbidden recipients. | §4.8, Table 5 |
| Aggregate request behavior remained close | Matched Levels 2–4 request-rate difference: **+0.084 percentage points**, session-clustered 95% interval **[−0.020, +0.189]**; **1,220/68,970 pairs** disagreed. This was not an equivalence test. | §4.7 |
| Shared payment behavior was mostly authorized | Of **1,293 Level 4 single-turn prompts**, **809 (62.6%)** elicited requests and successful payments from every tested model, always to the allowlisted recipient. | §4.6 |
| Failures were concentrated | The **140** model-alone Levels 2–4 outcomes came from **24/790 sessions**; one session contributed **67 (47.9%)**. A forged-receipt cohort contributed **111/113** multi-turn Level 2 outcomes. | §§5.1–5.2 |
| Judge agreement was uneven | Across **222,400** jointly judged evaluations, pooled Cohen’s κ was **0.772**, falling to **0.167** at Level 3; reported deterministic-outcome recall was **99.1%** for Grok and **64.4%** for Mistral. | §4.10 |

The evidence supports the configured engine’s enforcement of recipient restrictions on this corpus without wholesale suppression of payment requests. It does not establish legitimate-task utility, requester entitlement, equivalent individual model behavior, or protection against incorrect or bypassed policies. Level 5 is a separate structural enforcement test: its policy denies all payments and its prompt explicitly instructs tool use (§§3.6, 4.8, 6.2).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** One simulated banking domain, one transfer tool, self-selected CTF participants, uneven attack coverage, and no benign-task evaluation limit generalization and prevent estimating legitimate-task overblocking (§§4.8, 7).
- **Author-stated experimental limits:** Single stochastic run per cell, unnormalized provider decoding defaults, incomplete multi-turn coverage, possible contamination, and absent planned repeat-sampling and human-validation studies (§7).
- **Policy mismatch:** The local Level 4 engine adds an audit-code requirement absent from the published pack. Its zero therefore does not directly validate that published configuration (§3.10).
- **Confounding:** Attacks occur at only one level, so differences across levels combine policy and attack-cohort effects (§4.2).
- **Analyst assessment—reproducibility inconsistencies:** Section 8 says judge verdicts, denial reasons, and other fields cannot be reconstructed publicly, yet later claims public reproducibility of judge agreement and all tables. It also reports missing successful-payment information for withheld GLM transcripts. These conflicting statements leave the reproducible scope unclear.
- **Author conflict:** The author founded the company developing the evaluated layer and conducted the benchmark; independent replication remains important (§10).

## Open research questions
1. **What legitimate-task utility does enforcement preserve?** Attack-only traffic cannot measure overblocking (§4.8). Next experiment: evaluate benign payment tasks with independently verified entitlement and measure completion and false denials.
2. **How robust is the result to implementation and policy errors?** The result assumes correct policy and an unavoidable check; Level 4 differs from the published pack (§§3.10, 6.2). Next experiment: compare both implementations and deliberately vary policy correctness and dispatch bypass opportunities.
3. **How stable are model and multi-turn findings?** Single runs and a concentrated forged-receipt cohort limit generality (§§5.2–5.3, 7). Next experiment: repeat matched cells across seeds and evaluate independently collected attack families.
4. **Can the complete measurement pipeline be independently reproduced?** Public-field omissions and contradictory release claims remain unresolved (§8). Next experiment: replicate deterministic scoring and judge auditing using a reconciled release manifest and the planned human-labeled slice.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied prose clearly supports the central matched comparison and its narrow interpretation, but independent verification is unavailable.

Material missing information includes most table bodies, complete executable harness and judge prompts, repeated-run variance, benign-task performance, human validation, and a consistent account of which released fields reproduce each result. The reported release and implementation parity were not independently checked.
