# Paint-Anything: Unified Any-Color Control for Image Generation and Editing

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-21.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.20816) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20816)


## One-sentence takeaway
Paint-Anything combines object-level hex-color supervision with high-noise pure-color training to improve generation and recoloring fidelity in a shared model, without establishing exact color reproduction across arbitrary scenes (§§3–4).

## Problem and motivation
Professional design needs explicit color specifications rather than broad labels such as “blue.” The authors identify a gap between numerical color control and existing methods that require dedicated representations, separate controllers, or specialized inference procedures. They aim to make hex-conditioned generation and editing ordinary prompt-following capabilities within one model (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Unified hex interface:** Prompts wrap colors in `<color>#HEX</color>` spans. Generation and editing share a rectified-flow objective; editing additionally receives clean source-image latents. Training updates the denoising transformer while freezing the text encoder and VAE (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Paint-500K construction:** VLM grounding, SAM3 segmentation, and CIELAB MeanShift clustering produce object-level color labels. The dataset contains 400K generation samples and 100K editing pairs; editing sources are synthesized while targets remain original images (§3.3; Appendix D).
- **Timestep-gated color anchors:** Auxiliary solid-color images provide exact hex-to-pixel supervision. The final recipe trains these only at noise levels \(t \in [0.8,1]\), while natural-image samples span the full timestep range (§3.2; Appendix D).
- **ACBench:** A new benchmark evaluates object-level hex fidelity using 1,000 generation prompts and 500 real-image editing prompts, complemented by existing CompColor and GenColorBench evaluations (§4.1; Appendices A–C).

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Strong same-backbone color gains | FLUX.2-4B ACBench-T2I Overall rises **37.02 → 68.58**, and Edit **58.90 → 75.57**, on the 0–100 scale; reported relative gains are **85.3%** and **28.3%**. | Table 1; §6 |
| Gains extend to another backbone | Z-Image Base ACBench-T2I Overall rises **33.45 → 53.77** under the same training recipe; editing is unreported for this backbone. | Table 1; §4.2 |
| Better compositional binding | FLUX.2-4B CompColor average rises **0.38 → 0.79** with hex prompts and **0.72 → 0.79** with named colors, on the 0–1 scale. Authors report the highest compared average in both settings. | Tables 1–2; §4.3 |
| Gating improves over ungated anchors | With wrapping and anchors enabled, changing \(t_\text{gate}\) from **0 to 0.8** raises ACBench-T2I **63.83 → 68.58**, Edit **70.39 → 75.57**, and hex CompColor **0.71 → 0.79**. | Table 3 |
| Independent numerical-color evaluation supports transfer | GenColorBench NCU average rises **34.00 → 57.89** versus the same FLUX.2-4B base. Published FLUX + NumColor scores average **51.90**; these were quoted, not rerun. NCU uses four images per RGB/HEX prompt and CIEDE2000-based scoring. | Appendix A, Table 4 |
| Human judgments favor color fidelity | Across **15 participants × 80 T2I prompts = 1,200 judgments**, Paint-Anything wins **55%**, ties **32%**, and loses **13%** against FLUX.2-4B. | Appendix K |
| Advantage survives evaluator changes | Alternative region-color estimators preserve the finetuned model’s advantage over base models; the SAM3 threshold/coverage sweep also preserves its advantage over FLUX.2-4B. | Appendix H, Tables 7–8 |

These results support improved object-level color fidelity and binding under the tested protocols. ACBench is a tolerant region-average metric: average absolute channel error of at most 16 receives full credit, while error of at least 64 receives zero (§4.1). High scores therefore do not establish exact pixel-level hex matching. Cross-system comparisons also mix backbones and interfaces; same-backbone comparisons provide stronger evidence for the training recipe.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated scope:** Training lacks palette-specific supervision (§5). The three-object diagnostic scores a designated target rather than joint correctness of all object-color assignments (Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference—appearance preservation remains incompletely measured:** ACBench primarily scores color inside object masks. The reported image-quality preference study explicitly does not establish quality equivalence, and no dedicated quantitative editing-preservation result is supplied (Appendices G, K).
- **Analyst inference—difficult materials are undercharacterized:** Data-grounding templates avoid objects whose transparency, reflections, shadows, or texture prevent reliable color measurement. Generalization to these conditions is not established by stratified evaluation (Appendix F).
- **Analyst inference—uncertainty and ablation scope:** ACBench-T2I uses one seed per prompt; repeated-training variability and confidence intervals are unreported. Full finetuning beats LoRA under shared settings, but the comparison does not establish performance under separately optimized LoRA settings (§§4.1, 4.4).
- **Analyst inference—reproducibility and separation:** Source collections are private, and release status is unspecified. The separation check establishes no exact normalized prompt overlap and disjoint editing source images, not comprehensive semantic or near-duplicate separation (Appendices D, L).
- **Reported counterpoint:** Paint-Anything has the highest localization-failure rate among audited models, indicating that stronger color scores do not imply uniformly better object rendering (Appendix H, Table 9).

## Open research questions
1. **Can accurate recoloring preserve material, texture, and surrounding content?** Color-centric metrics leave this partly unresolved. Next experiment: measure outside-mask changes and texture preservation alongside blinded editing-quality judgments.
2. **Does hex control generalize uniformly across unseen colors and difficult materials?** The paper lacks color-space holdout tests and material-stratified results. Next experiment: hold out color neighborhoods and evaluate separately on reflective, transparent, textured, and shadowed objects.
3. **Can the interface scale to palettes and joint multi-object correctness?** Palette supervision is absent, and the three-object test measures only a designated target. Next experiment: add palette supervision and score every requested binding jointly as object count increases.
4. **Why does high-noise anchoring help?** Two qualitative trajectories motivate the gate, while ablations establish performance rather than mechanism. Next experiment: compare equally weighted timestep windows across repeated runs and track color and structure throughout denoising.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported within-paper improvements; lower for exact-color guarantees and broad deployment generalization.

Material gaps include public code/checkpoint/dataset availability, repeated-run uncertainty, detailed source-collection composition, strict held-out-color results, quantitative editing-preservation evaluation, and the size and selection details of the large-color-shift editing subset.
