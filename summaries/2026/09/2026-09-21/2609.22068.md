# CodeMidas: Scaling Agentic Coding RL Environments from Code Itself

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-21.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.22068) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22068)


## One-sentence takeaway
CodeMidas converts implemented repository functionality into verified coding RL tasks, and training MiMo-V2.5 on its 5,545-task dataset improves all five tested external benchmarks, although individual filtering contributions remain unresolved (§§3–5).

## Problem and motivation
Coding-agent RL needs diverse tasks with trustworthy execution rewards. The authors argue that pipelines relying on issues, commits, documentation, or existing tests restrict task coverage to recorded development artifacts. CodeMidas instead uses implemented functionality to derive specifications, starting environments, and verifiers directly from source code (§1).

The central challenge is ensuring that tests enforce the stated behavior while accepting alternative correct implementations (§§1, 3.2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Source-derived environment construction:** Agents identify observable functionality, remove its implementation, and jointly refine the behavioral specification and remaining repository. The original implementation becomes a separate reference solution (§3.1).
- **Execution-grounded verification:** Agents construct tests from reference executions, remove assertions unsupported by the specification, and check that starting environments fail while reference implementations pass. Hidden verifiers are injected only during grading (§§3–3.3).
- **Rollout-based filtering:** Adversarial agents probe implementation leakage; reviewing agents assess disagreement between solution correctness and test verdicts; outcome filtering retains tasks with both successful and failed screening attempts (§3.4).
- **Dataset and training evidence:** The resulting dataset contains 5,545 tasks from 3,185 repositories across 23 languages and 15 technical domains. GRPO training and ablations evaluate transfer, task scale, combined filtering benefits, and behavioral changes (§§3.5–5). The methodological contribution is environment construction; GRPO itself is adopted.

## Evidence and main results
External results compare the initial MiMo-V2.5 policy with CodeMidas RL under identical reported evaluation settings (§4.1).

|---|---|---|

| Program construction improves | ProgramBench Almost Solved: 4.5% → 21.5%, +17.0 points; metric requires passing at least 95% of a task’s tests | Fig. 5; §4.1 |
| Translation and terminal work improve | RepoZero C2Rust pass rate: 40.5% → 51.8%, +11.3 points; Terminal-Bench v2.1: 63.7% → 72.2%, +8.5 points | Fig. 5 |

| Larger filtered pools perform better | For 1k / 3k / 5,545 tasks, DeepSWE pass rates are 17.57% / 19.05% / 21.70%; CodeMidas Val: 41.30% / 43.22% / 44.73%, with shared training configurations | §5.1; Fig. 8 |
| Combined cleaning and filtering help | Full filtered pool exceeds approximately 8k vanilla tasks by 0.59 / 4.59 / 4.49 percentage points on SWE-bench Pro / DeepSWE / CodeMidas Val | §5.1; Fig. 8 |
| Agent-written checks correlate with success | Within task and checkpoint on CodeMidas Val, rollouts with agent-written and executed checks have +4.2 percentage points mean pass rate; task-resampled 95% CI: 1.8–6.6 | §5.2 |

These results support transfer from the constructed tasks for the tested policy and settings. They do not establish superiority over other environment-generation pipelines under matched budgets, isolate individual filters, or show that behavioral changes cause the gains. Task-set disjointness is reported; repository-level separation is not specified (§4.1).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated qualification:** All-pass or all-fail screening outcomes cannot distinguish difficulty from verifier or specification defects (§3.4).
- **Analyst inference—filter attribution:** The vanilla comparison removes cleaning, execution checks, and all post-rollout filters together; individual benefits remain unidentified (§5.1).
- **Analyst inference—verifier uncertainty:** Correctness audits rely on reviewing agents. No independent human audit or residual false-positive/false-negative rate is reported (§3.4).
- **Analyst inference—generalization scope:** Only MiMo-V2.5 is trained, and the ten most frequent languages account for 98.2% of tasks. Cross-model and per-language robustness are unestablished (§4.1; Fig. 2).
- **Analyst inference—statistical and behavioral validity:** Main benchmark gains lack reported confidence intervals or repeated-training-seed results. Behavioral comparisons are observational, and measured subsets can vary across checkpoints (§5.2; Table 3).
- **Analyst inference—scaling cost:** Repeated construction, execution, and screening are required, but construction cost and end-to-end resource efficiency are not quantified (§3).

## Open research questions
- **Which filters matter most?** The ablation bundles them together. Run component-removal experiments with matched training budgets and independently audited verifier errors.
- **Does screening discard useful tasks?** Mixed-outcome filtering may remove legitimate easy or difficult tasks (§3.4). Audit discarded tasks and compare training with verified difficulty-stratified pools.
- **Does self-verification cause better outcomes?** The reported association is observational (§5.2). Randomize a verification intervention under matched interaction budgets.
- **How broadly does transfer hold?** Evidence covers one trained policy without language-specific outcomes. Repeat training across policies and evaluate repository-disjoint, language-stratified splits.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text clearly supports the reported pipeline and benchmark improvements, but causal attribution and reproducibility are less secure.

Material missing information includes construction/review model identities and prompts, exact outcome-screening budgets, repository sampling criteria, public asset availability, construction and training costs, detailed external evaluation budgets, main-result uncertainty, checkpoint-selection rules, and repository-level overlap checks.
