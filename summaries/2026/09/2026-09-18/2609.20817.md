# FAMOS: Feed-Forward 3D Articulation Modeling from Sparse Observations

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.20817) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20817)


## One-sentence takeaway
FAMOS jointly processes unordered partial point clouds to predict movable parts and joints, outperforming the evaluated baselines across three synthetic benchmarks while showing only qualitative evidence of real-world transfer (Tables 1–3; §4.4).

## Problem and motivation
Articulation modeling requires identifying movable parts and their motion parameters despite incomplete geometry. The authors argue that single-observation methods depend heavily on learned shape priors, while optimization and video methods require denser or more structured observations. FAMOS targets sparse, unordered captures with unknown part counts, motivated by manipulation and interactable digital replicas (§§1–2).

## Approach and contributions
- **Multi-observation prediction:** Frozen PartField features and geometric embeddings feed a query-based transformer alternating within-observation attention with global point-and-query attention. Shared queries predict consistent part segmentation, joint types, axes, and origins across observations (§3.2).
- **Observed articulation span supervision:** An auxiliary objective predicts the joint-value range across observations where a part is visible. The authors propose this to discourage reliance on a single observation; the span head is used only during training (§§3.3–3.4).
- **Procedural training assets:** A generator assembles annotated articulated objects from geometric primitives. Procedural pre-training followed by mixed procedural/curated training improves benchmark performance (§3.5; Table 5).

The output describes observed geometry and joints; the formulation assumes aligned point clouds and single-degree-of-freedom revolute or prismatic joints (§3.1).

## Evidence and main results

Segmentation F1 uses IoU ≥0.5. MAO additionally requires correct joint type, axis error <5°, and origin error <0.05 of object scale. Main benchmark predictions are evaluated on the first input state (§4.2).

| Better strict motion estimation | Same setting, MAO F1: **98.4 vs 90.3**, **64.5 vs 36.7**, and **90.0 vs 68.5**, respectively. On ACD, FAMOS matches **947** parts versus **572**, reducing concern that its errors reflect only easier detections. | Table 2 |

These results support improved performance under the paper’s synthetic partial-observation protocol. Particulate receives the same training protocol; optimization baselines receive denser inputs, with ArtGS also given the movable-part count (§4.2). Consequently, comparisons involve different input and reconstruction requirements. The evidence does not establish quantitative real-world reliability or image-to-articulation pipeline latency including upstream reconstruction.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Performance declines on substantially unfamiliar object classes, small movable parts, and severely corrupted upstream reconstructions. The method focuses on depth-one kinematic trees (§F).
- **Analyst inference—restricted coverage:** Synthetic viewpoints come from an elevated front-facing region. The ArtiCraft subset excludes objects with parts invisible from the front and contains mostly in-domain classes, limiting claims about unrestricted viewpoints and category generalization (§A).
- **Analyst inference—mechanism attribution:** Component ablations use curated data only. They support those components in that setting but do not establish their individual contributions under full procedural training (Table 4).
- **Analyst inference—training-budget confound:** Table 5 adds a procedural pre-training stage without reporting an equally long curated-only control, so its pre-training comparison does not isolate data diversity from additional optimization.
- **Analyst inference—selection and uncertainty:** Span errors condition on successful matching and joint-type prediction; they do not measure unconditional success. Repeated-run variation and confidence intervals are not reported (§E; Tables 1–5).

## Open research questions
1. **How robust is the complete real-image pipeline?** Real-world evidence is qualitative and reconstruction corruption causes failures. Next experiment: annotate real captures and compare predicted versus reference geometry inputs under controlled reconstruction errors (§4.4; §F).
2. **Can the method handle deeper articulation hierarchies and small parts?** Both remain explicit scope limitations. Next experiment: generate deeper kinematic trees and systematically vary part size and visibility (§F).
3. **How much do additional states help through motion versus visibility?** Adding observations changes both factors. Next experiment: hold viewpoints fixed while varying articulation span, then hold articulation fixed while varying viewpoints (§4.5; Figure 7).
4. **Do the proposed components retain their gains under matched training budgets?** Existing component and data ablations do not jointly resolve this. Next experiment: factorial attention/span/data ablations with equal total training steps (Tables 4–5).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the reported method and benchmark comparisons; real-world applicability remains weakly quantified.

Material missing information includes quantitative real-world results, repeated-run uncertainty, compute-matched pre-training controls, full-training component ablations, upstream image-processing latency, and confirmed availability of code, weights, generator, and exact custom test-split identifiers.
