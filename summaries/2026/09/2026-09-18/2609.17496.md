# Verifiable Social Reasoning for LLM Assistants

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.17496) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17496)


## One-sentence takeaway
Fuse evaluates motive inference from simulated users’ subjective accounts, finding that user mediation and biased framing impair LLM performance even when human majority judgments recover the intended motive (§§3–4).

## Problem and motivation
Assistants giving everyday social advice must infer others’ intentions, beliefs, or emotions from incomplete, subjective reports. The authors identify two evaluation gaps: existing social-reasoning tasks often expose the full situation, while realistic consultations lack independently verifiable motives against which predictions can be scored (§1).

## Approach and contributions
- **Controlled evaluation framework:** Fuse assigns a hidden motive to a target persona, simulates interactions using Concordia, and lets a separate user agent recount events to the evaluated assistant. The assigned motive supplies ground truth; human judgments check whether behavior actually expresses it (§2).
- **Reusable benchmark:** The authors report releasing code, scenario templates, and 21,600 initial user messages derived from 1,200 simulations across 30 scenarios. Coverage includes desire, intention, belief, emotion, and knowledge, with two opposing motives per scenario (§3.1; Appendices A, D).
- **Diagnostic comparisons:** Experiments vary direct observation versus user mediation, reporting bias, narrative detail, and conversation length. Predictions receive correct, incorrect, or abstention labels from an LLM judge (§§3–4).
- **Abstention-aware scoring:** Mediated Social Reasoning (MSR) assigns credit to correct answers and partial credit to abstentions. Reported results use abstention credit \(d=0.75\), alongside separate outcome rates (§3.2).

## Evidence and main results
MSR values below use the paper’s percentage-point scale and \(d=0.75\).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Assigned motives are usually recognizable in raw simulations | Human majority predictions matched ground truth in approximately 97% of 300 transcripts, each rated by 10 people. | §3.3; Appendix B |
| Models trail the human majority benchmark | Best model MSR: 83.7, from Gemini 3.7 Flash; human majority MSR: 89.8, with approximately 88% correct. Human evaluation used 2,100 first messages with 10 raters each; the model benchmark contains 21,600 messages. | §§3.1, 3.3–4; Fig. 4 |
| User mediation adds difficulty | All evaluated models produced fewer correct predictions and higher error rates than when given raw events; some also abstained more. | §4.1; Figs. 5, 16 |
| Opposing user beliefs reduce performance | Default-to-biased MSR drops ranged from 6.9–12.5 points among the eight models outside the high-abstention Gemma/Claude group, versus 3.6 points for human majority judgments. | §4.2; Fig. 6 |
| More detail helps models disproportionately | Low-to-high detail increased mean model MSR from 74.2 to 80.1, versus 88.3 to 91.1 for humans; the average gap narrowed from 14.1 to 11.0 points. | §4.3; Fig. 8 |
| Longer dialogue gives uneven gains | Starting from low-detail messages, gains were strongest between turns 2 and 4, then mostly plateaued or reversed through turn 8; no model reached the first-response human baseline. | §4.4; Fig. 10 |

These results establish diagnostic performance differences within Fuse’s synthetic setting. Human agreement supports the recognizability of assigned motives, but does not establish realism across everyday consultations. The human baseline aggregates multiple raters and is intended as a solvability probe, not an estimate of typical individual human performance (§3.3). The experiments do not directly measure advice quality or downstream relationship outcomes.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated simulator confounding:** The evaluation does not empirically separate simulator artifacts from intrinsic difficulties of user mediation (§5.3).
- **Deliberately restricted scope:** Scenarios use strongly contrasting, primarily single motives; text-only evaluation excludes percepts and non-literal communication. Consultation occurs after the simulated events rather than interleaving with them (§2; Appendix A.1).
- **Scoring depends on policy choices:** Abstention credit is selected rather than empirically optimized, and high abstention compresses measured bias gaps. Open-ended responses and implicit leanings remain outside the evaluation (§3.2; §4.2; Appendix C).
- **Analyst inference—imperfect causal isolation:** Bias conditions preserve underlying events, but generate different retellings. Consequently, performance differences need not reflect framing alone; the human comparison helps assess this concern without eliminating it (§4.2).
- **Analyst inference—judge reliability remains uncertain:** The same model drives simulation and judging, and the supplied text reports no human validation of the judge’s response classifications (§3.2).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** §3.2 says “seven” closed-weight models but lists six, consistent with the overall total of 12. Full generation prompts and implementation details are deferred to the reported code release.

## Open research questions
- **How much of the mediation penalty transfers to human users?** Simulator effects remain unresolved (§5.3). Next experiment: compare human and simulated retellings of the same controlled interactions.
- **Can targeted clarification improve later-turn reasoning?** Dialogue gains plateau and some correct predictions reverse (§4.4). Next experiment: compare standard dialogue with a structured clarification policy under equal turn budgets.
- **How should assistants handle genuinely ambiguous motives?** Fuse deliberately selects contrasting endpoints and fixes abstention credit (§3.2; Appendix A.1). Next experiment: introduce mixed motives and compare calibration and abstention across scoring costs.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in the summary of reported methods and results; **medium** in their interpretation beyond this benchmark.
