# Srijika: OpenType-Layout-Reusing Font Restyling for Nine Indic Scripts

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-18.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.05661) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05661)


## One-sentence takeaway
Srijika produces installable fonts across nine Indic scripts by combining template-layout reuse, retrieval-conditioned diffusion, and template fallback, but style gains are usually small on its internal metric and lack independent validation.

## Problem and motivation
Indic fonts require complete sets of conjuncts, half forms, and vowel-mark variants connected through substitution and positioning rules; generating isolated glyph images does not produce a usable font. The author identifies scarce, genre-skewed font data and incomplete font assembly as barriers to expanding these typographic ecosystems (§§1–3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Layout-reusing font construction:** Replace outlines throughout a template’s glyph closure while preserving cmap and GSUB data. Reuse GPOS structure with explicit advance-width and anchor-translation policies (§4.1).
- **Retrieval-conditioned restyling:** Lipika maps language prompts to donor fonts; six donor glyph tiles condition a latent diffusion model. Content filtering, weight harmonization, baseline alignment, and shaped-cluster repair can revert glyphs to template outlines (§§4.2–4.4).
- **Script expansion and serving:** Warm-start grafting adds scripts; routing each script to a previously passing checkpoint avoids regressions at the cost of retaining multiple models (§5).
- **Evaluation and failure analysis:** A frozen ID/style-OOD benchmark, structural audits, and unsuccessful conditioning/objective experiments document the method’s limits (§§6–8). These are system and evaluation contributions; superiority over learned font generators is not established.

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Structural font production succeeds on reported checks | All 66 TTFs pass OpenType Sanitizer; HarfBuzz and CoreText match template glyph-ID sequences on three probe strings per script. | §7, pp. 14–15 |
| Copying usually wins reconstruction | With six disjoint reference tiles and guidance 3, template copy exceeds generation’s SSIM on 50/56 held-out faces and every script’s pooled score. | Table 3; §7; Tables 7–8 |
| Typical internal style movement is small | Across 23 held-out families, generation improves Lipika similarity over the template on 18; median template-to-target gap closure is 0.047, versus 0.006 for reference-only geometry. Geometry has higher SSIM on 17/23 families. | Table 6 |
| OOD transfer is family-specific | A single controlled checkpoint yields Alkatra closure of 0.935 in Bengali and 0.853 in Odia, with SSIM below template copy; the other seven unique OOD families have median closure 0.040. Alkatra’s two script results are correlated observations. | Table 5; §7 |
| Prompt-to-font examples pass the internal cluster critic | Nine retrieval-driven showcase fonts score 0.998–1.000 shaped-cluster accuracy and 0.82–1.00 legibility, taking 12–136 minutes per font on one L4. | §7, p. 13 |
| Fallback and metric changes are substantial | Full audit covers 80,915 glyphs: 50,066 template fallbacks and 30,849 accepted generated glyphs. Accepted-glyph advance increases have p95 0.358 em and maximum 1.93 em. | §7, pp. 14–15 |
| Targeted rendered auditing remains incomplete | Of 1,617 glyphs with large metric changes, 248 are reachable in the audit inventory; four introduced overlap cases were reverted. The remaining 1,369 feature-context variants lack this rendered audit. | §7, p. 15 |
| Added conditioning did not solve heavy-donor failures | Reviving skeleton conditioning raises its channel norm 17-fold, but Devanagari SSIM remains below the 0.7445 gate and AlfaSlab fallback rises from baseline 54% to 67%. | Figure 5; Table 4 |

The evidence supports template-based artifact construction and bounded internal style movement. It does not establish generally superior reconstruction, independently verified stylistic improvement, comprehensive positioning correctness, or human-rated prompt satisfaction. Lipika gap closure measures movement in the generator’s own style-encoder space, whose training included the evaluated families (§7; Appendix B).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Evaluation dependence:** All comparisons use no-learning baselines. There is no independent style metric or controlled human study; oracle-reference gates omit prompt retrieval (§9).
- **Limited generalization evidence:** Operational gates contain only 2–4 independent families per script. The controlled benchmark uses one training run; repeated seeds vary inference only. OOD selection was retrospective (§§6–7).
- **Visible inconsistency:** Template fallbacks can mix lighter and heavier glyphs within a font. Layout-scale styles transfer weakly, and per-preset coherence statistics are absent (§9; Figure 4).
- **Incomplete rendering assurance:** Glyph-ID parity and sanitizer acceptance do not establish anchor-to-ink quality or exhaustive collision behavior; most metric-tail variants remain unaudited in rendered context (§7).
- **Hypothesis rather than cause:** The proposed ink-density boundary rests on a scalar descriptor and two Latin donors; capacity, resolution, critic calibration, and cross-script transfer remain alternative explanations (§8.3).
- **Reproducibility:** Generator, serving and evaluation implementations, and generated font files are unavailable in this version; manifests and hashes do not replace executable artifacts (pp. 20–21).
- **Analyst inference:** High post-repair cluster scores cannot alone quantify the learned generator’s contribution because repair can substitute professionally drawn template glyphs (§4.4; §7).

## Open research questions
1. **Do style gains survive independent evaluation?** Shared encoder exposure leaves this unresolved. Retrain Lipika excluding benchmark families, then compare generation, geometry, and template copy using an independent embedding and blinded reader judgments.
2. **What actually causes heavy-style failure?** The density-boundary evidence does not isolate causality. Test multiple native-script and Latin donors across density bands, with controlled additions of heavy-style training targets (§8.3).
3. **Can one model add scripts without regressions?** Routing avoids updates rather than resolving dilution. Compare shared-backbone adapters or increased capacity under matched training budgets and fixed script gates (§§5.3, 9).
4. **Are repaired fonts coherent and correctly positioned across feature contexts?** Existing audits leave context-dependent variants and coherence unresolved. Expand shaping inventories to reach those variants and measure collisions, attachments, and within-font stroke dispersion (§§7, 9).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: high** in this account of the reported evidence; broader perceptual quality and generalization remain uncertain.

Material missing information includes independent style validation, human preference results, learned-baseline comparisons, training-seed replication, exhaustive positioning audits, per-preset coherence measurements, and the unreleased generator, evaluation code, and font artifacts.
