# IterSynth: Rethinking Deep Search Agents via Role-Decoupled Iterative Synthesis

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.29444) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29444)


## One-sentence takeaway
IterSynth combines shared-model planning and synthesis with role-specific reinforcement learning, reporting a 50.7% five-benchmark average versus 46.5% for the strongest listed ≤8B baseline, although test-time resampling contributes to headline results (Table 1; Appendix J).

## Problem and motivation
Long-horizon search agents must plan queries, evaluate evidence, and produce grounded answers while managing expanding search histories. The authors identify two interacting problems: coupling these responsibilities in one undifferentiated policy and accumulating context that obscures useful evidence. They argue that existing multi-agent and summarization workflows address these problems separately rather than jointly (§§1–2).

Their motivating experiment finds substantial context exhaustion for ReAct under a 64K-token limit, particularly on a sampled BrowseComp subset (Appendix H).

## Approach and contributions
- **Shared-policy, dual-role workflow:** A Planner searches or answers using the question and current summary. A Synthesizer integrates retrieved evidence into that summary. Both use the same parameters but different prompts and action constraints; previous history is replaced by the reconstructed workspace (§3.1).
- **Role-Decoupled Policy Optimization (RDPO):** After supervised fine-tuning, training combines terminal correctness with intermediate rubric rewards, normalizing advantages separately across Planner and Synthesizer turns for each query (§3.2).
- **Training and evaluation recipe:** Qwen3-8B learns from approximately 10,000 teacher-generated trajectories, then undergoes RDPO on approximately 1,000 selected questions. Experiments examine trained agents, prompting-only transfer, reward design, parameter sharing, and failure modes (Appendices B–J).

The proposed contribution is the combination of role separation, persistent summary updates, and role-specific optimization—not a new benchmark.

## Evidence and main results
| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Highest listed small-agent average | Across five benchmarks, IterSynth-8B scores **50.7%**, versus **46.5%** for MiroThinker-v1.0-8B: **+4.2 percentage points**. | Table 1 |
| Gains vary substantially by benchmark | Against MiroThinker-v1.0-8B, BrowseComp-ZH is **55.4% vs. 40.2%**, but GAIA text-only is **55.3% vs. 66.4%**. | Table 1 |
| Role-specific normalization improves this training setup | Five-benchmark averages: SFT **44.1%**, outcome-only GRPO **48.9%**, mixed-role composite-reward normalization **47.2%**, RDPO **50.7%**. RDPO trails GRPO on xBench-2510: **46.0% vs. 49.0%**. | Table 3 |
| Prompting-only transfer improves average scores | Across four benchmarks, Claude-4.5-Opus averages **66.1%** with IterSynth versus **60.6%** with ReAct; DeepSeek-V3.1 averages **47.9% vs. 43.4%**, without parameter updates. | Table 2 |
| Matched teacher and question pool support a workflow benefit | Five-benchmark SFT averages are **44.1%** for IterSynth versus **32.6%** for ReAct, using the same teacher, underlying questions, and data scale. | Appendix E, Table 8 |
| Resampling materially contributes to reported performance | One retry of an answer judged “not attempted” raises xBench-2510 accuracy **37.0%→46.0%**, BrowseComp **25.7%→30.9%**, and BrowseComp-ZH **49.1%→55.4%**; these endpoints match Table 1. | Appendix J, Table 15 |
| ReAct context exhaustion is substantial in the measured setting | On 100 sampled BrowseComp questions with a 64K context limit, non-completion is **59%** for DeepSeek-V3.1 and **65%** for Claude-Sonnet-4.6. | Appendix H, Figure 4 |

These results support improved benchmark performance from the combined workflow and training recipe, with useful within-framework ablations. They do not establish universal superiority, statistically reliable gains, or equivalent compute and retry budgets across all baselines. The prompting comparison also has exceptions: IterResearch outperforms IterSynth on Claude’s GAIA evaluation (Table 2).

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-observed memory failures:** Summaries can omit relationships between retrieved facts or preserve incorrect evidence that biases later searches. These are qualitative findings without reported prevalence (Appendix F.4).
- **Analyst inference—attribution limits:** Replacing a trained role with the base model tests the combined training effect, not RDPO independently of SFT. The matched ReAct comparison changes both role structure and context management, leaving their separate contributions unresolved (Tables 4 and 8).
- **Analyst inference—comparison ambiguity:** Table 1 averages sometimes cover different benchmark subsets. Appendix J does not fully specify the “not attempted” detector or whether baselines receive equivalent retries.
- **Analyst inference—retrieval contamination risk:** The case study retrieves a research-paper snippet echoing the benchmark question. This warrants a leakage audit but does not demonstrate that the answer was leaked (Appendix K, p. 24).
- **Internal inconsistencies:** Table 9 describes an equal total parameter budget while comparing one shared 8B model with two independent 8B models. Appendix E describes identical outcome-only RL rewards while reporting IterSynth-RDPO, whose defined reward includes rubrics. The claimed xBench-2510 gain in §4.2 also disagrees with Table 1.
- **Incomplete reproducibility:** Exact role prompts, consolidated rubrics, reward-score normalization, and evaluation judging details are unavailable. The promised artifacts’ release status is not established by the supplied text.

## Open research questions
- **Which workflow component drives the gain?** Role separation and workspace reconstruction change together. A matched factorial experiment could vary each independently under fixed training data and inference budgets.
- **Can structured memory reduce synthesis failures?** Appendix F.4 identifies lost relationships and incorrect consolidation. Compare free-text summaries with provenance-linked relational memory and contradiction-triggered re-search.
- **How much advantage remains under equal retry budgets?** Appendix J shows material resampling gains. Evaluate all workflows with the same abstention detector, retry allowance, and total token/tool budget.
- **Does performance survive retrieval decontamination?** Benchmark-level training separation does not resolve live-web leakage. Repeat evaluation after filtering benchmark reproductions and answer-bearing research pages.

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The supplied text supports the method description and reported comparisons, but causal and reproducibility claims are weaker.

Material missing information includes uncertainty estimates and repeated-seed results, complete prompts and rubrics, detailed answer/resampling adjudication, matched baseline tool and compute settings, retrieval snapshot details, and a live-web contamination audit.
