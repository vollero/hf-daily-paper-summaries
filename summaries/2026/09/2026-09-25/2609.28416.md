# Agent-Editing World Model: Rethinking World Modeling for LLM Agents

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.28416) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28416)


## One-sentence takeaway

AEWM judges and selectively replaces an agent’s proposed reasoning and action before real execution, with reported improvements across six benchmarks and transferable gains from fine-tuning on edited trajectories (§§2–4).

## Problem and motivation

The authors identify **task-state contamination**: unsupported assumptions, outdated plans, and premature success judgments persist in agent histories and misdirect subsequent actions. They argue that predicting execution-dependent tool responses offers limited benefit when real tools already provide feedback; the missing capability is helping agents interpret that feedback and choose productive continuations (§1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Decision judgment and editing:** Action Judge classifies proposed decisions as critical, exploratory, or noisy. State Revision replaces both reasoning and action for noisy proposals. EditAct executes the selected action and appends its actual observation; it preserves prior history rather than rewriting it (§§2.2–2.3).
- **Training and evaluation assets:** A unified Qwen3.5-35B-A3B model receives mid-training on 52.16B tokens and SFT on 120,000 examples, evenly split between judgment and revision. The authors construct a 3,000-decision Action Judge benchmark (§3; Tables 5–6, 10).
- **Transfer into agents:** AEWM-RFT fine-tunes agents on verified EditAct trajectories, including revisions, enabling inference without AEWM. Domain-specific agents are trained separately; Self-RFT comparisons match trajectory counts, filtering, and fine-tuning configurations (Appendix A.4; Tables 8–9).

The distinctive proposal is the combination of prospective decision-effect classification, execution-verified joint revision, and transfer through edited trajectories; broader novelty beyond the supplied comparisons is not independently established.

## Evidence and main results

All results below are author-reported. Task evaluations average three independent runs (§4.1).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Stronger action classification | On the pooled 3,000-decision benchmark, AEWM achieves **70.5% macro-F1**, versus **59.9%** for strongest baseline DeepSeek-V4-Pro: **+10.6 percentage points**. | Table 11; Appendix B.2 |
| Consistent task improvements | Across six benchmarks, mean scores are **41.8 vs. 35.1** for Qwen3.5-4B, **44.1 vs. 38.9** for 9B, and **48.8 vs. 45.6** for 35B-A3B, comparing EditAct with each backbone’s strongest baseline average. These averages mix accuracy, F1, resolved rate, and mean test pass rate. | Table 1; §4.1 |
| Gains on designated out-of-distribution tasks | With Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, EditAct versus trajectory-level Best@3 achieves **78.6 vs. 74.3 F1** on DeepSearchQA and **43.8% vs. 43.5% resolved rate** on SWE-Bench Pro. | Table 1 |
| Transfer without online guidance | For separately fine-tuned Qwen3.5-35B-A3B agents using ReAct, AEWM-RFT versus Self-RFT scores **45.4% vs. 43.2% BrowseComp accuracy**, **43.4% vs. 40.8% Terminal-Bench 2.0 task accuracy**, and **48.6% vs. 46.1% Doc2Repo mean test pass rate**. | Table 2; Appendix A.4 |
| Learned selection and joint revision help | Full AEWM exceeds random gating, agent resampling, reasoning hints, either single-component revision, and substitute world models on BrowseComp, Terminal-Bench 2.0, and Doc2Repo with the fixed 35B-A3B agent. | Table 3; Appendix C.3 |
| Fewer turns are not universal | On Doc2Repo, AEWM-RFT raises mean test pass rate from **46.1% to 48.6%** over Self-RFT while average turns rise from **76.3 to 89.8**. | Table 2 |

These results support the effectiveness of the complete intervention system and offline trajectory transfer in the tested settings. They do not establish compute-normalized superiority, universal generalization, or that repairing “task-state contamination” uniquely explains the improvements. No direct observation-prediction world-model baseline appears in the supplied experiments.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated boundaries:** Appendix A.1 explicitly says each judgment label describes one observed continuation, not an action’s value across all possible futures. Section 4.3 reports limited Terminal and SWE gains for the stronger Qwen3.5-Plus agent, attributing this tentatively to editor capacity.
- **Analyst inference—label validity:** Judgment supervision and benchmark labels rely heavily on model annotation and successful/high-scoring trajectories. Model review and consistency filtering help, but human agreement and performance on failed-trajectory decisions are not established (Appendices A–B).
- **Analyst inference—causal evidence:** Revision examples execute only the replacement, so local superiority over the original action is not measured through paired executions. Selected successful cases illustrate mechanisms rather than estimate their prevalence (§5.2; Appendices D, E.4).
- **Analyst inference—comparison precision:** Three-run means are reported without accompanying uncertainty estimates in the supplied results. Token costs, latency, and matched-compute comparisons are unavailable; small gains therefore need cautious interpretation (§4; Appendix C).
- **Coverage and reproducibility:** The source is truncated, including part of Appendix E. Search training uses internal data, and usable release links or availability are not established.

## Open research questions

- **Does editing beat alternatives at equal cost?** Reported comparisons do not establish matched inference expenditure. Next experiment: compare EditAct, Best@3, and ReAct under equal token, latency, and monetary budgets.
- **Do hindsight labels predict expected action value?** Labels reflect single realized continuations. Next experiment: branch multiple rollouts from identical pre-action states and compare Action Judge predictions with distributions of downstream outcomes.
- **When does the editor harm capable agents?** Stronger-agent gains are limited in some domains, and harmful-intervention rates are unavailable. Next experiment: vary editor strength and intervention thresholds while measuring beneficial and harmful edits separately.

## Confidence and missing information

**Confidence: medium.** The supplied methods, tables, and ablations support the reported findings, but coverage is incomplete because the source was truncated.

Material missing information includes uncertainty estimates, inference and training costs, human label validation, explicit training–evaluation overlap audits, systematic harmful-edit analysis, complete omitted prompts, and confirmed access to models, code, and datasets.
