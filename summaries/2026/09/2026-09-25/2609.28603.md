# Learning to Discover Interesting Mathematics

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-25.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.28603) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28603)


## One-sentence takeaway
Optimizing a proof-length-to-description-length metric produces Lean-verified theorems with higher measured interestingness and less judged mathlib overlap, but their broader novelty and downstream usefulness remain unproven.

## Problem and motivation
Autonomous mathematical discovery requires choosing worthwhile conjectures, not just proving supplied targets. The authors identify a missing quantitative signal for distinguishing useful mathematical developments from collections of valid but trivial statements. They target structural value measurable within formal libraries, explicitly excluding historical, social, and real-world sources of mathematical interest (§§1, 3).

## Approach and contributions
- **Conditional proof-difficulty prediction:** Expand mathlib dependencies to construct premise-conditioned proof-line labels, then train Qwen3.6-27B with GRPO. The reward combines label accuracy, compositional consistency, and monotonicity under premise removal (§2; Appendix B).
- **Interestingness and utility metrics:** Define interestingness as \(I(T\mid P)=100V(T\mid P)/L(T\mid P)\), where \(L\) includes the statement and definitions unavailable through the premises. Define utility through downstream proof savings, and measure its association with interestingness (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Conjecturer training:** Use the frozen difficulty predictor to reward concise, apparently difficult conjectures, with compilation, relevance, and triviality checks. Evaluate using accepted Lean proofs rather than predicted difficulty (§3.3; Appendix C).
- **Recursive discovery:** Generate, filter, prove, and promote theorems into an expanding premise library; compare interestingness-based promotion against random selection, proof length, and no pruning (§3.4; Appendix D).

The contribution is a library-grounded objective and its integration into training and discovery; the paper acknowledges earlier mathematical interestingness and compression proposals (Appendix A).

These results support improvement on the paper’s operational metric and on its LLM-based assessments. Lean verification establishes correctness of accepted final statements. It does not establish minimal proof length, novelty beyond mathlib, human mathematical interest, or future usefulness.

## Limitations and validity risks
- **Author-stated:** Source-line counts depend on proof style and automation; the metric captures only part of mathematical value. New definitions are excluded, and usefulness of generated theorems for future results remains unresolved (§4).
- **Analyst inference—correlation is not independent validation:** Interestingness and utility share the same proof-cost factor: \(I_0=100V/L\) and \(U_0=|D|V\). Their correlation therefore cannot alone establish that interestingness predicts downstream reuse (§§3.1–3.2).
- **Analyst inference—selection and repair:** Evaluation samples until each verified cohort is filled and permits constrained statement repairs. Without proposal counts, proof-success rates, or repair frequencies, the results do not measure unconditional generation quality or efficiency (Appendix C.2.1).
- **Analyst inference—judging limits:** Claude supplies proofs and containment/quality judgments. Blinding helps, but no independent human validation is reported; containment evidence is explicitly non-exhaustive (Appendices C.2, D).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** §3.4 and Figure 7 describe six rounds, whereas Appendix D reports ten. Appendix D’s scoring denominator uses normalized statement characters, while Equation 6 also charges for unavailable definitions; equivalence is not explained.
- **Missing ablations:** The supplied text does not isolate the difficulty reward’s individual components or report prediction performance on newly generated theorem distributions.

## Open research questions
- **Does the metric predict future reuse beyond its shared proof-cost factor?** Existing evidence is retrospective and algebraically coupled. Prospectively compare downstream proof savings from selected theorems against controls matched on proof cost and description length.
- **Do gains survive proof compression?** Accepted source length is not minimal proof length. Reprove the same statements with alternative provers and stronger automation, then recompute rankings.
- **Does training improve useful output per unit compute?** Verified-cohort sampling hides failed proposals and repair costs. Run a fixed-budget evaluation reporting exact proof success, repairs, compute, and accepted interestingness.
- **Can recursive discovery remain useful over longer horizons?** Long-running diversity and usefulness are explicitly unresolved. Extend multi-seed discovery runs across domains and measure independent theorem reuse and redundancy (§4).

## Confidence and missing information
**Confidence: medium.** The methods and reported comparisons are detailed, but broader discovery claims exceed the demonstrated evidence.

Material missing information includes proposal denominators, repair rates, compute costs, independent human judgments, prospective utility measurements, downloadable model/data/code availability, and reconciliation of the round-count and scoring-definition discrepancies.
