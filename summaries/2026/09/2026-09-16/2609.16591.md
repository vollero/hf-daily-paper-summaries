# FLAT: Resampling Image and Text into 1D Flexible-Length Aligned Transmodal Tokens for Retrieval and Generation

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.16591) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16591)


## One-sentence takeaway

FLAT jointly learns variable-length continuous image–text representations for retrieval, captioning, and image generation, with longer prefixes improving generative detail while retrieval remains effective with a single 64-dimensional token.

## Problem and motivation

Multimodal systems commonly train representation encoders separately from generative models, leaving generation dependent on frozen embeddings and requiring later realignment. The authors argue that representations should simultaneously support semantic discrimination and generation in both image-to-text (I2T) and text-to-image (T2I) directions (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

The identified gap is a unified, continuously aligned representation that supports retrieval and bidirectional generation while allowing its sequence length—and therefore compute and information capacity—to vary at inference time.

## Approach and contributions

- **Shared transmodal representation:** A pretrained vision-language model encodes either images or text through shared register tokens and a shared projection, producing up to 256 continuous tokens of 64 dimensions each (Section 3.1; Table 6).
- **Flexible-length training:** Nested dropout samples \(K\in\{1,4,16,64,256\}\) and retains only the first \(K\) tokens. The resulting model also produces coherent outputs at unseen intermediate lengths (Section 3.1; Figure 17).
- **Joint objectives:** The encoder is trained with bidirectional image–text contrastive alignment, autoregressive caption generation, and rectified-flow image generation (Section 3.2, Equations 3–9).
- **One representation for multiple uses:** The same token sequence directly supports retrieval, T2I synthesis, I2T captioning, interpolation, and latent arithmetic (Sections 4.2–4.4).
- **Compute–capacity control:** Prefix truncation reduces encoder and caption-decoder computation substantially, although savings are smaller for image generation (Appendix F.2, Table 12).

Together, these results show that the representation supports several tasks and exposes a useful capacity–compute tradeoff. Controlled task-only comparisons support a pretraining benefit primarily for generation and captioning. They do not establish universal state of the art: some listed captioning baselines score higher, retrieval pretraining gains are small and direction-dependent, and several geometry claims rely on qualitative examples or diagnostics rather than broad controlled benchmarks.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-demonstrated failures:** Generated images can miss typography or fine layout; captions can omit secondary objects and actions; composed retrieval struggles with count edits while preserving category identity (Figures 16 and 23).
- **Retrieval pretraining benefit is mixed:** Against matched task-only adaptation, FLAT improves COCO I2T R@1 by 0.6–1.3 points but is 0.4–0.9 points worse for T2I R@1 (Appendix D, Table 11).
- **Zero-shot retrieval worsens with longer prefixes:** On COCO, pretrained I2T R@1 drops from 44.90 at \(K=1\) to 35.48 at \(K=256\); task adaptation largely removes this sensitivity (Appendix C, Table 10).
- **Caption metrics partly measure style alignment:** The authors report that zero-shot outputs are longer and more editorial than COCO references, so fine-tuning gains partly reflect matching annotation style (Appendix C, Figure 11).
- **Null-padding comparison is not fully controlled:** The compared runs use different prefix-sampling and learning-rate recipes; the authors therefore call the instability result qualitative evidence (Appendix I).
- **Analyst inference — limited generalization evidence:** Multilingual and video transfer are illustrated qualitatively, without quantitative benchmarks in the supplied text (Appendix G.1–G.2).
- **Analyst inference — compute accessibility:** Pretraining uses 64 H200 GPUs for 135K steps, which may impede independent replication (Table 6).
- **Reporting ambiguity:** Appendix A says 65.7M training pairs, while Table 6 reports 67M and gives an approximate composition totaling 67.1M; the exact corpus size is unclear.

## Open research questions

1. **Can longer prefixes improve retrieval without harming zero-shot performance?** Zero-shot recall declines as \(K\) grows, although fine-tuning removes most of the gap (Table 10 versus Table 3). A next experiment could regularize token-wise alignment or compare pooling and similarity functions across prefix lengths.

2. **Do the latent operations remain reliable at benchmark scale?** Interpolation and arithmetic are primarily shown through selected examples, while CIRR exposes difficulty with count edits (Figures 8–9 and 23). A systematic intervention benchmark could score identity preservation, attribute control, relations, and counting.

3. **How well does FLAT transfer beyond English image–text pairs?** Multilingual and video behavior is qualitative despite English-only static-image training (Appendix G). Quantitative evaluation on multilingual retrieval/generation and established video-understanding tasks would test this claim.

4. **Can similar quality be obtained with less pretraining compute or data?** The reported run uses roughly 67M pairs and 64 H200 GPUs (Table 6), but no scaling study is supplied. Training matched models across data, model, and hardware budgets would reveal the efficiency frontier.
