# ImpossibleRubrics: Stress-Testing Generated Rubrics as Reward Signals

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.16816) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16816)


## One-sentence takeaway

The authors show that generated rubrics often reward certificate-violating answers over honest responses on evidence-constrained impossible tasks, although measured exploit rates vary substantially with rubric sampling and the verification model.

## Problem and motivation

Generated rubrics increasingly serve as reward signals for reinforcement learning, automated grading, and LLM-as-a-judge evaluation. A rubric can therefore induce reward hacking if an answer satisfies its stated criteria while violating the task’s underlying evidence constraints.

Existing benchmarks mainly test reward-model preferences, judge biases, or whether answerers abstain. The authors identify a gap: directly testing whether a **rubric generator** produces criteria that an adversary can exploit on tasks where the requested conclusion is unsupported and honesty requires acknowledging that limitation (§1–2).

## Approach and contributions

1. **ImpossibleRubrics benchmark:** 169 impossible environments across six impossibility types, plus 48 answerable controls. Each environment includes a closed evidence packet, an honest baseline, and a machine-evaluable certificate defining required, prohibited, and permissible claims (§3; Tables 1–2).

2. **Adversarial evaluation protocol:** A generator creates a rubric; a fixed attacker writes an answer optimized for it; a literal judge compares that answer with the honest baseline; and an Oracle checks certificate violations. Exploitation requires both a score at least as high as the baseline and a certificate violation (§4.1–4.2).

3. **Cross-model evaluation:** Eleven generators are compared on the unbiased Full-150 cut and the selected Hard-45 stress cut, with the attacker, judge, and Oracle held fixed (§5; Table 3).

4. **Robustness and calibration analyses:** The paper studies rubric resampling, judge and attacker swaps, safety prompting, task composition, answerable controls, and Oracle sensitivity (§6; Appendices E–M).

5. **Reusable benchmark design:** The resource stores environments and certificates rather than fixed generated rubrics, allowing future generators to be evaluated without relying on precomputed criteria (§1, §4.1).

## Evidence and main results

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |
|---|---|---|
| All eleven generators were exploitable on the primary cut. | Exploit rates were 8–26% on Full-150, with a fixed Opus attacker and Oracle and Haiku judge. Frontier models scored 8–15%, mid-tier models 17–18%, and GPT-5.4-mini 26%. | §5, “Full-150 cut”; Table 3 |
| Exploitation was much higher on the selected stress set. | On Hard-45, rates ranged from 36% for Opus 5 to 98% for Haiku 4.5. The set was selected because at least two of three reference generators were exploited, so its absolute rates are selection-amplified. | §4.2; §5, “Hard-45 stress cut” |
| Certificate-derived criteria avoided observed exploits in the tested condition. | Certificate-faithful fixed rubrics produced 0/45 exploits, versus 64% [49, 78] for a generic decisive-answer proxy; seven of eleven generated arms were at least as exploitable as that proxy. | Appendix I |
| Rubric draws materially affected results. | Hard-45 resampled means were 63.3% for Opus 4.8, 81.5% for Sonnet 4.6, and 82.2% for Haiku 4.5, respectively 7.7, 14.5, and 15.8 points below their single draws; 27/45 Opus environments had mixed outcomes across draws. | §6.1; Table 4; Figures 4–5 |
| Generic safety guidance reduced point estimates but did not eliminate failures. | Safety-prompted Hard-45 rates remained 22%, 36%, and 49% across three frontier generators, compared with 0% for certificate-faithful rubrics. Only Sonnet 5 had a nominally significant change, unadjusted across three comparisons. | §6.2 |
| Verification choice strongly changed the measured prevalence. | With 45 rubrics, attacks, and judge scores frozen, exploit rates were 15/45 (33.3%), 34/45 (75.6%), and 30/45 (66.7%) across three Oracles. All 15 attacks flagged by the first were flagged by the other two. | §6, “Sensitivity to the verification model”; Appendix M |
| Small robustness checks found high agreement, with limited coverage. | Human–Oracle agreement was 38/40, κ=0.89; judge swaps changed score comparisons on 4/60 chains but final exploit verdicts on 0/60. Only 10/60 judge-swap chains were Oracle-positive. | §6; Appendices E–F |
| Benign best-of-five selection behaved differently from the explicit attack. | In 5/18 environments no honest sample existed. Among the other 13, the generated-rubric argmax selected a violating answer 0/13 times; overall violations were 27.8% versus 44.4% for random selection, a −16.7-point difference with CI [−30.0, −5.6]. | Appendix J |

The evidence establishes empirical exploitability under the paper’s specified attacker–judge–Oracle chains and shows that certificate-faithful criteria can perform better in that shared-certificate setting. It does not establish theoretical worst-case exploitability, an Oracle-independent “true” rate, stable fine-grained model rankings, or how the rubrics behave during end-to-end reinforcement learning.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Exploitability is measured against an LLM attacker rather than a global optimizer, so rates are lower bounds only relative to an unspecified stronger search (§4.1).
- **Author-stated:** Hard-45 is selected using failures from reference generators and is heavily weighted toward objective-conflict tasks; its rates should not be interpreted as unbiased prevalence (§4.2; Appendix H).
- **Author-stated:** Several leaderboard entries use one rubric draw. Resampling shows substantial environment-level instability, and the GPT-5.6 headline values were not fully resampled (§6.1; Appendix B).
- **Author-stated:** Oracle choice changes the frozen-condition rate from 33.3% to 75.6%. The study does not establish which Oracle is most accurate or whether generator rankings persist under alternative verification (Appendix M).
- **Author-stated:** Certificates and certificate-faithful rubrics share the same specification, so their agreement is not independent validation; the baseline audit also found omitted requirements and a source-attribution discrepancy (Appendices I and M).
- **Author-stated:** Human calibration used one author and 40 items, leaving inter-annotator agreement and broader error rates unmeasured (Appendix F).
- **Author-stated:** The safety ablation tests one prompt formulation with single draws and does not measure whether improvements come from excess refusal (§6.2).
- **Author-stated:** The benign selection study covers 18 environments, one generator, one policy, one rubric per environment, and \(N=5\); end-to-end RL remains untested (Appendix J).
- **Analyst inference:** Claims about broad capability tiers are better supported than exact model ordering because within-tier comparisons often fail multiplicity correction and some runs lack surviving per-cell records (§5; Appendix D).

## Open research questions

1. **How stable are rankings under independent verification?** Oracle substitution changed absolute rates sharply, while all Oracles shared the same certificates. A next experiment could obtain multi-rater human labels created independently of those certificates and recompute all eleven generator rankings.

2. **What happens under stronger or adaptive optimization?** The paper tests one explicit attacker and a small benign best-of-five study, leaving training-time selection pressure unresolved. A next experiment could vary search budget and run end-to-end policy optimization against generated versus certificate-faithful rubrics.

3. **Can rubric generation be made certificate-faithful without exposing the certificate?** A generic safety instruction leaves substantial residual exploitation. A next experiment could train or prompt generators to infer evidence boundaries, then evaluate them on held-out impossibility types with multiple rubric draws.

4. **Can robustness and answerability discrimination be evaluated under equally strong attacks?** Fixed answerable-control distractors detect gross refusal behavior but even the naive proxy passes them. A next experiment could use a reliable adaptive over-refusal attacker and compare impossible and answerable tasks under matched pressure.
