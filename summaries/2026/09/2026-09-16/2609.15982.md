# The Router Within: Eliciting Native Skill Routing from a Frozen LLM

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.15982) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15982)


## One-sentence takeaway

Gavel extracts skill-routing signals from a frozen agent LLM using two trained linear projections, then combines hidden-state matching, task likelihood, and relevance judgment to outperform external retrieval pipelines across written-task, trajectory, and live-harness evaluations.

## Problem and motivation

LLM agents must select the right skill from potentially large libraries. Progressive disclosure places every skill’s metadata in the context, consuming context space and dispersing attention; external retrieve-and-rerank systems avoid that burden but delegate selection to separate models that may not share the agent’s task understanding or see its live rollout.

The authors target the resulting gap: routing with the agent LLM’s own capabilities while keeping skill text outside its context until selection and avoiding retraining when a skill is installed (Sections 1 and 3.1).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Native two-stage routing.** Gavel keeps the agent backbone frozen and trains only two linear maps totaling 7.9M parameters. A **glance** ranks the full library from intermediate hidden states; a **verdict** applies the same backbone’s full attention to shortlisted skill-task pairs (Sections 3.2–3.5).

2. **Token-level skill indexing.** Each skill is converted once at installation into a bank of projected token keys. Task tokens vote for skills using maximum token similarity, avoiding an additional query-model forward pass (Section 3.2).

3. **Bounded bank compression.** A farthest-first ε-cover reduces redundant skill keys. The authors prove that compression cannot increase a maximum similarity and lowers it by at most ε for a unit query; vote stability additionally depends on distance from top-k boundaries (Section 3.3 and Appendix D).

4. **Product-of-experts ruling.** The final score combines glance similarity, mean task log-likelihood conditioned on the skill, and the model’s yes/no relevance log-odds (Sections 3.4–3.5).

5. **New evaluation asset.** SkillTraj contains 372 simulated multi-turn trajectories across user-request, tool-evidence, agent-plan, and wrong-skill-recovery scenarios (Section 4.3 and Appendix H.4).

These results support the authors’ claim that a frozen LLM’s internal states contain useful skill-routing information and that the three routing signals are complementary. They also show strong results under the paper’s adjudicated Hit@1 and trigger-rate protocols.

They do not establish downstream task-completion gains after loading a selected skill, superiority on arbitrary model families or hosted APIs, or reliable routing without task-skill supervision. Comparisons with frontier models in Table 1 also combine different models and harnesses rather than providing a controlled backbone-only comparison.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope:** Routing tools, memories, and MCP servers remains untested (Section 5).
- **Author-stated gate limitation:** The live gate is a simple linear classifier trained once on SkillTraj and was not extensively engineered; sequence modeling and dedicated call-token training remain future work (Appendix K).
- **Analyst inference — synthetic evaluation:** SkillTraj trajectories are generated by GPT-5.6 Sol and filtered by a Claude judge. This offers controlled scenarios but may not reproduce naturally occurring agent behavior (Section 4.3 and Appendix H.4).
- **Analyst inference — adjudication dependence:** Main written-task results use GPT-5.6 Sol adjudication because gold labels are incomplete. Human agreement on 200 sampled pairs is **86%**, while class-level agreement is only **.559** for judge-labeled ties and **.646** when the judge prefers the committed skill (Appendix H.2, Table 5). Raw results preserve the reported ordering, which reduces but does not remove this concern.
- **Analyst inference — limited backbone diversity:** Body results center on Qwen3-32B; one separately rebuilt Gemma model supports family transfer, but broad generality is not established (Section 4.1 and Appendix E).
- **Analyst inference — infrastructure access:** Gavel requires hidden states, resumed forward passes, and token probabilities. The hosted-API design is proposed rather than experimentally validated (Appendix K).
- **Analyst inference — end-to-end metric:** Skill-Use measures whether the correct skill is loaded, not whether the agent ultimately completes the executable task successfully (Section 4.4 and Table 1).
- **Analyst inference — tuning dependence:** Projection training, ε, fusion weights, and pruning margin use SkillRet training or validation data; other libraries are zero-shot afterward, but fully training-free deployment is not demonstrated (Section 4.1).

## Open research questions

1. **Does better routing improve completed-task quality?** The live study reports trigger rate rather than execution success. A next experiment could compare task completion, regressions, latency, and unnecessary loads with and without Gavel on executable agent tasks.

2. **How well does Gavel handle tools, memories, and MCP servers?** The authors explicitly leave these resource types open. A next experiment could train the same projections once and test zero-shot routing across separately labeled tool, memory, and MCP catalogs.

3. **Can routing be exposed through hosted APIs efficiently?** Appendix K gives a provider-side design without empirical validation. A next experiment could implement the proposal and measure latency, cache reuse, privacy exposure, and cost across changing client libraries.

4. **Can the gate and token aggregation learn from realistic rollouts?** The retrieval-token experiment learned recency from clean single-turn supervision, while the deployed gate is a simple linear classifier. A next experiment could train sequence-aware aggregation and gating on naturally collected multi-turn traces, then test timing and false-call rates out of distribution.
