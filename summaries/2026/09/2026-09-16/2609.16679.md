# AI for Games in the Foundation Model Era

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.16679) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16679)


## One-sentence takeaway

This survey organizes AI for games into six output-based roles and argues that reusable capabilities increasingly connect them, but effectiveness must still be revalidated for each game’s rules, interfaces, state representation, and player population.

## Problem and motivation

Research on game-playing agents, player and world modeling, game design, implementation, runtime adaptation, and testing has largely developed in separate communities. This fragmentation makes it difficult to tell which capabilities and artifacts transfer across roles or games and which remain tied to a specific engine, interface, rule set, or population.

The authors address this gap with a lifecycle-wide framework centered on three questions: what structure is supplied versus assigned to AI, what transfers or can be reused, and what empirical claims are supported at the output’s actual point of use (Section 2, pp. 5–6).

## Approach and contributions

1. **Output-based taxonomy.** The survey assigns systems to six roles according to the immediate use of their output: play and act; model players and games; design; build and maintain; generate and adapt at runtime; and test and evaluate (Section 2; Table 1, p. 6).

2. **Cross-role evidence framework.** It distinguishes capability transfer from artifact reuse and asks whether outputs are validated where they are consumed. For example, a plausible mechanic, working implementation, and valid test verdict are treated as separate achievements (Introduction, pp. 4–5).

3. **Lifecycle connections.** It identifies exchanges such as gameplay trajectories training world models, learned environments training policies, design specifications driving implementations, and play or test feedback guiding revision (abstract, p. 1).

4. **Comparative synthesis and system index.** The paper maps representative systems, benchmarks, mechanisms, inputs, and evaluation boundaries across the six roles (Figures 3–4, pp. 7–9; Tables 15–20, pp. 106–115).

| Finding | Evidence / setting | Location in paper |

| Dreamer 4 illustrates both progress and remaining long-horizon difficulty. | From 2,500 hours of action-labeled Minecraft data, its policy obtained an iron pickaxe in 29% of 60-minute episodes and diamonds in 0.7%, under native mouse/keyboard control and prescribed prompts. | Section 4.1, p. 20 |

| Current construction and testing evaluations expose substantial dependence on task and protocol. | Examples include Play2Code at 66.8% rubric pass after feedback on 200 tasks, GameEngineBench’s best result of 55.5% on 110 tasks, and GameGen-Verifier at 92.2% mean Acc@5 with state injection that bypasses natural reachability. | Tables 18 and 20, pp. 112, 115 |

These examples support the authors’ claim that evidence is highly dependent on the output, interface, and evaluation protocol. They do not establish a single ranking of systems, universal cross-game transfer, or that better proxy metrics reliably improve player experience. The supplied text also does not expose the survey’s full evidence analysis because a large middle portion was removed.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated scope boundary:** The foundation-model era is an analytical lens rather than a strict inclusion rule; the survey also includes earlier symbolic and specialist systems (Section 2, p. 5).
- **Author-stated comparability risk:** Shared model weights, algorithm reuse, held-out levels, fine-tuning, and adaptation with demonstrations test different forms of generalization (Sections 3.1–3.3, pp. 10–15).
- **Author-stated evaluation risk:** Visual quality does not establish correct mechanics or persistent state, and simulated or model-based judgments do not automatically establish human relevance (Sections 4.1–4.3, pp. 20–28).
- **Author-stated external-validity risk:** Control schemes, rules, engines, representations, and player populations often remain setting-specific, requiring downstream validation (abstract, p. 1).
- **Analyst inference:** As a broad survey spanning heterogeneous studies, its conclusions depend on evaluation protocols that are often not directly comparable; the supplied text presents no formal meta-analysis.
- **Analyst inference:** Some indexed evidence comes from technical announcements, official product reports, small studies, or model judges rather than shared independent benchmarks (for example, pp. 20–21 and Tables 19–20).
- **Source limitation:** The collector omitted 280,341 characters from the middle of the paper. Sections covering much of design, development, runtime systems, testing, benchmark synthesis, discussion, and conclusions are therefore unavailable or represented only through the contents and appendix tables.

## Open research questions

1. **How can policies transfer across native control interfaces and unfamiliar rules?**  
   The survey shows that shared weights, semantic APIs, demonstrations, and native controls provide materially different support. A next experiment could hold out entire games while separately varying visual appearance, action mapping, rules, and adaptation budget.

2. **How can learned worlds preserve mechanics and persistent state over long interaction horizons?**  
   Current evidence often tests visual continuity, selected numerical variables, or bounded games separately. A next experiment could compare video-only, retrieved-memory, structured-state, and executable-state models on identical long-horizon inventory, geometry, rule, and revisit probes.

3. **When does a player model improve the experience of the population it represents?**  
   Prediction accuracy and successful personalization are distinct outcomes, and several studies use narrow or synthetic populations. A next experiment could evaluate calibration on held-out players and then randomize whether the resulting profile controls adaptation.

4. **Can automated testing provide representative behavioral coverage and independently reliable verdicts?**  
   Tester exploration, state injection, model judges, and bug oracles each affect reported performance. A next experiment could benchmark them against diverse human traces and independently verified defects while measuring reachability, coverage, false positives, and repair success separately.
