# PhysStream: Streaming Physics-Grounded Video Generation with Structured Scene Memory and Fine-Grained Motion Control

> [← Daily summary](../2026-09-16.md) · [Relationship graph](relationships.md) · [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.17521) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17521)


## One-sentence takeaway

PhysStream generates tabletop physics videos frame by frame, using sparse object velocity commands and online depth and tracking memory to improve motion accuracy while allowing users to intervene during generation.

## Problem and motivation

Existing controllable video generators commonly require the entire control schedule in advance or use pixel trajectories that prescribe where objects should move rather than expressing physical interventions. This prevents users from observing partial output and deciding the next action dynamically.

The authors target four properties together: sparse controls, physically meaningful controls, mid-generation interaction, and object-level control in multi-object scenes. They argue that prior streaming methods lack history-derived physical scene state, while simulator-mediated alternatives can diverge from the generated video (Introduction; Table 1).

## Approach and contributions

- **Sparse physical control:** Users provide 3D velocity increments for selected objects at chosen frames. These vectors are painted onto the objects’ first-frame masks rather than specifying complete trajectories (Section 3.2, Eqs. 4–5).

- **Structured scene memory:** Previously generated frames are processed online into normalized 3D positional maps using monocular depth and object-tracking maps using SAM2. These maps condition subsequent frames (Sections 3.1 and 3.3; Figure 2).

- **Two-stage training:** A Wan2.2-TI2V-5B backbone first learns velocity conditioning with bidirectional attention. It is then converted into a causal, teacher-forced autoregressive model that learns the memory branches (Sections 3.1–3.3; Appendix A).

- **Causal condition injection:** VAE-encoded controls and memory are shifted forward by one latent frame and concatenated with the noisy video latent, preventing current or future frame information from leaking into generation (Section 3.4, Eq. 11).

- **Synthetic training resource:** The authors curate approximately 100,000 rendered videos of indoor, multi-object rigid-body dynamics, including collisions and repeated velocity perturbations (Section 4.1; Appendix B.1).

These results establish stronger motion alignment on the authors’ synthetic benchmark and favorable judgments on a small in-the-wild study. They also provide targeted evidence that positional and tracking memory help particular cases.

They do not establish general physical simulation accuracy across arbitrary environments or materials. Some baselines receive modified or privileged inputs, and the reported consistency metrics can reward static or rigidly drifting outputs, as the authors demonstrate in Figure 4.

## Limitations and validity risks

- **Author-stated:** Extremely complex motion, especially tumbling, remains difficult (Section 5).

- **Author-stated:** The main validated scope is rigid-body dynamics; deformable balls and cloth require separate 10,000-clip datasets and additional finetuning (Section 4.3; Appendix B.2).

- **Author-stated:** Long autoregressive generations accumulate appearance drift, although control response reportedly remains high (Section 4.4).

- **Author-stated:** The system is not real time; interaction denotes the ability to intervene during generation (Introduction; Sections 4.6 and 5).

- **Author-stated:** Standard consistency metrics can favor nearly static motion or other degenerate outputs (Section 4.2; Figure 4).

- **Analyst inference:** Synthetic evaluation is narrow: each principal test set contains 64 videos drawn from static-camera tabletop scenes (Section 4.2).

- **Analyst inference:** Baseline comparisons are not fully symmetric. Tora and FlashMotion receive ground-truth center-of-mass trajectories, while RealWonder receives the ground-truth scene instead of reconstructing it (Table 2 caption).

- **Analyst inference:** Teacher-forcing creates a train–inference mismatch because training uses clean ground-truth history while inference uses generated history. Appendix F compares alternative training schemes but does not eliminate this exposure-bias risk.

- **Analyst inference:** Online errors from depth estimation and mask tracking can feed back into later generations. The tracking-drop study measures graceful degradation, but no broad robustness study across estimator failures is reported.

- **Analyst inference:** Reproducibility is incomplete in the supplied text because model, dataset, and code release status is not stated, and several tables omit their numerical bodies.

## Open research questions

1. **Can the system handle richer physics without material-specific finetuning?**  
   This remains open because cloth and elastic-ball results use separate 10,000-clip datasets. A next experiment could train a shared model across rigid, elastic, and cloth simulations and test held-out material properties.

2. **How robust is generation to erroneous scene memory?**  
   Depth and tracking are estimated from the model’s own potentially drifting outputs. A controlled experiment could inject calibrated depth noise, mask loss, identity swaps, and delayed memory updates, then measure motion and control degradation.

3. **Can exposure bias be reduced while retaining online memory?**  
   Teacher-forcing performed best under the reported budget, but it trains on clean histories. A next experiment could run truncated generated rollouts with cached or asynchronously estimated memory and compare long-horizon drift under matched compute.

4. **Does the method generalize beyond static tabletop cameras?**  
   First-frame normalization and camera-frame controls rely on a static-camera assumption. A next experiment could introduce known and unknown camera motion and compare camera-compensated memory against the current formulation.
